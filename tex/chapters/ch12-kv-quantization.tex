\chapter{Quantizing the KV Cache}\label{ch:kv-quantization}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
At long context and large batch the KV cache, rather than the weights,
dominates both memory and the bytes read per step, and quantizing it is the
most direct relief. Its errors behave differently from weight errors: they
enter every attention score of every later token, and keys carry outlier
channels that per-token scaling handles badly. This chapter derives
per-channel key and per-token value quantization, FP8 caches with calibrated
scales, four-bit and lower schemes, and pre-RoPE and rotated variants; it
measures all of them on the real keys and values of a real model's first
layer, and shows how to measure long-context damage rather than trusting
short-context perplexity.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{Twice the context in the same memory}

llama.cpp stores its KV cache in 16-bit floats unless told otherwise; one
flag stores it in the 8- or 4-bit block formats it uses for weights. For
Llama~3.2~3B the choice shrinks a token's keys and values from 112~KiB to
59.5~KiB at 8 bits or 31.5~KiB at 4 (derived from the formats: 34 and 18 bytes
per block of 32 values). A 16,384-token context whose cache needs 1.88~GB in
16 bits needs 1.00~GB at 8 and 0.53~GB at 4: in the same memory, 3.6 times as
many such conversations.

The speed does not follow the bytes. On the M1, with flash attention on and
16,384 tokens in the cache, llama.cpp decoded at \textbf{12.4} tokens per second
with the 16-bit cache, \textbf{9.5} with the 8-bit cache and \textbf{11.4} with
the 4-bit cache (measured; record \texttt{2026-09-23-m1-part2}). The quantized
caches were slower at every depth measured, from an empty cache to 16,384
tokens. Subtract each format's empty-cache step time and the attention over a
16,384-token cache read its bytes at about 46~GB/s in 16 bits, 16~GB/s at 8
bits and 14~GB/s at 4 (derived). In this engine, on this GPU, unpacking the
cache inside the kernel costs more time than the bytes it saves.

So the case for a quantized cache on this machine is capacity alone. The case
for speed needs two more things: a workload in which the cache is most of the
bytes, and a kernel that reads the smaller format at close to full bandwidth.
The napkin math below prices the first; only a measurement settles the second.
And both assume the smaller cache still gives the model the answers the full
one would --- which, for a cache, is a sharper question than for weights, and
the second half of this chapter measures it.

\Foundations{Errors can change both the address and the payload}{
A key participates in the score that decides how strongly a position is attended to. A
value supplies the content mixed into the output. Thus key error can change the mixing
weights, whereas value error changes what is mixed; treating them as interchangeable
arrays hides a useful distinction. Both effects can propagate through later layers and
generated positions. The cache is produced from the actual request, unlike a fixed
weight tensor. Chapter~\ref{ch:kv-cache} establishes its lifetime and
Appendix~\ref{app:numerics} separates representation error from task-level quality
loss.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: when the cache is most of the bytes}

For $B$ sequences with mean context $\bar{S}$, the cache's share of a decode
step's bytes is
\begin{equation}
\phi_{kv} = \frac{B\,\bar{S}\,m_{kv}}{W + B\,\bar{S}\,m_{kv}},
\label{eq:kv-share}
\end{equation}
and \Chref{napkin-math} found $\phi_{kv}\approx0.8$ for Qwen3-8B on an H100 with
a full memory of 4,096-token sequences. When the cache is most of the bytes,
shrinking it pays twice: every step reads less, and twice as many sequences
fit. Quantizing only the cache of that configuration from BF16 to FP8 --- the
weights untouched --- lets the H100 hold 197 sequences instead of 98 and, with
the step model of equation~\eqref{eq:step-time}, roughly doubles throughput
and halves the cost per token (derived, with the same illustrative
efficiencies, and assuming the kernel reads FP8 as efficiently as BF16 --- the
assumption the opening measurement warns about). No weight format change in
\Chref{weight-quantization} buys that much at this operating point.

The per-token arithmetic is worth doing once. Llama~3.2~3B keeps, per token and
layer, 8 key heads and 8 value heads of 128 values: 2,048 values, 57,344 across
its 28 layers. In 16 bits that is 112~KiB; in Q8\_0, 34 bytes per 32 values,
59.5~KiB; in Q4\_0, 18 bytes per 32, 31.5~KiB; in a 4-bit format with a 16-bit
scale and minimum per 32 values, 35~KiB (derived). A laptop with 4~GiB to spare
holds 37,000 tokens of 16-bit cache and 133,000 of Q4\_0 --- the difference
between one long document and the model's whole 131,072-token context.

\section{Why cache errors are different}

A quantized weight's error enters one layer's output for the current token. A
quantized key's error enters the attention score of \emph{every later query}
that looks at that token, for the rest of the request. And it enters through an
exponential: a score error $\Delta$ multiplies the token's attention weight by
$e^{\Delta}$ before normalization. An error that is harmless at 500 tokens can
accumulate into a wrong answer at 50,000, because the model's ability to
retrieve one fact from far back depends on one key standing out from many.
Values are gentler: their errors are averaged by the attention weights.

Put numbers to the exponential. A key whose score is off by 0.5 gets
$e^{0.5} = 1.65$ times its correct weight before normalization; by 0.1,
$1.105$ times. Scores are dot products of 128 channels scaled by
$1/\sqrt{128}$, so a key error spread evenly over the channels partly averages
out, but an error concentrated in the one channel where the query is also
large does not. That is why the \emph{shape} of a key's error --- which channels
it lands on --- matters more than its size, and why the rest of the chapter is
about where the quantizer puts its error.

\emph{A near tie, by hand.} Two keys score 5.0 and 4.9 against a query, and
nothing else is close. Their attention weights are in the ratio $e^{0.1}:1$,
0.525 and 0.475 of the pair. Quantization lowers the first score by 0.1 and
raises the second by 0.05: now 4.9 against 4.95, weights 0.4875 and 0.5125. The
most-attended token has changed, yet the two distributions differ by only 0.0028
nats of KL divergence (derived). Both measures in the lab's table are right; they
answer different questions. KL divergence asks how far the weights moved; the
top-token count asks whether the winner changed, and a winner decided by a near
tie changes for errors far smaller than the ones that move a distribution.

\section{Keys have outlier channels}

Key vectors are not evenly distributed across their dimensions: a few channels
carry consistently large magnitudes, the same channels token after token. KIVI
measured this and drew the design conclusion: quantize keys \emph{per channel},
so that each channel gets its own scale across a group of tokens, and values
\emph{per token}, because their outliers are not channel-aligned
\cite{liu2024kivi}. With that split, KIVI stored both at 2 bits with little
quality loss, cut peak memory 2.6 times including the weights, and fit up to four
times larger batches, for 2.35 to 3.47 times the throughput on real workloads.

The lab sees the same thing in layer 0 of Llama~3.2~3B. Averaged over the 3,217
tokens, the loudest key channel's magnitude is 16.0 times the median channel's
before RoPE and 15.4 times after it; in the first head the loudest are channels
79, 80 and 76, the same in both. The values' loudest channel is 7.7 times their
median. Per-token quantization gives each token one scale per 32 channels, and
the one loud channel in a group sets it: its neighbours, 16 times smaller, are
left a handful of levels.

\emph{A group by hand.} Take one token's group of 32 key channels in which one
channel holds 16 and the other 31 lie between $-1$ and 1, and quantize it to 4
bits with a minimum and a scale, as the lab's per-token rows do. If the group
spans $-1$ to 16, the fifteen steps between those ends are $17/15 = 1.13$ wide,
so every small channel lands on $-1$ or $0.13$, and a value of 0.5 comes back as
0.13: the small channels keep about one bit of information between them. Now
quantize per channel instead, one channel across 32 tokens: the loud channel's
values, all near 16, share their own range, and each quiet channel's values
between $-1$ and 1 share a range with steps of $2/15 = 0.13$, eight times finer
(derived). The bits per value are the same; only the direction along which the
scale is shared has changed, and with it which values the loud channel can
starve.

\section{Keys are the fragile half}

The argument that values are gentler can be tested by quantizing one half of the
cache at a time. With 4-bit per-token values and exact keys, the lab's attention
outputs are 8.3\% off, but not one most-attended token changes and the attention
distributions are untouched --- values do not enter the softmax at all. With 4-bit
per-token keys and exact values, the output is 22.5\% off, 23.1\% of the
(query, head) pairs attend to a different top token, and the distributions move
by 0.031 nats: all of the damage of the full 4-bit per-token row, and most of
its output error. At 2 bits the split is starker: 72.6\% output error from the
keys alone, 40.9\% from the values alone (measured). A value error stays where it
is, averaged over the tokens a query attends to; a key error decides which tokens
those are. Everything a cache format does for keys matters twice.

\section{Per-channel keys, per-token values}

The table makes the case. At 4 bits, keys and values both quantized per token
change the most-attended token for 23.1\% of the (query, head) pairs; quantize
the keys per channel instead, in groups of 32 tokens, and it falls to 8.1\%,
with twelve times less KL divergence ($2.5\times10^{-3}$ against
$3.1\times10^{-2}$) for the same 5 bits per value. At 2 bits the gap is wider
still: per-token keys and values move attention by half a nat per pair and
change the top token for 57\% of the pairs, which is noise; KIVI's split holds
it to 34\%.

The asymmetry has a systems cost. A per-token scale can be computed the moment a
token's key is produced. A per-channel scale spans many tokens, so it cannot be
fixed until a group of tokens exists; recent tokens wait in higher precision
until their group is complete and can be quantized together. The engine's
cache becomes two-tiered --- a quantized body and a small full-precision tail
--- and every attention kernel must read both. The lab keeps the last,
incomplete group in 16 bits for that reason; the second worked problem prices
the tail.

\section{FP8 caches and their scales}

The simplest production option stores the cache in FP8. vLLM offers E4M3 and
E5M2 caches, with one scale per tensor or per attention head, computed from a
calibration dataset or left at a default of 1.0 \cite{vllm2026kvcache}.
FP8 halves the cache with small, usually negligible error --- provided the
scale is right. E4M3's largest value is 448 \cite{micikevicius2022fp8}; a cache
whose values exceed the range at a scale of 1.0 saturates, and one whose values
are tiny wastes most of the format's precision. ``Usually negligible'' is a
claim to measure per model. The choice between the two FP8 formats follows from
Chapter~\ref{ch:low-precision-float}'s lesson: E5M2's five exponent bits span a ratio
of about $2^{32}$ between its largest and smallest values without any scale, which forgives a missing
calibration, and its two mantissa bits halve the precision of E4M3's three,
which a calibrated scale would have made unnecessary.

On the lab's layer the largest key is 19.6 and the largest value 0.94, so a
scale of 1.0 saturates nothing, and calibrating the scale to the tensor's
maximum barely helps: 4.4\% output error against 4.6\%, both changing the top
token for about 5\% of the pairs. The surprise is the row above them. llama.cpp's
\texttt{q8\_0} cache --- 8-bit integers with a 16-bit scale per token per 32
channels --- is three times more accurate than FP8 (1.6\%) for half a bit more,
the same verdict \Chref{low-precision-float} reached for weights: for storage,
fine integer scales beat a floating-point grid. FP8's case is elsewhere: an
attention kernel can feed FP8 keys and values straight to FP8 tensor cores, as
FlashAttention-3 does \cite{shah2024flashattention3}, which an integer cache
cannot do without converting.

\section{Four bits and below}

Below eight bits the cache needs the channel-aware treatment above, or
rotations. KVQuant combined per-channel keys, pre-RoPE quantization,
non-uniform per-layer code books and separately stored outliers, and reported
under 0.1 perplexity increase at 3 bits --- enough to serve LLaMA-7B with a
million-token context on one 80~GB GPU \cite{hooper2024kvquant}. Rotation
methods spread outliers across channels before quantizing: QuaRot quantizes the
cache to 4 bits alongside weights and activations \cite{ashkboos2024quarot}.
TurboQuant rotates each vector randomly and applies an optimal scalar quantizer
per coordinate, and reports quality neutrality at 3.5 bits per channel
\cite{zandieh2025turboquant}; vLLM added it as an attention backend in April
2026, with FP8 keys and 4-bit values as its most conservative preset
\cite{vllm2026turboquantpr}.

\section{Before or after RoPE; rotations}

Rotary position embedding rotates pairs of key channels by angles that depend
on position (Appendix~\ref{app:rope}), which mixes the outlier channels with
their partners and makes their statistics depend on position. Quantizing keys
\emph{before} RoPE preserves the channel structure that per-channel scales
exploit, at the price of applying RoPE when the key is read rather than when it
is written \cite{hooper2024kvquant}. The lab confirms it: per-channel keys
quantized before RoPE and rotated afterwards move attention half as much as the
same keys quantized after RoPE --- $1.2\times10^{-3}$ against $2.5\times10^{-3}$
nats at 4 bits, $3.1\times10^{-2}$ against $7.1\times10^{-2}$ at 2 --- and change
the top token for 5.8\% of the pairs instead of 8.1\%.

Rotation methods use the opposite idea: apply a known orthogonal rotation that
spreads outliers evenly, so that plain per-token quantization works. llama.cpp
does this by default for quantized caches. When the cache type is quantized and
the head width a multiple of 64, it multiplies keys by a Walsh--Hadamard matrix
the size of the head and values by 64-point blocks --- smaller, its source notes,
because that ``seems beneficial'' for values --- and undoes the rotation on the
way out (\texttt{attn\_rot\_k} and \texttt{attn\_rot\_v} in
\texttt{src/llama-kv-cache.cpp} at \texttt{d006858}; an environment variable
turns it off). \Chref{kv-cache}'s allocation log showed both flags set for its
\texttt{q8\_0} and \texttt{q4\_0} caches. In the lab the same rotation cut
\texttt{q4\_0}'s KL divergence by 40\% and its output error from 18.9\% to
15.8\%, but left the top token changed for 21.8\% of the pairs: a rotation spreads
the loud channel's energy over all 128, and then every channel carries part of
it, and 4 bits per token remain coarse. Per-channel keys did better at the same
bit budget.

\section{Does the damage grow with context?}

The warning at the start of this chapter --- an error harmless at 500 tokens can
matter at 50,000 --- is a claim about scaling, and the lab can test its first
factor of ten. It rebuilds each cache from only the first $m$ tokens of the text,
for $m$ from 256 to 3,217, and lets the last 64 of those tokens attend (measured,
record \texttt{2026-09-26-m1-pass3}). The KL divergence per (query, head) barely
moves with $m$: $1.6$--$2.0\times10^{-4}$ nats for Q8\_0 throughout,
$1.4$--$1.7\times10^{-2}$ for rotated Q4\_0, $1.1$--$1.3\times10^{-3}$ for 4-bit
per-channel keys before RoPE. The count of changed top tokens tells a different
story for the coarse formats: 18.2\% at 256 tokens and 27.1\% at 3,217 for rotated
Q4\_0, and 31\% to 35\% for 2-bit KIVI, while Q8\_0 stays near 2\%.

The two measures disagree for a reason. A longer context puts more keys near the
top of each query's scores, so the same key errors break more near-ties: the
winner changes more often even though the distribution, taken as a whole, moves
no further. For a model, which of the two matters depends on what it is doing.
A summary depends on the distribution; a retrieval of one fact from far back
depends on a single winner standing out, and that is the measure that grows with
context. Three thousand tokens of layer 0 cannot show the fifty-thousand-token
case, nor what deeper layers, whose keys are shaped by earlier attention, do with
the errors; that takes the long-context evaluations of the next section.

\section{Kernels that read a quantized cache}

A quantized cache is only useful if the attention kernel reads it without
first expanding it back to 16 bits in memory, which would spend the bandwidth it
saved. The kernel dequantizes blocks as it loads them, inside the tiled loop of
\Chref{flashattention} and \Chref{decode-attention}. llama.cpp makes the
coupling explicit: it refuses a quantized value cache unless flash attention is
enabled (``V cache quantization requires flash\_attn'', \texttt{llama\_init\_from\_model}
in \texttt{src/llama-context.cpp} at \texttt{d006858}), tying the cache's format to
the fused kernel, and with flash attention left on \texttt{auto} it also checks
that the format's block size divides the head width. The dequantization is
extra arithmetic in what should be a bandwidth-bound loop, and it is not always
free. In the opening measurement, llama.cpp's Metal kernels read an 8-bit cache
at about a third of the rate at which they read a 16-bit one, so the smaller
cache was the slower one; the third worked problem finds the context length at
which the 4-bit cache would overtake it. Whether a format pays in time is a
property of the kernel and the hardware, not of the format.

Per-channel schemes cost kernels more than per-token ones. A per-token block
arrives with its own scale; a per-channel group needs a scale per channel for a
block of tokens, applied along the other axis of the tile, and a separate path
for the full-precision tail. None of the kernels this book measures implements
KIVI's layout; llama.cpp's quantized caches are per-token blocks with a
rotation, and the lab's table shows what that choice costs at 4 bits.

\section{Measuring long-context damage}

Perplexity on short passages measures almost nothing about a cache: the
errors that matter accumulate over long contexts and show up as failures to
retrieve or use distant information. Measure with long inputs: retrieval of
planted facts across the whole context, long-document question answering, and
comparisons of the quantized and unquantized models' output distributions at
many context lengths. The TurboQuant integration in vLLM reported its FP8-key,
4-bit-value preset keeping 100\% on a needle-in-a-haystack test while
accuracy on a mathematics benchmark fell from 0.900 to about 0.860 on
Qwen3-4B \cite{vllm2026turboquantpr} --- one metric unmoved, another clearly
moved, by the same change.

The same kind of measurement, token by token over the whole model, is cheap to
make with llama.cpp's own tools. Against Llama~3.2~3B with its 16-bit cache, over
2,040 scored tokens, the \texttt{q8\_0} cache chose the same next token 99.5\%
of the time with a mean KL divergence of 0.00009; the \texttt{q4\_0} cache, with
llama.cpp's rotation on, 93.5\% of the time, with a mean divergence of 0.021 and,
at the worst token, a 41-point swing in a probability (record
\texttt{2026-09-26-m1-part8}; \Chref{fast-wrong-answers} builds the harness).
The lab's layer-0 attention and the whole model's next-token distribution agree
on the order: 8 bits per token is almost free, 4 bits per token is not.

\section{Quantized blocks in a paged, shared cache}

A cache format is also a data-structure decision, and it meets the serving
machinery of the next part of this book in three places. \emph{Paging}
(\Chref{paged-kv}) stores the cache in fixed blocks of tokens --- 16 in vLLM by
default (\Chref{decode-attention}) --- and a per-token format fits a block
without ceremony: each token is quantized when it is written, into the block
that holds it. A per-channel format does not. Its groups of 32 tokens span two
16-token blocks, its scales belong to the group rather than to either block, and
its tail waits in a separate full-precision area until the group completes; the
block table must now describe two kinds of storage. \emph{Prefix sharing}
(\Chref{prefix-caching}) reuses the cached blocks of a common prompt across
requests. With a quantized cache, what is shared is the quantized bytes: every
request that reuses a prefix inherits the same rounding, so the error becomes
part of the shared state, and a cache written in one format cannot be reused by
a request that wants another. \emph{Tiering} (\Chref{kv-storage-tier}) moves
cold blocks to host memory or disk and back. There a smaller format pays at its
full ratio, because the transfer, not an attention kernel, is the bottleneck:
half the bytes is half the time over a PCIe link, whatever the kernels do (all
derived from the formats' layouts).

\section{Choosing a cache format}

The chapter's measurements reduce to a few rules. \emph{Eight bits per token
is almost free.} On the lab's layer, llama.cpp's \texttt{q8\_0} cache changed the
most-attended token for 2\% of the pairs at any context length, and over the whole
model it left 99.5\% of next tokens unchanged; FP8 with a sensible scale was
within a few percent too. The remaining question is speed, and that belongs to
the kernel: on the M1 the 8-bit cache was the slowest of the three, while an
FP8 cache feeding FP8 tensor cores is the point of FP8. \emph{Four bits per token
is not free.} Rotated or not, per-token 4-bit keys changed the top token for a
fifth to a quarter of the pairs, and 6.5\% of the model's next tokens; they buy
capacity for workloads that need it and should be measured before they are
trusted. \emph{Below that, keys need per-channel treatment}, preferably before
RoPE, or a rotation designed for the purpose; the published 2- and 3-bit schemes
all do one or the other, and the lab shows why. \emph{Values can go lower than
keys}, because their errors never change which tokens a query attends to, only what it reads from them. And
\emph{measure where the application lives}: at the context lengths and on the
tasks it serves, with a token-by-token comparison of distributions, not a short
perplexity.

work. A model whose keys had no loud channels would make per-token and

saturate an FP8 cache at scale 1; run the lab on another model before trusting
this chapter's ranking for it.

\emph{Predict} before you run: which moves attention more, 4-bit
per-token keys with a rotation or 4-bit per-channel keys without one?
\emph{Explain} why the FP8 rows barely depend on the scale for this layer.
\emph{Break} it: quantize the keys per channel in groups of 256 tokens instead of
32 and watch the tail grow and the scales coarsen; run the queries over only the
first 512 tokens and see whether short contexts hide the damage.

\HappenedSection{half the bytes, slower}

When Hermon moved its KV-cache writes into C, storing the cache in 16-bit
floats was \textbf{slower} than storing it in 32-bit: 6.39~ms against 1.16~ms
for the same bulk write, despite moving half the bytes. The conversion from
32- to 16-bit floats was written in software with five branches per element
--- for NaN, overflow and subnormals --- and it cost more than the bytes it
saved while preventing the loop from vectorizing. Replacing it with the ARM
instruction that converts four values at once made the 16-bit write 8.8 times
faster and, as it should always have been, faster than the 32-bit one (Hermon
\texttt{docs/KERNEL\_DESIGN.md}, section 1; the kernels are selected by an
environment variable, so PREVIEW). A smaller format is a promise about bytes,
not about time; the conversion is part of the cost. llama.cpp's Metal cache
kernels in the opening measurement are the same lesson at a larger scale.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-27}

KV-cache formats moved from research into engines' command lines between 2024
and 2026: FP8 caches are routine \cite{vllm2026attentionbackends}, and
rotation-plus-scalar schemes such as TurboQuant ship behind a flag
\cite{vllm2026turboquantpr}. Four-bit floating point has reached the cache as
well: NVIDIA's TensorRT-LLM offers an NVFP4 KV cache, enabled with
\texttt{KvCacheConfig(dtype='nvfp4')} after offline quantization with its Model
Optimizer, and for now only together with FP8 weights and activations
\cite{nvidia2026trtllmquant}. Architectures attack the same bytes from the other
side --- latent attention stores a compressed cache by design
(\Chref{shrinking-kv}), and DeepSeek-V4 compresses along the sequence, reporting
a tenth of DeepSeek-V3.2's cache at a million tokens \cite{deepseek2026v4}.
