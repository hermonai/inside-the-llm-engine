\chapter{Modern Speculation: The EAGLE Family, Medusa and Multi-Token Prediction}\label{ch:modern-speculation}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
The best drafters now come from the target model itself. This chapter
follows the EAGLE family --- drafting from the target's hidden features,
dynamic draft trees, and EAGLE-3's direct token prediction with fused
multi-layer features --- alongside Medusa's extra heads, multi-token-prediction
modules trained with the model, and parallel drafting that produces a whole
draft in one pass. It explains tree attention for verifying many candidate
continuations at once and compares the methods by what they cost to train, to
run and to integrate.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{Drafting from the model's own features}

The obvious drafter is a smaller model. On the M1 we paired an
8-billion-parameter target, DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B, with a 3-billion-parameter
drafter, Qwen2.5-Coder-3B, both quantized to four bits, in llama.cpp's server.
The drafter disagreed with its target more than half the time: of the tokens
it drafted, the target accepted 98 of 208 on one task and 106 of 231 on another,
46 to 47\% (measured; record \texttt{2026-09-24-m1-part4}). It was not cheap
either. One step of the drafter took 0.38 of a step of the target, close to the
ratio of their bytes; and because the two models' vocabularies differ, the
server translated every draft between them through text. The result, priced in
\Chref{when-speculation-loses}, was decoding at half its normal speed.

A drafter must predict what the target is about to say, cheaply. A separate
model trained on different data and tokenized differently predicts what
\emph{it} would say, at a third of the target's cost. The drafters of this
chapter reverse both terms. They are built from the target itself --- its
hidden features, its output head, heads trained alongside it --- so they agree
with it far more often, and they cost a small fraction of a target step.

\Foundations{A tree contains incompatible histories}{
Two draft branches can share a prefix and then diverge. Their later states belong to
different histories even if stored in one tensor. Verification must use a mask and
position map that allow each node to see only its ancestors, not its siblings. Extra
prediction heads or hidden features change how candidates are proposed; they do not
eliminate the need to specify the acceptance contract. Read
Chapter~\ref{ch:speculative-decoding} before comparing draft architectures. Count
proposed, verified, accepted and published tokens separately: these quantities answer
different performance and correctness questions.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: draft cost against acceptance length}

Let a drafter propose $\gamma$ tokens per verification pass at a cost of $c$
target steps each, and let the target accept $\tau$ tokens per pass on average,
counting the one it always produces. Ignoring the extra cost of verifying
several tokens at once, the speedup is
\begin{equation}
S \approx \frac{\tau}{1 + \gamma\,c}.
\label{eq:draft-speedup}
\end{equation}
Every modern drafter attacks the denominator. A separate model a third the
size of its target has $c \approx 0.37$ in bytes read, the opening's case, so
four drafted tokens cost more than a whole target step. A drafter built from one
extra transformer layer that reuses the target's embedding table and output
head reads that layer plus the output head per drafted token. For Llama~3.2~3B
the output head alone is 394 million parameters --- 12\% of the model --- so
such a drafter still costs about 15\% of a step; for Llama~3's
70-billion-parameter model, with the same vocabulary, 80 layers and width 8,192
\cite{llama2024herd}, the head is about 1.5\% of the parameters and one layer
about 1.2\% (derived). Drafting from the target's
own features pays best on large models, which is where its published speedups
were measured.

\section{Drafting from hidden features}

EAGLE drafts not tokens but features: a small network, a single transformer
layer, predicts the target's next second-to-top-layer feature vector from the
current one and the token just sampled, and the target's own output head turns
each predicted feature into a token distribution \cite{li2024eagle}. Features
carry more of what the target is about to do than tokens do, and feeding the
drafter the token sequence advanced by one step resolves the ambiguity of which
continuation was sampled. Verification uses the rule of
\Chref{speculative-decoding}, so the output distribution is unchanged. Its
authors reported latency speedups of 2.7 to 3.5 times on LLaMA2-Chat 70B and
doubled throughput (their measurement).

\section{EAGLE and EAGLE-2: dynamic draft trees}

A single chain of drafted tokens fails at its first wrong guess. A tree hedges:
at each depth the drafter keeps several candidates, and one verification pass
checks every root-to-leaf path. The tree's shape matters, and the best shape
depends on the context: after \emph{New} a tree should branch; inside a quoted
URL it should run deep. EAGLE-2 grows the tree where its drafter is confident,
using the drafter's probabilities as estimates of acceptance, which its authors
found well calibrated; they reported speedups of 3.05 to 4.26 times, 20 to 40\%
faster than EAGLE \cite{li2024eagle2} (their measurement).

\section{EAGLE-3: tokens, not features}

EAGLE-3's authors found that predicting features limited how much the drafter
improved with more training data. Their third version predicts tokens directly,
fuses features from several layers of the target rather than only the top one,
and during training feeds the drafter its own earlier predictions, as it will
see them when drafting several steps ahead --- a procedure they call
training-time test \cite{li2025eagle3}. They reported speedups up to 6.5 times,
about 1.4 times EAGLE-2, and 1.38 times the throughput at batch 64 inside
SGLang (their measurement). EAGLE-3 is supported by vLLM and SGLang
\cite{vllm2026specdecode,sglang2026specdecode}.

\section{Tree attention and verification}

Verifying a tree in one forward pass needs an attention mask shaped like the
tree: each drafted token attends to the prompt, to the tokens already accepted
and to its own ancestors in the tree, and to nothing on other branches. The
tree's nodes are laid out as one flat sequence of positions, the mask encodes
the branching, and the target computes logits for every node at once. SpecInfer
introduced this token-tree verification, reporting 1.5 to 2.8 times faster
distributed serving and 2.6 to 3.5 times faster offloaded serving
\cite{miao2024specinfer} (their measurement). After verification the engine
keeps the cache entries of the longest accepted path and discards the rest, the
tree version of \Chref{speculative-decoding}'s rollback. SGLang exposes the
tree's shape as three settings --- drafting depth, branching per step and
the number of tokens verified --- with defaults that differ by model family
\cite{sglang2026specdecode}.

The array order is an implementation choice; ancestry is part of the attention contract. For row D, allow P, A and D even if B appears earlier in the flattened array. An ordinary triangular mask would incorrectly connect branches. Verification must also restore logical positions and retain only the chosen path.

\section{Medusa heads}

Medusa skips the separate drafter: it adds several output heads to the target,
head $i$ predicting the token $i$ positions ahead from the last hidden state,
and builds a tree from the top candidates of each head \cite{cai2024medusa}.
With the backbone frozen, its authors reported more than 2.2 times speedup;
training heads and backbone together, 2.3 to 3.6 times (their measurement).
Medusa also offered a \emph{typical acceptance} rule that accepts plausible
tokens rather than exactly verified ones. It raises acceptance and gives up the
guarantee that the output distribution is the target's: an engineering choice
the ledger must record, not a free improvement.

\section{Multi-token prediction trained with the model}

The drafter can be part of the model from the start. Gloeckle and colleagues
trained models to predict several future tokens with independent heads on a
shared trunk; the auxiliary task improved 13-billion-parameter models on
coding benchmarks, and models trained to predict four tokens ran up to three
times faster with self-speculative decoding, including at large batch sizes
\cite{gloeckle2024mtp} (their measurement). DeepSeek-V3 trains one extra
multi-token-prediction module. Its report states that the module can be
discarded at inference, and that used as a drafter its second-token prediction
was accepted 85 to 90\% of the time, raising decoding speed to 1.8 times the
tokens per second \cite{deepseek2024v3}. When a model ships with such a module,
the drafter's training cost has already been paid.

\section{Parallel drafting}

An autoregressive drafter still runs $\gamma$ small steps in sequence before
each verification, and at high speed those steps are the bottleneck. P-EAGLE
drafts all $\gamma$ tokens in one forward pass of the drafter, which lets it
draft deeper at the same cost; its developers reported acceptance 13 to 31\%
higher than EAGLE-3 at equal depth and speedups over EAGLE-3 of 1.55 to 1.69
times at a concurrency of one \cite{vllm2026peagle}. Speculative speculative
decoding attacks the remaining sequential dependency: while the target verifies
one draft, the drafter prepares drafts for the verification's likely outcomes,
and its authors reported 30\% more speed than optimized speculative decoding
\cite{kumar2026ssd} (their measurement).

\section{Choosing a method}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.2\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.22\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.2\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
Drafter & Training needed & Draft cost & Distribution \\
\midrule
Prompt or suffix lookup & none & a table lookup & exact \\
Smaller model & none, if one exists & a large fraction of a step & exact \\
EAGLE family & a one-layer drafter per target & one layer plus the output head & exact \\
Medusa & heads per target & heads only & exact or typical \\
Multi-token prediction & with the model & one module & exact \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Lookup drafters cost nothing and win only where the output repeats its context
(\Chref{speculative-decoding}). Model-based drafters win broadly but must be
trained for each target and kept in step with it through every fine-tune.
Multi-token-prediction modules shift that cost into pre-training, where the
model's builder pays it once.

\LedgerSection

Drafting from the target's own features, heads or trained modules:

\begin{ConstraintLedger}
Latency & buys & Several accepted tokens per target pass: published speedups of 2.2 to 6.5 times at low concurrency. \\
Compute & spends & Draft passes and verification of rejected tree branches; the saving shrinks as the batch fills (\Chref{when-speculation-loses}). \\
Memory capacity & spends & Drafter weights or heads, and cache entries for every tree node verified. \\
Correctness & risks & Exact with rejection sampling; not exact with typical acceptance. \\
\end{ConstraintLedger}

\HappenedSection{parallel drafting under load}

P-EAGLE's release shows how a drafter's benefit depends on load. On NVIDIA
B200 GPUs, with GPT-OSS and Qwen3-Coder models, parallel drafting beat EAGLE-3
by 1.55 to 1.69 times at a concurrency of one --- and by only 1.05 to 1.23 times
at a concurrency of 64 \cite{vllm2026peagle} (the developers' measurement).
Nothing about the drafter changed between the two numbers. At concurrency one
the GPU idles between tokens and every accepted token is time saved; at 64 the
batch already keeps the GPU busy, and the extra drafted and verified tokens
compete with other requests' work. The feature ships in vLLM from version 0.16
as an option on the speculative configuration, and the numbers say when to turn
it on --- the subject of \Chref{when-speculation-loses}.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-24}

Model-based drafting is now built into the major engines --- EAGLE-3, MTP
modules and parallel drafting in vLLM, EAGLE-2 and EAGLE-3 trees and MTP in
SGLang \cite{vllm2026specdecode,sglang2026specdecode} --- and into models
themselves: DeepSeek-V3 trains a multi-token-prediction module
\cite{deepseek2024v3}, and Qwen3.5's published configuration declares one
\cite{qwen2026qwen35config}. Research is removing the remaining sequential
steps \cite{vllm2026peagle,kumar2026ssd}. llama.cpp, in the build measured
for this part, declares an EAGLE-3 draft type whose drafting function is not
yet implemented (\texttt{common/speculative.cpp} at commit \texttt{d006858}).

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Derivation.} Using equation~\eqref{eq:draft-speedup}, find the
acceptance length $\tau$ at which a drafter costing $c = 0.15$ per token with
$\gamma = 4$ breaks even, and compare it with $c = 0.02$.
\item \emph{Prediction.} A draft tree has depth 3 and branching 2 at every
node. How many tokens does its verification pass process, and how many can it
accept at most?
\item \emph{Implementation.} Add dynamic branching to the lab's tree builder:
expand the most probable nodes until a budget of verified tokens is spent.
\item \emph{Falsification.} Construct a distribution for which typical
acceptance produces an output sequence the target model would never sample.
\end{enumerate}
