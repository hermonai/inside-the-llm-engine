\chapter{Prefill and Decode: Two Workloads, One Model}\label{ch:prefill-decode}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
Processing a prompt and generating a reply use the same weights and almost
nothing else in common. Prefill multiplies the weights by a matrix of prompt
tokens and is limited by compute; decode multiplies them by one vector per
sequence and is limited by memory bandwidth. This chapter measures both phases
on one machine --- prefill across prompt lengths from one token to four
thousand, decode across contexts from empty to eight thousand tokens ---
derives time to first token and time per output token from the roofline, and
proves on a small engine that the two phases compute exactly the same numbers
at very different costs. It then shows why each phase wants a different batch
size, a different kernel, a different scheduler and --- at scale --- different
hardware. The tension between them drives chunked prefill
(\Chref{chunked-prefill}) and disaggregated serving
(\Chref{disaggregated-serving}).
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{The same model reads twelve times faster than it writes}

On the M1 laptop of \Chref{roofline}, llama.cpp processed a 512-token prompt
through Llama~3.2~3B at \textbf{248 tokens per second}, then generated at
\textbf{21 tokens per second} (measured; same record). Both numbers come from
the same weights, the same GPU and the same arithmetic: every token, read or
written, costs about 6.4~GFLOP. Reading costs 4.0~ms per token. Writing costs
47.6~ms per token --- twelve times as much. Three days later, on a quieter
desktop, the same machine gave 288 and 25 tokens per second (measured, record
\texttt{2026-09-26-m1-pass3}): both numbers rose by a sixth to a fifth, and the
ratio stayed near twelve.

Nothing in the model explains the difference. A prompt token and a generated
token pass through identical layers. What differs is how many of them arrive
at once. The 512 prompt tokens are all known before the first layer runs, so
one pass over the weights serves all of them; a generated token does not exist
until the previous one has been sampled, so each needs its own pass. Prefill
is a matrix--matrix product and decode is a matrix--vector product, and
\Chref{roofline} showed which roof each one hits.

Every user feels both phases. The prompt's cost arrives as the wait before
the first word; the reply's cost arrives as the pace of the words after it.
They are different quantities with different physics, and an engine that
treats them alike serves both badly.

\Foundations{Known positions versus unknown choices}{
A prompt's tokens are already known. A causal mask prevents each position from seeing
later positions, but the engine can still evaluate those known positions in parallel
within each layer. During ordinary generation, the next input token depends on the
previous sampling result. That dependency, not the causal mask by itself, serializes
decode steps. A prompt of length $S$ does not require $S$ separate model invocations
in a normal prefill. Conversely, batching different requests does not let one request
skip its own dependency. Appendix A.3 develops the feedback loop;
Chapter~\ref{ch:forward-pass} identifies the operators inside one invocation.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: time to first token and time per output token}

Let a model have $P$ parameters in its layers, stored at $b_w$ bytes each,
and let a machine provide bandwidth $\beta$ and effective compute
$\eta\pi$, where $\eta$ is the fraction of peak a real kernel achieves. A
prompt of $S$ tokens costs about $2PS$ FLOPs in the layers, plus the output
projection once, for the last token only --- an engine does not need logits
for positions whose next token it already knows. It also reads the weights at
least once. Prefill time is therefore about
\begin{equation}
t_{\text{prefill}}(S) \approx \max\!\left(\frac{P\,b_w}{\beta},\;
\frac{2PS}{\eta\pi}\right).
\label{eq:prefill-time}
\end{equation}
The two terms are equal at $S \approx b_w\eta\pi/(2\beta)$ --- the same
$B^{*}$ as batched decode in \Chref{roofline}, now counted in prompt tokens.
On the M1 it is a dozen or so tokens; on an H100 with BF16 weights, one to
three hundred, depending on $\eta$. Beyond it, prefill time grows linearly with
the prompt.

Decode, one sequence at a time, is the other term's opposite:
\begin{equation}
t_{\text{decode}} \approx \frac{W}{\beta}
\label{eq:decode-time}
\end{equation}
per token, where $W$ is the bytes read per step --- the weights, plus the KV
cache once contexts grow (\Chref{kv-cache}).

For the M1: a 512-token prompt costs 2.07~s (measured, $512/247.75$) and each
generated token 48~ms (measured). The prefill rate implies an efficiency:
$247.75 \times 6.43$~GFLOP per token is 1.59~TFLOP/s, $\eta = 0.61$ of the
2.6~TFLOP/s Apple quotes; on the quieter day, 288 tokens per second is
$\eta = 0.71$. With $\eta = 0.6$ the crossover of
equation~\eqref{eq:prefill-time} is at
$0.63 \times 0.6 \times 2.6\times10^{12} / (2 \times 49\times10^{9}) = 10$
prompt tokens. For Qwen3-8B in BF16 on an H100, with $\eta = 0.6$
(illustrative) and 6.95~billion layer parameters (derived from its
configuration): a 2,000-token prompt costs about
$2\times 6.95\times10^{9}\times 2{,}000 / (0.6\times 989\times10^{12})
\approx 47$~ms, and each generated token at least
$15.1~\text{GB}/3.35~\text{TB/s}\approx 4.5$~ms. The phases scale with
different hardware resources, so moving between machines changes them by
different factors. An H100 has about 380 times the M1's peak compute (989
dense BF16 TFLOP/s against 2.6 TFLOP/s, whose precision Apple does not state)
but only about 49 times its bandwidth (3.35~TB/s against 68~GB/s): moving from
the laptop to the data center speeds prefill far more than it speeds decode.

\section{The prefill curve, measured}

Equation~\eqref{eq:prefill-time} predicts a simple shape: the prompt rate
climbs in proportion to $S$ while the weight bytes dominate, then flattens at
$\eta\pi/2P$ tokens per second once the arithmetic does. On the M1 that should
be a climb from about 25 tokens per second at one token to about 290 by a dozen
tokens. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04-prefill-curve} is what llama.cpp's own benchmark
measured for prompts of 1 to 2,048 tokens.

\EngineFigure{ch04-prefill-curve}{Prefill throughput of Llama~3.2~3B on the M1's

Three features of the measured curve are not in the equation. First, the climb
is a staircase, and it is the same staircase as batched decode in
\Chref{roofline}: one prompt token runs at 25.6 tokens per second, two at
23.7 --- the matrix--vector kernel reads the weights once per row --- four and
eight at 56 and 64 with the small-batch kernel, sixteen at 134 and thirty-two
at 261 once the tiled kernel takes over. A step of 16 tokens takes 119~ms and a
step of 32 takes 123~ms: the tiled kernel pays for 32 columns either way. So
the knee is set by the kernel's tile width, 32, rather than by the ten tokens
the roofline predicts. The tile shows at every multiple of 32, not just the
first. Measured around the edges, 30 and 32 tokens take 122~ms, 34 take 221~ms;
62 and 64 take 228--233~ms, 66 take 325~ms; 126 and 128 take 432--437~ms, 130
take 538~ms (record \texttt{2026-09-26-m1-pass3}): each started tile costs
about 108--122~ms, full or not, so two tokens past a multiple of 32 cost as
much as thirty more. Second, the plateau is real and broad: 284--297 tokens
per second from 64 to 512 tokens, $\eta = 0.70$--$0.73$. Third, past 512 tokens
the rate falls --- 281 at 1,024, 260 at 2,048, 225 at 4,096 (measured) ---
because causal attention costs more per token the longer the prompt. The
equation's second term assumed a fixed cost per token; attention makes it
grow.

The practical reading is that a short prompt is expensive per token and a
long prompt is not. A four-token prompt costs 71~ms, almost the same as two
decode steps; a 512-token prompt costs 1.78~s, 3.5~ms per token. Any engine
that can gather prompt tokens --- from several requests, or from one request's
tail --- into steps of at least a tile's width runs its prefill an order of
magnitude more cheaply per token than one that cannot.

\section{Prefill: a matrix of tokens}

During prefill the activations for all $S$ prompt tokens form a matrix
$\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times d}$, and each linear layer computes
$\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}$ for a weight matrix
$\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d'}$. Each weight is loaded once and used $S$
times; the intensity is about $2S/b_w$ FLOPs per byte. For the 512-token prompt
at 0.63 bytes per weight that is $2 \times 512 / 0.63 \approx 1{,}600$~FLOP/byte,
forty times the M1's ridge; for any prompt longer than a few hundred tokens it
is past the ridge of every machine in \Chref{roofline}, and prefill runs under
the compute roof.

Prefill also writes the KV cache: for every prompt token and every layer, a
key and a value vector, the state that decode will read (\Chref{kv-cache}).
For Llama~3.2~3B that is 112~KiB per token, so the 512-token prompt leaves
56~MiB in the cache. And it computes causal attention among the prompt tokens:
the $S \times S$ score matrix of \Chref{forward-pass}, with the upper triangle
masked, per head and per layer. At 4,096 tokens that is about 2.9~TFLOP beside
the matrices' 23~TFLOP (derived), and the attention kernels run less efficiently
than the matrix kernels, which is why the curve falls. llama.cpp's
flash-attention path, which computes the same scores tile by tile without
writing the score matrix to memory (\Chref{flashattention}), processed the
4,096-token prompt in 15.7~s instead of 18.2~s (measured).

\section{Decode: one token at a time}

During decode each sequence contributes one new token per step. Its
activations are a vector, each linear layer is a matrix--vector product, and
the intensity is $2/b_w$ --- one to four FLOPs per byte. The step reads every
weight and, for attention, the sequence's whole KV cache, to produce one
token. Batching $B$ sequences multiplies the useful work per weight byte by
$B$, but not per cache byte: each sequence reads its own cache.

Decode is also sequential in a way prefill is not. The next step's input is
the token the current step samples, so the steps of one sequence cannot
overlap. Everything the engine does between steps --- sampling, detokenizing,
checking stop conditions, scheduling the next batch --- sits on the critical
path of every token (\Chref{request-lifecycle}). \Chref{why-inference} traced
one such request: its six prompt tokens took 146~ms, the cost of about three
decode steps, and each of its eight generated tokens 50~ms (measured). At six
tokens the prompt is in the small-batch regime of
Figure~\ref{fig:ch04-prefill-curve}: a prompt that short gains only a factor of
two from being processed at once.

step or token by token must leave the same keys and values in the cache and
produce the same distribution for the next token --- if it did not, an engine
could not split prompts into chunks (\Chref{chunked-prefill}) or move a
prefilled cache to another machine (\Chref{disaggregated-serving}). The
chapter's lab tests this on the capstone engine of \Chref{capstone}, whose
kernels compute every output element in the same order whatever else is in the
batch. Its two ways of reading a prompt are six lines apart:

\section{Attention's share grows with context}

The napkin math above counted only the linear layers. Attention adds two costs
that grow with context, one per phase.

In prefill, causal attention over $S$ tokens costs about $2S^{2}H_qd_h$ FLOPs
per layer, against $2P_{\ell}S$ for a layer's linear parameters $P_{\ell}$.
They are equal at $S\approx P/(H_qd_hL)$. For Qwen3-8B that is
$6.95\times10^{9}/(4{,}096\times36)\approx 47{,}000$ tokens (derived): below
it, prefill is mostly matrix multiplication; above it, mostly attention.

In decode, attention's cost is bytes: each step reads $S$ tokens of KV cache
per sequence. For Qwen3-8B the cache is 144~KiB per token (derived from its
configuration: $2\times36$ layers $\times\,8$ KV heads $\times\,128\times2$
bytes), so a single sequence's cache equals the 15.1~GB of weights at about
100,000 tokens. With 32 sequences in a batch, whose weight reads are shared
but whose cache reads are not, the cache overtakes the weights at about 3,200
tokens per sequence. In a busy server, long-context decode is a
cache-reading workload. \Chref{kv-cache} and Part~\ref{part:serving} are about
exactly that.

For Llama~3.2~3B on the M1, the cache adds 112~KiB per token of context to
every step, so at 8,192 tokens a step reads 0.94~GB of cache beside 2.01~GB of
weights: at the 49~GB/s the GPU's streaming kernel reached, 19~ms more per
token. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04-decode-depth} shows what llama.cpp measured, with
its flash-attention kernel off (\texttt{llama-bench}'s default in the build
used) and on.

\EngineFigure{ch04-decode-depth}{Decode time per token of Llama~3.2~3B on the

The flash-attention curve is the one the napkin math predicts: from 36.9~ms at
an empty cache to 54.9~ms at 8,192 tokens, 18~ms more for 0.94~GB, about
52~GB/s of cache reads. The default path grows from 39.6 to 93.9~ms, reading the
same cache at an effective 17--20~GB/s beyond 1,024 tokens. The difference is
the kernel, not the bytes. For a single query per head, the flash-attention
decode kernel in llama.cpp's Metal backend splits each head's cache among 32
workgroups that run in parallel and then combines their partial results
(\texttt{ggml/src/ggml-metal/ggml-metal-ops.cpp}, checked at commit
\texttt{d006858}), so the whole GPU streams the cache; the default path runs
attention as ordinary matrix products, which with one query per head keep the
memory system far less busy. Splitting a long cache across the machine is the ``flash
decoding'' idea of \Chref{decode-attention}; on this laptop, at 8,192 tokens, it
is worth 70\% more tokens per second.

\section{One model, two code paths}

Because the phases stress different resources, a mature engine does not run
them through the same code. It runs the same model --- the same weights, the
same mathematics --- through different kernels, different data layouts and
different launch mechanisms, chosen by the shape of the step. Every engine
this book reads makes the split somewhere.

\emph{Matrix kernels by row count.} llama.cpp's Metal backend chooses among its
matrix--vector, small-batch and tiled kernels by the number of rows in the step
(\Chref{roofline}), so a decode step and a prefill step of the same model run
different kernels, and a mixed step runs whichever its total row count selects.

\emph{Attention kernels by query count.} The same backend's flash attention has
two kernels: a vector kernel, used when a step has fewer than 20 queries per
head, which splits each head's cache among 32 workgroups, and a tiled kernel for
more queries, which reuses each loaded key across many queries
(\texttt{ggml\_metal\_op\_flash\_attn\_ext\_use\_vec} in
\texttt{ggml-metal-ops.cpp}, checked at commit \texttt{d006858}). One query
against a long cache is a streaming problem; many queries against a short one
are a matrix product. The measured difference at 8,192 tokens of context was
70\% in decode speed.

\emph{Attention algebra by phase.} For models with multi-head latent attention
(\Chref{shrinking-kv}), vLLM computes prefill and decode with different
algebra: prefill reconstructs full keys and values and runs ordinary attention,
which its source calls the ``compute friendly'' path; decode folds the
reconstruction into the query and attends directly over the compressed cache,
the ``data-movement friendly'' path (\texttt{mla\_attention.py} at commit
\texttt{bcdacfc}). The two paths produce the same attention output and move very
different numbers of bytes.

\emph{Expert kernels by phase.} DeepSeek's expert kernels lay a batch out
contiguously, one segment per expert, for prefill, and use a fixed ``masked''
layout for decode so that decode steps can be replayed from a captured graph
\cite{deepseek2025deepgemm}; its communication library likewise offers a
high-throughput mode for prefill and a low-latency mode for decode
\cite{deepseek2025deepep}, and vLLM exposes both as separate backends
(\Chref{expert-parallelism}).

\emph{Launch mechanism by phase.} A decode step of a small model is so short
that launching its kernels one by one --- about a hundred for a
1-billion-parameter model, at about 2~$\mu$s each on an H100
\cite{spector2025megakernel} --- is a visible share of it, so engines capture
decode steps as CUDA graphs and replay them with one launch;
vLLM's default captures full graphs where it can and piecewise graphs around
attention elsewhere \cite{vllm2026cudagraphs} (\Chref{kernel-languages}).
Prefill steps, long and variable in shape, gain little from capture.

\emph{Hardware by phase.} At the extreme, the phases run on different
processors. KTransformers, which serves very large mixtures of experts on one
GPU and a server's CPUs, uses the CPUs' matrix extensions for prefill and their
vector units for decode \cite{chen2025ktransformers}; disaggregated serving puts
the phases on different GPUs altogether (\Chref{disaggregated-serving}).

The pattern is the chapter's in miniature: the arithmetic of a token is fixed
by the model, but the cost of computing it depends on how many tokens share a
step, and an engine that knows which phase it is in can choose the kernel,
layout and launch that fit.

\section{Latency, defined: TTFT, TPOT, ITL and end to end}

Four measurements describe what a user experiences, and the book uses them
precisely. Following DistServe \cite{zhong2024distserve}:

\begin{itemize}
\item \Term{Time to first token} (TTFT): from the request's arrival to the
first generated token. It includes queueing as well as prefill, whose last
position yields the first token.
\item \Term{Time per output token} (TPOT): the average time between generated
tokens after the first, for one request.
\item \Term{Inter-token latency} (ITL): the individual gaps between
consecutive tokens. Its distribution matters more than its mean; a stall
shows up here and nowhere else.
\item \Term{End-to-end latency}: arrival to last token, about
$\text{TTFT}+(n-1)\,\text{TPOT}$ for $n$ output tokens.
\end{itemize}

Worked on the M1 with the measured rates: a request with a 512-token prompt
and a 200-token reply, alone on the machine, has a TTFT of about 1.8~s (the
prompt at 288 tokens per second), a TPOT of about 43~ms (decode at 22--24 tokens
per second over a context that grows from 512 to 712 tokens), and an
end-to-end latency of about $1.8 + 199 \times 0.043 = 10.4$~s. Four fifths of
the wait is decode, although the prompt is two and a half times as long as the
reply. Double the prompt and the TTFT roughly doubles; double the reply and the
end-to-end latency nearly doubles. Which phase a request is ``made of'' decides
which optimization it can feel (the ``What actually happened'' section below
measures the consequence).

\Chref{request-lifecycle} defines where each timestamp is taken; a token is
not the same event as a byte a client can display. Throughput metrics come
later, in \Chref{napkin-math} and \Chref{benchmarking}: an engine can raise
aggregate tokens per second while every user's TPOT gets worse.

\section{Where the phases collide}

An engine that serves many users runs both phases at once, and they interfere.
If a 512-token prompt joins a batch of decoding sequences, the step that
processes it takes as long as the prompt's prefill. Figure~\ref{fig:ch04-collision}
draws it on the M1 with steps timed from the measured rates: two sequences
decoding at 86.5~ms per step, then a step that carries the new prompt and their
next tokens --- 514 rows, seventeen of the kernel's tiles, about 1.89~s during
which neither of them produces a token, twenty-two ordinary steps. On an H100, a 2,000-token prompt for Qwen3-8B would open a gap
of about 47~ms, ten times an ordinary step.

\EngineFigure{ch04-collision}{Two sequences decode on the M1 when a 512-token
prompt arrives (step times derived from the measured llama.cpp rates). Processed
in one step, the prompt stops both sequences for 1.89~s. Split into chunks of 62
tokens, each processed together with the two sequences' next tokens so that
every step fills exactly two of the kernel's 32-column tiles, it stops them for
at most 0.23~s at a time, and delays its own first token from 1.89 to 1.98~s
after its prefill begins.}{fig:ch04-collision}

The bottom half of the figure is the fix every modern engine uses: split the
prompt into chunks and give each step a mixture of one chunk and the decoding
sequences' next tokens. The chunk size is a direct trade, and on this machine
it is a trade in tiles. With chunks of 62 tokens every step has 64 rows and
costs 0.23~s, so the decoders' worst gap shrinks eightfold and the new
request's first token comes 5\% later. With chunks of 64 tokens --- the round
number an engineer would pick --- every step has 66 rows, three tiles, and costs
0.32~s: 40\% longer gaps and a first token 37\% later, for two tokens per
chunk. Chunks of 126 give 0.43~s steps and a first token slightly sooner than
no chunking at all (1.85~s); chunks of 30 give 0.12~s steps and a first token
at 2.14~s (all derived from the measured step times; the worked problems give
the arithmetic). Each engine picks a token budget per step that sits above the
kernel's knee and below its users' tolerance for a stall, and the best budgets
are counted in the kernel's tiles, not in round numbers (\Chref{chunked-prefill}).

The reverse interference is subtler. If the scheduler admits prompts only
between decode steps, new requests queue behind the running batch, and TTFT
grows with the load rather than with the prompt. Neither phase can be
optimized without deciding how the other is treated. \Chref{chunked-prefill}
splits prompts into pieces that fit beside the decodes;
\Chref{scheduling} decides who waits.

\section{Two phases, two ideal machines}

Taken separately, the phases want different hardware. Prefill wants compute:
tensor-core throughput, and low-precision formats that double it
(\Chref{low-precision-float}). Decode wants memory bandwidth and capacity: HBM
to stream weights and caches, and room for many sequences' caches at once.
They also want different parallelism: prefill tolerates splitting a model
across many GPUs because its large matrices amortize communication; decode's
small steps do not (\Chref{model-parallelism}).

The ratio of the two roofs says how far apart the phases are on a given
machine. On the M1's GPU, prefill runs at about 12 times decode's rate per
token; on its CPU, where the ridge is lower, about 5 times; on an H100, with
the numbers of the napkin section, a 2,000-token prompt costs 24~$\mu$s per
token against 4.5~ms per decode token, a ratio near 190. The faster a machine's
arithmetic relative to its memory, the more its decode steps waste, and the
more there is to gain by giving each phase its own machine.

Running each phase on hardware and parallelism chosen for it, and moving the
KV cache between them, is disaggregated serving
(\Chref{disaggregated-serving}). DistServe reported serving up to 7.4 times
more requests within latency targets by separating the phases onto different
GPUs \cite{zhong2024distserve} (reported by the authors; not reproduced here).

\LedgerSection

This chapter's technique is the simplest one an engine can apply to the two
phases: mixing a prefill into a running decode batch rather than making it
wait.

\begin{ConstraintLedger}
Compute & buys & The prompt's large matrices raise the step's arithmetic intensity; the GPU is busier. \\
Latency (TTFT) & buys & New requests start without waiting for the batch to drain. \\
Latency (ITL) & spends & Every decoding sequence in the step waits for the prefill: about 1.89~s, twenty-two ordinary steps, for a 512-token prompt on the M1 (derived from measured rates); chunking bounds it at the chunk's cost. \\
Memory capacity & spends & The new request's KV cache is allocated immediately: 56~MiB for a 512-token prompt of Llama~3.2~3B. \\
\end{ConstraintLedger}

\HappenedSection{a decode speedup hidden by prefill}

Hermon records a measurement that shows why the phase split matters for every
optimization. Its prompt-lookup speculation (\Chref{speculative-decoding}) was
tested on a retrieval workload in which the model quotes a passage from its
prompt: eight concurrent requests, Qwen2.5-1.5B, two CUDA GPUs. Speculation
worked as designed. It cut the decode steps from about 22 to 12, with 60\% of
drafted tokens accepted. The wall-clock time improved by only
\textbf{1.09$\times$}, against the 1.5--2.5$\times$ its developers had
predicted, because the model stopped after about eleven tokens per request:
the long passages' prefill dominated each request, and speculation does
nothing for prefill (Hermon \texttt{docs/PERFORMANCE.md}, measured 2026-05-12;
the feature is off unless \texttt{HERMON\_SPEC\_NGRAM} and
\texttt{HERMON\_SPEC\_LOOKAHEAD} are set, both defaulting to zero in
\texttt{crates/hermon-runtime/src/batched.rs} at commit \texttt{2a3fd52}, so
PREVIEW).

The same optimization had measured 1.33$\times$ on a workload with longer
replies. An optimization's benefit is bounded by the share of time spent in
the phase it accelerates --- Amdahl's law, applied per request. With the M1
numbers of the latency section, halving TPOT would cut the 10.4~s request to
6.1~s, but a request with a 4,096-token prompt and a 10-token reply --- 18.2~s of
prefill and nine decode steps of about 64~ms --- would go from 18.8~s to
18.5~s.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-27}

The balance between the phases is moving in both directions at once.
Reasoning models generate thousands of hidden tokens before answering, making
serving more decode-bound (\Chref{reasoning-workloads}); agents resend long,
growing contexts on every turn, making it more prefill-bound, unless prefixes
are cached (\Chref{prefix-caching}). Mixing the phases carefully has become the
default: vLLM's V1 engine enables chunked prefill by default and schedules
decodes first \cite{vllm2026optimization} (\Chref{chunked-prefill}).
Separating them has moved from research \cite{zhong2024distserve} into
production stacks, unevenly: SGLang serves DeepSeek with prefill and decode on
separate nodes \cite{sglang2025largeep}, NVIDIA's Dynamo \cite{nvidia2026dynamo}
and llm-d \cite{llmd2026} orchestrate disaggregated engines, Moonshot's
Mooncake serves Kimi from separate prefill and decode clusters around a pooled
cache \cite{qin2024mooncake} --- while vLLM still labels its own disaggregated
prefill experimental and warns in its documentation that it ``DOES NOT improve
throughput'' (\texttt{docs/features/disagg\_prefill.md} at commit
\texttt{bcdacfc}; all checked 2026-09-24). Disaggregation buys control of TTFT
and ITL separately, not free throughput; \Chref{disaggregated-serving} measures
the difference.
