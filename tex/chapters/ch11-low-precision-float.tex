\chapter{Below 16 Bits in Floating Point: FP8, MXFP4 and NVFP4}\label{ch:low-precision-float}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
Floating-point formats divide a limited bit budget between range and precision. Modern
block-scaled formats add metadata shared by groups of values. This chapter explains
those encodings before comparing the hardware and kernels that can use them.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\section*{Low-bit floating point still needs a scale}
Recall the widening gaps between representable values in Part I. Now ask how an exponent
range and a block scale place a group's values on that grid. Read one format completely
before comparing several acronyms. Storage type, multiplication type and accumulator
type can differ; a device's advertised peak is meaningful only with those choices and
the actual operation stated.

\OpeningSection{117 billion parameters in 65 gigabytes}

OpenAI's gpt-oss-120b has 117~billion parameters \cite{openai2025gptoss}. In
the 16-bit format models are usually trained in, that would be 234~GB of
weights --- three 80~GB GPUs before a single token of cache. The checkpoint
OpenAI published is fourteen files totalling \textbf{65,248,893,184 bytes}
(measured: the sizes in the model repository's file listing, read 2026-09-24):
\textbf{4.46 bits per parameter}, and it runs on one 80~GB GPU.

Almost all of that saving comes from one decision: the mixture-of-experts
weights, about 115~billion of the 117~billion parameters, are stored in
MXFP4, a four-bit floating-point format in which every block of 32 numbers
shares one 8-bit scale. The attention weights, embeddings and router ---
about two billion parameters --- stay in 16 bits. The arithmetic checks
against the file: 114.7~billion expert parameters at 4.25 bits plus 2.1~billion
at 16 bits is 65.1~GB (derived from the published configuration; within 0.2\%
of the measured total).

Four-bit weights are not new; \Chref{weight-quantization} covers integer
formats that llama.cpp has used since 2023. What is new is that the four-bit
numbers are floating point, that the newest tensor cores multiply them
without converting them first, and that models are now \emph{trained} to be
served this way. This chapter explains the formats and what each one costs.

\Foundations{Range and resolution are different budgets}{
A floating-point format spends bits on sign, exponent and significand. Exponent bits
mostly control range; significand bits control the spacing between nearby normal
values. A format with fewer bits need not preserve either property of a larger format.
Block scaling adds another level: a shared scale moves a group's representable values
into the useful range. The scale is itself encoded, stored and loaded. Read
Chapter~\ref{app:numerics} before comparing low-bit formats, and distinguish element
encoding from accumulation precision. This chapter's newer formats change these
budgets; they do not remove them.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: bits per value, including the scales}

A block-scaled format stores $k$ elements of $b_e$ bits and one scale of $b_s$
bits, so each value costs
\begin{equation}
b_{\text{eff}} = b_e + \frac{b_s}{k}\quad[\text{bits}].
\label{eq:bits-per-value}
\end{equation}
The formats this chapter covers, from their specifications
\cite{rouhani2023mx,nvidia2025nvfp4,micikevicius2022fp8}:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
Format & Element & Block $k$ & Scale & Bits per value \\
\midrule
BF16 & E8M7 & --- & --- & 16 \\
FP8, per-tensor scale & E4M3 or E5M2 & tensor & FP32 & $\approx$8 \\
MXFP8 & E4M3 or E5M2 & 32 & E8M0 & 8.25 \\
MXFP6 & E2M3 or E3M2 & 32 & E8M0 & 6.25 \\
MXFP4 & E2M1 & 32 & E8M0 & 4.25 \\
NVFP4 & E2M1 & 16 & E4M3 (+ FP32 per tensor) & 4.5 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Bytes per token follow directly: a dense 70-billion-parameter model reads about
140~GB per decode step in BF16, 70~GB in FP8, 37~GB in MXFP4 and 39~GB in NVFP4.
Compute follows the hardware. The B200's dense tensor-core peak doubles with
each halving of precision: 2.25~PFLOP/s in BF16, 4.5 in FP8, 9 in FP4 (NVIDIA
HGX specification, logged in \texttt{research/FRONTIER.md}). A format that
halves the bytes \emph{and} doubles the arithmetic keeps a workload's position
relative to the ridge (\Chref{roofline}) while making everything twice as
fast --- if the accuracy survives. The rest of the chapter measures when it
does.

\section{Floating point with fewer bits}

A floating-point number spends its bits on a sign, an exponent that sets the
scale, and a mantissa that sets the precision within that scale. Take bits
away and one of the two suffers. The four-bit format used by both MXFP4 and
NVFP4, E2M1, has one sign bit, two exponent bits and one mantissa bit; its
non-negative values are 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4 and 6
\cite{nvidia2025nvfp4}. They are not evenly spaced: the gap doubles as the
magnitude does, which matches the roughly bell-shaped distribution of weights
and activations better than an integer grid of the same size.

\EngineFigure{ch11-fp4-grid}{The eight non-negative values of FP4 (E2M1)
against a uniform 3-bit grid with the same maximum. Floating point spends its
resolution near zero, where most values lie; the integer grid spends it
evenly. A shared per-block scale decides which real interval these eight
points cover.}{fig:ch11-fp4-grid}

\emph{A number by hand.} Encode 0.3 in E4M3, whose exponent field has a bias of
7. Write it as $1.2 \times 2^{-2}$: the exponent field is $-2 + 7 = 5$, binary
0101, and the mantissa must approximate 0.2 in three bits, in steps of $1/8$:
$0.2 \times 8 = 1.6$ rounds to 2, binary 010. The code is 0\,0101\,010, 0x2A,
and its value is $(1 + 2/8) \times 2^{-2} = 0.3125$ --- an error of 4.2\%, from
neighbours $0.28125$ and $0.3125$ that are 11\% apart. Three mantissa bits give
every binade eight steps, so the relative error of any normal E4M3 value is at
most one part in 16. (The lab's test checks the code.)

Eight magnitudes cannot represent a tensor on their own. The idea that makes
four-bit formats usable is the shared scale: a whole block of values is
divided by one scale factor chosen so that the block's largest magnitude lands
near the top of the grid. The element bits then only have to represent the
values' shape within the block; the scale carries the magnitude.

\section{FP8: E4M3 and E5M2}

The two FP8 formats standardized in 2022 split eight bits differently
\cite{micikevicius2022fp8}. E4M3 has four exponent bits and three mantissa
bits; it gives up infinities and keeps a single NaN pattern to extend its
range to $\pm448$. E5M2 has five exponent bits and two mantissa bits, keeps
IEEE-style special values, and reaches $\pm57{,}344$ with less precision. The
lab's decoders, written from those rules and tested against every one of the
256 codes of each, count 127 non-negative finite E4M3 values from $2^{-9}$ to
448 --- eighteen binades --- and 124 for E5M2, from $2^{-16}$ to 57,344. The
usual division of labour is E4M3 for weights and activations, where precision
matters more, and E5M2 for gradients in training, where range does --- but
DeepSeek-V3 used E4M3 everywhere, relying on fine-grained scales to supply the
range \cite{deepseek2024v3}.

\emph{One scale for a whole tensor, by hand.} The lab's real projection has a
largest weight of 0.0849 and a root-mean-square of 0.0114 (read from the file;
\Chref{weight-quantization}). One FP32 scale maps the largest weight to 448, so
$s = 0.0849/448 = 1.9 \times 10^{-4}$, and a typical weight becomes
$0.0114/s \approx 60$ --- in E4M3's binade from 32 to 64, where the step is 4. A
step of 4 around 60 is a relative step of about 7\%, so a typical weight is
rounded by at most 3.5\%, and on average by less; the lab measured 2.65\% RMS
error. The smallest weights, a hundred times smaller, land near 0.6, among E4M3's
subnormals, where the step is $2^{-9}$ in absolute terms and the relative error
grows --- which is what finer scales are for (derived).

\section{Scaling granularity: tensor, channel, block}

One scale per tensor is cheap and fragile: a single outlier anywhere in the
tensor sets the scale, and every other value is squeezed into a few levels of
the grid. One scale per row or column isolates outliers to their own channel.
One scale per small block isolates them to a few dozen values. Finer scaling
costs scale storage (equation~\eqref{eq:bits-per-value}) and, in a kernel,
the work of applying the scales.

DeepSeek-V3's FP8 recipe shows the choices a production system makes:
activations scaled per token per 128 channels ($1\times128$ tiles), weights per
$128\times128$ block \cite{deepseek2024v3}. Activations get the finer
granularity because they are computed at run time, carry outlier channels, and
are quantized on the fly; weights are quantized once, offline, and change
slowly across a block.

\section{Microscaling: shared exponents}

The OCP microscaling (MX) formats standardize block scaling for hardware: a
block of 32 elements shares one 8-bit scale in E8M0 --- an exponent with no
mantissa, so every scale is a power of two \cite{rouhani2023mx}. Multiplying by
a power of two is exact and cheap in hardware: it adjusts exponents without
rounding. An E8M0 byte stands for $2^{c-127}$, so the scales span more exponents
than FP32 itself, and the MX paper notes that its scale's representable
exponents are a superset of FP32's \cite{rouhani2023mx}: an MX block can never
run out of range, only of precision. The family covers MXFP8, MXFP6, MXFP4 and
MXINT8, each with the same 32-element blocks. The conversion the MX paper gives as a working example is
three lines: the shared exponent is
\begin{equation}
e_{\text{shared}} = \lfloor \log_2 \max_i \lvert v_i \rvert \rfloor - e_{\max},
\qquad X = 2^{e_{\text{shared}}},
\label{eq:mx-exponent}
\end{equation}
where $e_{\max}$ is the exponent of the element format's largest normal value
(2 for E2M1, whose largest value is $6 = 1.5 \times 2^2$; 8 for E4M3), and each
element is $v_i / X$ rounded to the element format, with values beyond its
largest normal clamped to it \cite[Algorithm~1]{rouhani2023mx}.

\emph{A block by hand.} Take \Chref{weight-quantization}'s eight weights, 0.90,
$-0.35$, 0.12, 0.05, $-0.61$, 0.28, $-0.02$, 0.44, as an MXFP4 block. The largest
magnitude is 0.9, $\lfloor \log_2 0.9 \rfloor = -1$, so $e_{\text{shared}} = -3$ and
$X = 1/8$. Dividing by $X$ gives 7.2, $-2.8$, 0.96, 0.4, $-4.88$, 2.24, $-0.16$ and
3.52; rounding to the E2M1 grid gives 6, $-3$, 1, 0.5, $-4$, 2, 0 and 4; and
multiplying back gives 0.75, $-0.375$, 0.125, 0.0625, $-0.5$, 0.25, 0 and 0.5.
The block's largest weight has lost a sixth of its value: 7.2 lies above the
grid's top, 6, and was clamped. The relative error over the block is 15.9\%,
against 7.4\% for \Chref{weight-quantization}'s 4-bit integers with one exact
scale (derived; the lab's test checks the block).

The clamp is not bad luck; it is built in. A power-of-two scale maps the
block's largest value somewhere in the top binade, $[4X, 8X)$ for E2M1, but the
format's grid stops at $6X$, so every block whose maximum lands in $(6X, 8X)$
saturates. The second worked problem predicts how often that happens, and the
lab counts it on real weights: 38\% of the MXFP4 blocks of a real projection
clamp their largest value, and even MXFP8, whose E4M3 grid stops at
$1.75 \times 2^{8}$, clamps 18\%. The clamp belongs to the recipe, not to the
format. The MX paper offers Algorithm~1 as a working example and says in so many
words that the specification allows other, implementation-defined conversion
recipes \cite{rouhani2023mx}; a converter that chose the scale one binade higher
would never clamp and would round every element on a grid twice as coarse. The
bytes on disk do not say which recipe made them, which is one more reason to
measure a quantized checkpoint rather than trust its format's name.

\section{NVFP4: two-level scaling}

NVIDIA's NVFP4 keeps the E2M1 elements but changes the scaling
\cite{nvidia2025nvfp4}: blocks of 16 instead of 32, and a scale stored in E4M3
--- a real fraction, not just a power of two --- chosen to fit the block
closely. Because an E4M3 scale has limited range of its own, a second, FP32
scale per tensor brings each tensor's scales into the E4M3 range. NVIDIA's
NVFP4 pretraining paper writes the recipe as equations
\cite{nvidia2025nvfp4pretrain}: with $a_{\text{tensor}}$ the tensor's largest
magnitude and $a_b$ block $b$'s,
\begin{equation}
s_{\text{enc}} = \frac{6 \cdot 448}{a_{\text{tensor}}},\qquad
S_b = \operatorname{e4m3}\!\left(\frac{a_b}{6}\, s_{\text{enc}}\right),\qquad
\hat{v}_i = \operatorname{e2m1}\!\left(v_i \frac{s_{\text{enc}}}{S_b}\right)\frac{S_b}{s_{\text{enc}}},
\label{eq:nvfp4}
\end{equation}
so that the tensor's largest block scale lands on E4M3's largest value, 448, and
each block's largest element lands on E2M1's, 6. The price is a quarter of a
bit per value more than MXFP4 (4.5 against 4.25); the gain is smaller blocks and
scales that fit. The same eight weights show it: $s_{\text{enc}} = 2{,}986.7$, the
block scale $0.9/6 = 0.15$ is stored exactly (as 448), and the weights divided by
0.15 --- 6, $-2.33$, 0.8, 0.33, $-4.07$, 1.87, $-0.13$, 2.93 --- round to 6, $-2$, 1,
0.5, $-4$, 2, 0 and 3, which are 0.9, $-0.3$, 0.15, 0.075, $-0.6$, 0.3, 0 and 0.45.
The largest weight survives exactly, and the block's error falls from 15.9\% to
5.6\% (derived). NVIDIA reports that DeepSeek-R1-0528 served in NVFP4 lost 1\%
or less of accuracy against FP8 on its evaluations (vendor measurement, not
reproduced here).

\noindent Four lessons, each against a common assumption. \emph{Mantissa bits
buy precision, exponent bits buy range, and weights need little range once
they are scaled.} E2M3, six bits with three mantissa bits, beats both 8-bit
E5M2 formats with two; E3M2 matches MXFP8 (E5M2) to the fourth digit, because
within a scaled block the extra exponent range is never used. \emph{For weights,
integers with fine scales beat FP8.} Q8\_0-style 8-bit integers with a scale
per 32 are three times more accurate than FP8 with one scale per tensor: the
floating-point grid spends its levels on range a scaled block does not need.
FP8's case rests on activations, whose range is dynamic, and on tensor cores
that multiply it natively. \emph{Power-of-two scales cost precision.} MXFP8
trails per-tensor FP8 on these weights because of its clamped blocks, and MXFP4
trails NVFP4 by 2.5 points of output error --- the clamp again, and blocks twice
as long. \emph{At four bits floating point beats integers in the output.} NVFP4
and MXFP4 both beat 4-bit integers with a scale per 32 on the layer's output
(10.3\% and 12.8\% against 15.1\%), though MXFP4's weight error is the larger:
the grids put their error on different weights, and \Chref{weight-quantization}
showed that where the error lands is what the output feels.

\section{The same formats on the activations}

Weights are one operand of a product; the activations are the other, and they
are a different kind of tensor. The lab quantizes the same layer's inputs
instead --- the RMS-normalized embeddings of 897 distinct tokens --- keeps the
weights exact, and measures the error in the output again (measured, record
\texttt{2026-09-26-m1-pass3}). First the inputs themselves: in each token's
vector of 3,072 values, the largest is on average 13.3 times the vector's
root-mean-square, where a Gaussian vector of that length would reach about 3.7,
and the loudest channel's mean magnitude is 12.3 times the average channel's.
These are the outlier channels of \Chref{weight-quantization}, at the very
first layer.

\section{One outlier in a block}

Activations, unlike weights, carry outliers: a few channels whose values are
tens of times larger than the rest (\Chref{weight-quantization}). The lab puts
one such value, 30, in a block of 31 values between $-1$ and 1 and asks each
format to hold it. MXFP8 (E4M3) keeps the small values to within 2.6\% and
clamps the outlier to 28. MXFP4 keeps nothing: the shared exponent puts
$X = 4$, every small value divided by 4 is at most 0.25, and E2M1's smallest
non-zero value is 0.5, so all 31 round to zero --- and the outlier itself clamps
to 24. NVFP4 represents the outlier exactly and loses the 15 small values that
share its 16-element block; the other block keeps a scale of its own, and only
one of its values, a tiny one, rounds to zero. That is 16 of 31 set to zero and
70\% error on the small values: bad, but half the damage (measured).
This is why the formats split the tensors the way this chapter's next sections
describe: four bits are for weights, which have no outliers of that size;
activations get eight bits, fine blocks, or both.

\section{Tensor cores that consume scales}

A format is only fast if the hardware multiplies it directly. Blackwell's
fifth-generation tensor-core instructions include block-scaled variants that
take the scale factors alongside the operands: one UE8M0 scale per 32 elements
of the reduction dimension for the MX types, one UE4M3 scale per 16 for NVFP4
(CUTLASS documentation, logged in \texttt{research/FRONTIER.md}). The kernel
never expands a block to 16 bits in registers; the tensor core applies the
scales inside the multiply--accumulate. Because the blocks run along the
reduction dimension, a matrix quantized for $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}$ is not
quantized for $\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}$: the MX paper notes that converting
to an MX format and transposing do not commute \cite{rouhani2023mx}, which
matters to training, whose backward pass multiplies by the transpose.

\emph{Why a block-scaled product is cheap.} For two vectors in an MX format,
the dot product splits by blocks:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \sum_{\text{blocks } j} X^{(a)}_j X^{(b)}_j
\sum_{i \in j} p^{(a)}_i\, p^{(b)}_i ,
\label{eq:block-dot}
\end{equation}
with the elements' products inside and the scales outside. E2M1 values are
halves of the integers 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8 and 12, so a product of two is a
quarter of an integer no larger than 144, and a block's sum of 32 such products
is a quarter of an integer no larger than 4,608: exactly representable in FP32
(derived). The inner sum can therefore be computed without rounding, and for MX
the scale product $X^{(a)}_j X^{(b)}_j = 2^{e_a + e_b}$ is an addition of
exponents. A four-element example shows the arithmetic: $(1.5, -2, 0.5, 6) \cdot
(4, 3, -1, 0.5)$. Doubled, as llama.cpp stores them (below), the elements are the
integers $(3, -4, 1, 12)$ and $(8, 6, -2, 1)$, whose integer dot product is
$24 - 24 - 2 + 12 = 10$; dividing by the four the doubling introduced gives 2.5,
exactly the real dot product $6 - 6 - 0.5 + 3$. With scales $2^{-3}$ and $2^{5}$,
the block contributes $2.5 \times 2^{2} = 10$ to the accumulator, and no bit was
lost until that addition. Rounding enters only when the blocks are accumulated. NVFP4's E4M3
scales make the outer product a real multiplication, of two 4-bit significands,
which is still small beside the 32 multiply--adds it scales.

\emph{How an engine without FP4 tensor cores stores it.} llama.cpp reads MXFP4
checkpoints such as gpt-oss's into a 17-byte block: one E8M0 byte and sixteen
bytes of 4-bit codes, 4.25 bits per value. Its decoder turns each code into an
8-bit integer from a table of the E2M1 values \emph{doubled} --- 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6,
8, 12 and their negatives --- and multiplies by half of $2^{e-127}$ to compensate
(\texttt{block\_mxfp4}, \texttt{kvalues\_mxfp4} and
\texttt{dequantize\_row\_mxfp4} in \texttt{ggml} at \texttt{d006858}). Every
weight is then an 8-bit integer times a power of two, which puts MXFP4 on the
integer dot-product path that \Chref{weight-quantization}'s lab found fastest on
a CPU. The same build defines an NVFP4 block as well: 64 values with four UE4M3
scales, one per 16, and 32 bytes of codes --- 36 bytes, 4.5 bits per value, the
per-tensor scale kept outside the block.

Hopper has FP8 tensor cores but no FP4, and its FP8 path has a subtlety that
DeepSeek found in production training: on H800 GPUs, FP8 matrix products keep
only about 14 bits of precision in their accumulators. Summing thousands of
products at that precision loses accuracy, so DeepSeek copies partial sums to
FP32 registers every 128 elements of the reduction and accumulates there
\cite{deepseek2024v3}. The same arithmetic happens in an FP8 inference GEMM on
the same hardware; the fix costs some of the speed the format bought.

\section{Weights, activations and the cache}

The three kinds of tensor tolerate low precision differently. Weights are
fixed: they can be quantized once, with care, offline, and checked. Activations
are computed per request, contain outlier channels, and must be quantized on
the fly with scales computed from the data itself, which costs a reduction per
block at run time, and the lab's layer showed what four bits do to them. The KV cache is written once and read at every later step;
its errors enter every attention score that follows
(\Chref{kv-quantization}). A deployment chooses per tensor: gpt-oss keeps
attention and embeddings in 16 bits and stores only expert weights in MXFP4;
Kimi~K3 reports MXFP4 weights with MXFP8 activations
\cite{moonshot2026kimik3}.

\section{Training in low precision changes inference}

Until recently, low precision was something done to a model after training,
with an accuracy cost to be measured and minimized. The 2025--26 checkpoints
changed that. DeepSeek-V3 was trained in FP8 and published with FP8 weights in
$128\times128$ blocks, so serving it in FP8 introduces no conversion at all
\cite{deepseek2024v3}. gpt-oss was post-trained with its expert weights in
MXFP4, and its reported evaluations use the quantized model
\cite{openai2025gptoss}. Kimi~K3 reports quantization-aware training for its
MXFP4 weights \cite{moonshot2026kimik3}. NVIDIA trained a 12-billion-parameter
model on 10~trillion tokens in NVFP4 with loss matching an FP8 baseline, and
it took more than a format to do it: random Hadamard transforms to spread
outliers before quantizing, two-dimensional scaling so that a weight matrix and
its transpose see consistent blocks, stochastic rounding for gradients, and a
few layers kept in higher precision \cite{nvidia2025nvfp4pretrain}. Each of
those is an answer to something this chapter measured: the outliers of the
activation table, and the MX paper's warning that quantizing and transposing do
not commute. When a model is trained or post-trained in a
format, the question ``how much accuracy does quantization cost?'' changes to
``what does it cost to serve this model in any \emph{other} format?'' The
lab's numbers above are the post-training answer for one layer of a model
that was trained in 16 bits; a model trained for the format has learned to
live on its grid.

\section{Choosing a format}

The measurements and sources of this chapter reduce to a short decision
procedure. \emph{First, ask what the model was made in.} A model trained or
post-trained in a format --- DeepSeek-V3 in FP8 blocks, gpt-oss's experts in
MXFP4 --- is served most faithfully in that format, and any other format is a
new quantization to be measured. \emph{Second, ask what the hardware multiplies.}
On Blackwell, FP4 weights and FP8 activations run on the tensor cores at two to
four times the BF16 rate; between the two four-bit formats, the lab's layer
favoured NVFP4 (10.3\% output error against MXFP4's 12.8\%). On Hopper, FP8 is
the native low-precision type and FP4 checkpoints must be converted or decoded
in the kernel; per-block FP8 scales, as in DeepSeek-V3, avoid per-tensor
fragility, with FP32 promotion of partial sums to keep the accumulators honest.
On CPUs and Apple GPUs no floating-point format below 16 bits has dedicated
matrix hardware in this book's measurements, and integer block formats win
(\Chref{weight-quantization}). \emph{Third, separate the tensors.} For weights,
precision within a scaled block matters and range does not: on the lab's layer,
8-bit integers with a scale per 32 were three times more accurate than FP8 with
one scale, and six bits with three mantissa bits beat eight with two. For
activations and the KV cache, range matters: outliers are real
(\Chref{kv-quantization}), and a four-bit format with 32-value blocks loses
whole blocks to them. \emph{Fourth, measure the output, not the tensor}: every
comparison in this chapter was decided by the layer's output error, and in two
cases --- AWQ in \Chref{weight-quantization}, MXFP4 against INT4 here --- the
output reversed the verdict of the weight error.

Read this as a representation path rather than a promise of tensor-core speed. The two operands may use different encodings and scaling rules. A format is useful only when a supported kernel can consume that complete representation and produce the required output.

\LedgerSection

Serving a model's large matrices in a four-bit block-scaled format (MXFP4 or
NVFP4) instead of BF16:

\begin{ConstraintLedger}
Memory bandwidth & buys & 3.6--3.8$\times$ fewer bytes per weight read (equation~\eqref{eq:bits-per-value}). \\
Memory capacity & buys & gpt-oss-120b in 65~GB instead of about 234~GB (measured file sizes; derived). \\
Compute & buys & 4$\times$ the dense tensor-core peak of BF16 on Blackwell; nothing on Hopper, which has no FP4 tensor cores. \\
Correctness & risks & On a real layer of a model trained in 16 bits: 10.3\% output error in NVFP4, 12.8\% in MXFP4, whose power-of-two scales clamp 38\% of blocks (measured); negligible when the model was trained for the format, to be measured when it was not. \\
\end{ConstraintLedger}

\CodeLines{../code/labs/ch11-block-formats/src/formats.rs}{210}{232}

\noindent The tensor scale is computed once (line 212); each block's scale is the

\HappenedSection{fourteen bits of accumulator}

DeepSeek's FP8 training run is the clearest recorded case of a low-precision
format failing silently in a production kernel and being fixed by design
\cite{deepseek2024v3}. FP8 inputs were not the problem; the accumulator was.
The H800's FP8 matrix instructions accumulate at about 14 bits of precision,
enough for short reductions and not for the thousands of products in a
large matrix product. The fix changed the kernel's structure rather than the
format: partial sums leave the tensor cores every 128 elements for FP32
registers on the ordinary cores, where the long sum is completed. It is a
reminder that a format's specification describes its storage, not the
precision of every operation performed on it --- and that the only way to find
the difference is to test against a higher-precision oracle.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-27}

Four-bit floating point moved from research to shipping checkpoints in about a
year: MXFP4 in gpt-oss (August 2025) and Kimi~K3 (2026), NVFP4 pretraining
at 10~trillion tokens \cite{nvidia2025nvfp4pretrain}. Blackwell Ultra parts
list more dense FP4 throughput than B200 while trimming INT8 and FP64 (NVIDIA
HGX specification), and NVIDIA's Rubin, ``ramping into full production'' in
2026, lists 35~PFLOP/s of
dense NVFP4 for training against 17.5 of FP8 and 4 of BF16 per GPU, with
50~PFLOP/s of NVFP4 for inference quoted under a sparse specification
\cite{nvidia2026verarubin}: every generation widens the gap between four bits
and sixteen. AMD's MI355X goes further for six bits: it lists 10.1 dense
PFLOP/s for MXFP6 and MXFP4 alike, twice its FP8 rate and four times its BF16
rate, so on that part a six-bit format costs no more arithmetic time than a
four-bit one \cite{amd2025mi355x} --- and the lab's table says six bits buy
four times less error.
