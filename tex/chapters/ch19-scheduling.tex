\chapter{Scheduling Under Pressure: Preemption, Fairness and SLO-Aware Admission}\label{ch:scheduling}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
Demand eventually exceeds memory. Then the scheduler must decide which
requests run, which wait and which are evicted, and whether an evicted request
is recomputed or swapped out. This chapter covers admission control,
preemption by recomputation and by swapping, fairness between tenants,
priorities and deadlines, and admission that is aware of service-level
objectives, using a little queueing theory to explain why a system near
saturation behaves so badly. Its target metric is goodput --- throughput that
meets its latency objectives.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{Out of memory at the peak}

Give llama.cpp's server one shared cache of 4,096 cells and four request slots,
and send it four requests at once, each with a 200-token prompt and asking for
1,200 tokens. Together they need about 5,600 cells, so the cache must run out,
and on the M1 it did: after each request had generated 824 tokens, the next
step found no free cell. The server retried with smaller and smaller batches,
down to a single token, and then sent the same error to every request in
flight: \emph{Context size has been exceeded}. \textbf{All four failed at the
same instant}, 73.5 seconds after they were sent, and none received the rest of
its answer. With 8,192 cells, the same four requests all completed their 1,200
tokens in 113 seconds (measured; record \texttt{2026-09-24-m1-part3}).

The behavior is written down in the server's source. When a decode step fails
for lack of cache space even at a batch of one, the loop sends an error to
every slot that is processing and releases them all; the comment beside it
reads, in part, \emph{try to terminate only the largest active slot}, a change
not yet made (\texttt{tools/server/server-context.cpp} at commit
\texttt{d006858}). vLLM's scheduler handles the same moment differently: it
evicts one running request --- the most recently admitted, or the lowest
priority --- frees its cache, and puts it back at the head of the queue to be
recomputed later (\texttt{vllm/v1/core/sched/scheduler.py} at commit
\texttt{bcdacfc}). At the peak, the policy decides whether one user waits or
every user fails.

\Foundations{Arrival rate, service rate and overload}{
A queue stores work that has arrived but cannot yet run. If offered work persistently
exceeds sustainable service, waiting grows unless the system rejects, sheds or slows
arrivals. Memory can fill before compute reaches its limit because each waiting or
active request may retain state. Preemption interrupts admitted work; admission
control decides whether to accept it in the first place. Neither guarantees fairness
without a declared service policy. Chapter~\ref{ch:continuous-batching} supplies the
execution unit. Chapter~\ref{app:reproducing} distinguishes open-loop arrivals from
clients that reduce their offered load whenever the server slows.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: utilization, queues and Little's law}

Two laws explain why serving systems degrade abruptly. Little's law says that
the average number of requests in a system equals their arrival rate times
their average time in it, $N = \lambda T$, for any stable system. And for a
single server with random arrivals and service times, the classic M/M/1 model
gives the mean time in system
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{1/\mu}{1-\rho},\qquad \rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu},
\label{eq:mm1}
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is the service rate and $\rho$ the utilization. At 50\%
utilization a request spends twice its service time in the system; at 90\%, ten
times; at 95\%, twenty (derived). An LLM server is not an M/M/1 queue ---
continuous batching serves many requests at once, and service times depend on
the batch --- but the shape survives: near saturation, small increases in load
produce large increases in waiting, and by Little's law the number of requests
held, each with its cache, grows with the waiting.

That is the second pressure. The cache a running request needs grows every
step, and its final size depends on an output length nobody knows at admission.
A scheduler can reserve for the worst case and waste memory
(\Chref{paged-kv}), or admit optimistically and prepare to take memory back ---
which is what preemption is.

\section{Admission: will this request fit?}

Admission control decides whether a new request may start. A conservative
policy admits a request only if its prompt and its maximum output fit; it never
preempts and wastes the unused reservation. An optimistic policy admits a
request if its prompt fits now, keeps a margin of free blocks for the running
requests to grow, and preempts when the margin is exhausted. Engines choose the
optimistic side because output lengths are skewed: most requests stop long
before their limit. llama.cpp's server, in the opening, admits anything that
fits a slot's context and has no preemption, so its optimism ends in failure
rather than in waiting.

\section{Preemption by recomputation}

To preempt by recomputation, the scheduler frees a victim's cache blocks,
keeps the tokens it has already generated, and returns it to the waiting queue.
When it runs again, its prompt and its generated tokens are prefilled together
--- one pass of compute, at prefill efficiency --- and decoding continues where
it stopped. The output is unaffected, because the recomputed cache is the same
function of the same tokens. vLLM's V1 engine preempts this way by default,
choosing recomputation over swapping because of its lower overhead in that
engine \cite{vllm2026optimization}, and its implementation resets the victim's
computed-token count to zero and prepends it to the queue so that it resumes
first. A prefix cache (\Chref{prefix-caching}) can make the recomputation
partly free, if the victim's blocks are still cached when it returns.

\section{Preemption by swapping}

The alternative moves the victim's blocks to host memory and back. Swapping
spends interconnect bandwidth twice instead of compute once, and host memory
instead of nothing. It wins when the cache is large relative to the compute
that would rebuild it --- long contexts, cheap host transfers --- and loses when
a short prompt could simply be prefilled again. The same arithmetic reappears
when caches are deliberately kept in host memory or on disk
(\Chref{kv-storage-tier}).

\section{Fairness between tenants}

First come, first served lets one client's burst occupy every slot while
another client's single request waits behind it. Counting requests does not
fix this, because requests differ in cost by orders of magnitude. The Virtual
Token Counter scheduler measures service in tokens --- a weighted count of
input and output tokens processed for each client --- and always serves the
backlogged client that has received the least \cite{sheng2024vtc}. Its authors
proved that over any interval, however long, the service received by two
backlogged clients differs by at most twice the larger of two quantities --- the
weighted cost of the longest prompt and of the most tokens the running batch
can hold --- and that the bound is tight, while the scheduler stays
work-conserving: spare capacity is never withheld (their analysis).

\section{Priorities and deadlines}

Some requests matter more: an interactive user ahead of a batch job, a paying
tier ahead of a free one. vLLM offers a priority scheduling policy in which
each request carries a priority value, and when it must preempt it picks the
running request with the lowest priority, breaking ties by the latest arrival
(\texttt{vllm/config/scheduler.py} and \texttt{vllm/v1/core/sched/scheduler.py}
at commit \texttt{bcdacfc}). Deadlines are harder than priorities: a request
whose deadline has passed is worth nothing, so continuing to serve it wastes
the capacity that could rescue another.

\section{Admission that knows the objective}

The measure that captures this is goodput. DistServe defined it as the maximum
request rate a system can serve while meeting both a time-to-first-token and a
time-per-output-token objective for a stated fraction of requests
\cite{zhong2024distserve}. Throughput counts every token; goodput counts only
requests that met their objectives. An admission policy aimed at goodput
rejects, early, requests it predicts will miss: a request refused in
milliseconds costs its client a retry, while a request admitted, prefilled and
then abandoned costs everyone the compute it consumed.

\section{Overload: shedding and backpressure}

When demand stays above capacity, some work must be refused; the only choice
is where. Refusing at the door --- an immediate error with a hint to retry
later --- keeps the requests already admitted within their objectives.
Accepting everything lets queues and caches grow until requests time out
after consuming resources, or, as in the opening, fail together. Two further
rules matter at scale: bound the queue, so that waiting time cannot grow
without limit; and make retries back off, because clients that retry at once
multiply the load that caused the refusal.

\LedgerSection

Admitting optimistically and preempting under memory pressure, with fair and
objective-aware admission:

\begin{ConstraintLedger}
Memory capacity & buys & More concurrent requests than worst-case reservation allows; memory is reclaimed from one victim instead of failing everyone. \\
Compute & spends & Recomputation of each preempted request's prompt and generated tokens. \\
Memory bandwidth & spends & Swapping, when used: each victim's cache crosses the interconnect twice. \\
Latency & moves & From the requests that keep running to the victims and the rejected; goodput, not mean latency, is the target. \\
Correctness & risks & A recomputed cache must equal the evicted one; fairness and priority change who waits, not what is computed. \\
\end{ConstraintLedger}

\HappenedSection{early rejection in a production system}

Moonshot AI's Mooncake, which serves the Kimi assistant, separates prefill and
decode onto different machines (\Chref{disaggregated-serving}), and its
designers wrote down what overload did to it \cite{qin2024mooncake}. A request
rejected by the decode cluster after its prefill had finished wasted the
prefill's compute, so they rejected requests early, at arrival, when the decode
cluster was predicted to be too busy. That created a new failure: the two
clusters' loads began to oscillate in opposite phases. Rejections starved the
prefill cluster of work; acceptance then overloaded decode; which triggered
rejections again. Their fix was to base the decision on a prediction of the
decode load at the time the request would arrive there, not on its current
value.

They measured the policies by replaying a trace of 23,000 requests at twice
its original speed. The baseline rejected 4,183 requests; early rejection,
3,771; prediction-based early rejection, 3,589 (the authors' measurement). The
lesson generalizes beyond disaggregation: an admission controller is a feedback
loop, and a feedback loop that reacts to the present value of a delayed quantity
can oscillate.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-24}

Recomputation has become the default preemption policy in vLLM's V1 engine,
with priority scheduling as an option \cite{vllm2026optimization}; the
research frontier is scheduling for goodput under service-level objectives ---
fairness measured in tokens \cite{sheng2024vtc}, admission that predicts
load \cite{qin2024mooncake}, and disaggregated deployments whose two pools
are scheduled separately \cite{zhong2024distserve}. llama.cpp's server, in the
measured build, still fails every in-flight request when its cache is
exhausted, a limitation its own source records.
