\chapter{Mixture of Experts}\label{ch:mixture-of-experts}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
A mixture-of-experts layer routes each token to a few of many feed-forward
experts, so a model can hold hundreds of billions of parameters while each
token touches only a few billion. That decouples memory capacity from
per-token bandwidth and compute --- until batching, because a batch of tokens
activates the union of their experts. This chapter derives routing, the
active-versus-total parameter split, the expert union as a function of batch
size, load balance and capacity, and the grouped GEMMs that execute experts
efficiently.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{Thirty billion parameters, three billion per token}

On the M1's GPU, llama.cpp multiplies one token by one of Qwen3-30B-A3B's
expert projections --- the 8 experts the token selects out of its layer's 128,
each a 2,048-by-768 matrix in 4-bit K-quant form --- in 0.10 milliseconds.
Eight tokens take 0.92 milliseconds: each pays as if it were alone. Thirty-two
tokens take 6.2 milliseconds, more per token than one token alone. Then the
curve turns: 64 tokens take 7.1 milliseconds and 256 take 7.3, \textbf{0.029
milliseconds per token}, less than a third of the single-token cost (measured
with the engine's own kernel benchmark at commit \texttt{d006858}, three runs;
record \texttt{2026-09-24-m1-part5}; Figure~\ref{fig:ch26-expert-batch}).

Two things produce that shape. A batch's tokens together select almost every
expert --- 8 for one token, about 112 of the 128 for 32 tokens under uniform
routing (derived below) --- so the weights a batch must read grow quickly and
then stop growing. And the engine changes method at 32 tokens: below that it
multiplies each token by each of its experts separately, above it sorts the
batch by expert and multiplies each expert's group of tokens at once. The
first is the arithmetic of mixture of experts; the second is the engineering.

The model these matrices come from holds 30.5 billion parameters and uses 3.3
billion per token. At batch 1 it decodes like a small model; at batch 64 it
reads nearly all of its experts every step; and it needs every one of them
resident, which is why this chapter measures one layer's kernel instead of a
model that does not fit on the laptop.

\Foundations{Stored parameters and selected parameters}{
A router chooses which expert networks process a token. Parameters in unselected
experts still occupy storage even though that token does not execute them. Across a
batch, different tokens may select different experts; the union of selected experts
determines how much weight reuse is possible. Routing itself costs work, and unequal
assignments create stragglers. A sparse model is therefore neither a dense model of
its total size nor automatically one of its active size. Chapter~\ref{ch:forward-pass}
introduces the expert computation; Chapter~\ref{ch:roofline} explains why a batch
changes the bytes-per-token estimate.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: the expert union as batch grows}

Take a layer with $E$ experts of which each token selects $k$. If routing
were uniform and independent across tokens, an expert escapes one token's
selection with probability $1-k/E$, and a batch of $B$ tokens activates on
average
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[U(B)] = E\left(1-\Bigl(1-\frac{k}{E}\Bigr)^{B}\right)
\label{eq:expert-union}
\end{equation}
distinct experts. For Qwen3-30B-A3B ($E=128$, $k=8$) the union is 8 experts
for one token, 29 for 4, 52 for 8, 82 for 16, 112 for 32 and 126 for 64
(derived). A decode step reads the weights of every expert in the union, so the
bytes of expert weights read \emph{per token} fall from 8 experts' worth at
batch 1 to about 2 at batch 64 and 1 at batch 128 --- the same amortization a
dense model gets from batching (\Chref{roofline}), arriving later.

The arithmetic does not amortize: every token still does its own $k$ experts'
work. Once the union covers every expert, a step reads all $P$ parameters and
computes with $P_{\mathrm{act}}$ per token, so its intensity is about
$2BP_{\mathrm{act}}/(P\,b_w)$ FLOP per byte, and it reaches a machine's ridge
$I^{*}$ at
\begin{equation}
B^{*} \approx \frac{I^{*}\,b_w}{2}\cdot\frac{P}{P_{\mathrm{act}}} .
\label{eq:moe-ridge-batch}
\end{equation}
The first factor is the dense model's answer; the second is the sparsity.
With 8-bit weights on an H100 ($I^{*}\approx 590$ FLOP per byte in FP8), a
dense model becomes compute-bound near 300 tokens per step, and Qwen3-30B-A3B
($P/P_{\mathrm{act}} = 30.5/3.3 \approx 9$) near 2,700 (derived). An MoE model
is cheap per token only for a server that can gather thousands of tokens per
step --- which is why the largest MoE deployments spread one model over
hundreds of GPUs (\Chref{expert-parallelism}).

Routing is not uniform in practice, and skew helps the union: if a few experts
take most tokens, fewer distinct experts are read per step. It hurts
elsewhere, as the load-balance section shows.

\section{Routing and the sparse FFN}

An MoE layer replaces the transformer's feed-forward network
(\Chref{forward-pass}) with $E$ smaller ones and a router. The router is a
linear map from the hidden state to $E$ scores; the layer keeps the $k$ highest,
turns them into gate weights, runs the token through those $k$ experts and sums
their outputs weighted by the gates. Shazeer and colleagues introduced the
sparsely gated layer in 2017, with up to 137 billion parameters and ``greater
than 1000x improvements in model capacity'' for minor losses in efficiency
\cite{shazeer2017moe}. GShard scaled it to a 600-billion-parameter translation
model trained on 2,048 TPU v3 accelerators in four days \cite{lepikhin2021gshard},
and Switch Transformers simplified routing to one expert per token and trained
models of up to a trillion parameters, with pre-training up to 7 times faster
than dense models of the same compute \cite{fedus2022switch}.

Current models vary the details. Mixtral routes each token to 2 of 8 experts,
47 billion parameters with 13 billion active \cite{jiang2024mixtral};
DeepSeek-V3 computes a sigmoid affinity for each of 256 routed experts, keeps
8, normalizes the selected scores into gate values and adds one shared expert
that every token uses (report section 2.1.2, \cite{deepseek2024v3});
gpt-oss-120b selects 4 of 128; Qwen3.5-397B 10 of 512 plus a shared expert; and
Kimi~K3 16 of 896 (configurations in \texttt{research/FRONTIER.md}). The
router's own cost is negligible: a $d \times E$ matrix, 0.26 million parameters
per layer for Qwen3-30B-A3B.

\section{Active and total parameters}

Qwen3-30B-A3B's name states the split. Each of its 48 layers holds 128
experts of three $2{,}048 \times 768$ matrices, 4.7 million parameters each and
604 million per layer, 29.0 billion in all; a token uses 8 per layer, 1.8
billion. Attention, embeddings and routers add about 1.5 billion used by every
token, giving the model card's ``30.5B in total and 3.3B activated'' (derived;
\texttt{config.json} and model card, \texttt{research/FRONTIER.md}).

The two numbers bind different resources. Memory capacity pays for $P$:
every expert must be somewhere a step can reach it. Compute pays for
$P_{\mathrm{act}}$. Bandwidth pays for something in between that depends on the
batch --- $P_{\mathrm{act}}$ at batch 1, approaching $P$ as the union grows
(Equation~\ref{eq:expert-union}). A dense model has one number for all three
resources; an MoE model's ledger has three.

\section{Batching activates the union}

The union is the MoE engine's central quantity. At batch 1 an MoE model decodes
like a model of its active size: in 4-bit form, the experts one token of
Qwen3-30B-A3B touches come to 1.02~GiB --- 48 layers of 8 experts of 2.5 to
2.9~MiB each --- out of 16.35~GiB for all of them (Hermon
\texttt{docs/KERNEL\_DESIGN.md}, section K6.1, from the packed checkpoint). At
batch 64 the same step reads 98\% of its expert
weights (derived), and the model decodes like a dense 30-billion-parameter model
that happens to compute less --- bandwidth-bound on $P$, not $P_{\mathrm{act}}$.

This makes MoE capacity planning different from dense. A dense model's
decode cost per token falls steadily with batch; an MoE model's falls slowly
while the union grows, then like a dense model's once the union saturates. The
batch at which the union covers most experts is small --- at $B = 2E/k$, 32
tokens for Qwen3-30B-A3B, it covers 87\% of them --- and the batch that
saturates compute is large (Equation~\ref{eq:moe-ridge-batch}). Between them an MoE server is
bandwidth-bound on its total size, and the only ways to go faster are more
bandwidth (more devices, \Chref{expert-parallelism}) or larger batches.

\section{Load balance and capacity}

A router trained without constraint tends to collapse onto a few experts.
Training therefore adds pressure toward balance: an auxiliary loss that
penalizes uneven load in Switch and GShard \cite{fedus2022switch}, or
DeepSeek-V3's per-expert bias, added to the affinity scores only when choosing
the top $k$, raised when an expert is underloaded and lowered when it is
overloaded \cite{wang2024lossfree,deepseek2024v3}. The gate values still come
from the unbiased scores, so balance is bought without an extra loss term.

Inference inherits whatever balance training achieved, and imbalance costs
differently there. Switch Transformers gave every expert a fixed capacity,
$(\text{tokens per batch}/\text{number of experts}) \times$ a capacity factor,
and passed the overflow tokens to the next layer unprocessed through the
residual connection (section 2.2, \cite{fedus2022switch}). An engine that
dropped tokens that way would make a request's output depend on what else was
in its batch. The systems examined here are dropless: DeepSeek-V3 drops no tokens in
training or inference (section 2.1.2, \cite{deepseek2024v3}); MegaBlocks
reformulated the computation with block-sparse kernels precisely so that no
token is dropped and no expert padded to a fixed capacity \cite{gale2023megablocks};
and llama.cpp's expert kernel computes every selected expert for every token.
Without dropping, imbalance becomes time: on one device, an overloaded expert's
matrix product is the long pole of the step; across devices, the device holding
it becomes every step's straggler. DeepSeek's answer at scale is to duplicate
the hot experts, chosen from live load statistics every ten minutes or so ---
32 redundant experts in their prefill deployment (section 3.4.1,
\cite{deepseek2024v3}).

\section{Grouped GEMM}

Executing a batch through its experts is a gather, many small matrix products
and a scatter. The naive loop --- for each token, for each of its experts,
multiply --- turns a batch into $Bk$ matrix--vector products and throws away
everything batching was for. Engines instead sort the batch's (token, expert)
pairs by expert, so each expert multiplies one contiguous block of its tokens,
and run all experts' blocks in one kernel launch: a grouped GEMM.
vLLM's fused MoE path sorts the token indices by assigned expert and pads each
expert's block to a multiple of the kernel's tile size
(\texttt{moe\_align\_block\_size} at commit \texttt{bcdacfc});
DeepGEMM groups along the token dimension only, with a contiguous layout for
prefill and a masked layout for decode under CUDA graphs, where the CPU does not
know how many tokens each expert received \cite{deepseek2025deepgemm}.
llama.cpp's Metal backend at \texttt{d006858} switches between the two regimes
by batch: below 32 rows it runs a matrix--vector kernel per selected expert,
and from 32 it first maps tokens to experts and then runs its matrix--matrix
kernel over each expert's group (\texttt{ggml/src/ggml-metal/ggml-metal-ops.cpp}).

Grouping has its own waste. At decode, $Bk$ pairs spread over the union give
each expert $Bk/U(B)$ tokens --- 4 each for Qwen3-30B-A3B at batch 64 --- so
tiles sized for 64 or 128 rows run mostly empty. That is why MoE decode stays
bandwidth-bound far past the batch at which a dense model's GEMM fills its
tiles.

The opening's measurement shows both regimes, and a third. On the
matrix--vector path, below 32 tokens, time grew in proportion to the batch:
each (token, expert) pair read its expert's weights again, at 61 to 69~GB/s,
at or near the M1's bandwidth (the benchmark repeats one call over the same
weights, so on-chip caches may flatter the highest figure). On the grouped path the 16-bit experts ran at the
union's bandwidth, about 50~GB/s. The 4-bit experts did not: their grouped
kernel moved the union's bytes at about 16~GB/s, a third of the rate, and at
512 tokens it took longer than the 16-bit one (10.7 against 9.9 milliseconds).
Dequantizing inside a matrix--matrix kernel costs arithmetic, and once the
union saturates it is arithmetic, not bytes, that the 4-bit experts run out of
(measured; the derived union lines are in Figure~\ref{fig:ch26-expert-batch}).

\section{Shared and fine-grained experts}

DeepSeekMoE changed the shape of experts rather than their number
\cite{dai2024deepseekmoe}. It split each expert into $m$ smaller ones and
activated $m$ times as many, so that combinations could specialize more
finely, and it isolated a few shared experts that every token uses, to hold
common knowledge the routed experts would otherwise all duplicate. Its authors
report that a 16-billion-parameter DeepSeekMoE matched LLaMA2~7B with about 40\%
of the computation (their measurement). Fine-grained experts are now the norm
--- 128 to 896 per layer, 4 to 16 active --- and shared experts are common
(DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3.5, Gemma~4's 26B~A4B).

For the engine, both are good news and bad. Finer experts mean smaller
matrices, which are harder to run efficiently, and more of them to schedule;
the fraction of the model a batch touches depends on $k/E$, which fine-graining
leaves unchanged (Equation~\ref{eq:expert-union}). A shared expert is a small dense FFN inside every MoE layer: always
resident, always read, batched like a dense layer, and a natural thing to keep
on every device when the routed experts are spread across many.

\section{All experts resident}

The capacity cost is absolute. Qwen3-30B-A3B at 4.5 bits per weight is about
17~GB; it does not fit on the 16~GB M1 beside anything else, which is why this
chapter's opening measures one layer's kernel instead of the model. DeepSeek-V3's
671 billion parameters are 671~GB even in FP8, more than eight 80~GB
accelerators hold. An
MoE model's promise --- the compute of a small model with the knowledge of a
large one --- is paid in memory, and the three ways to pay are more devices
(\Chref{expert-parallelism}), fewer bits (\Chref{low-precision-float}; gpt-oss and
Kimi~K3 store experts in MXFP4), or slower tiers of memory for the experts that
are not needed this step (\Chref{offloading}, \Chref{expert-streaming}).

\LedgerSection

Replacing a dense FFN with $E$ experts of which $k$ are active:

\begin{ConstraintLedger}
Compute & buys & FLOPs per token scale with $P_{\mathrm{act}}$, not $P$: $3.3/30.5$ for Qwen3-30B-A3B. \\
Memory bandwidth & buys & At batch 1, bytes per token scale with $P_{\mathrm{act}}$; the saving shrinks as the union grows and is mostly gone by $B \approx 2E/k$. \\
Memory capacity & spends & All $P$ parameters resident, or paged from a slower tier. \\
Latency & risks & Load imbalance turns into stragglers; a batch's slowest expert sets the step time. \\
\end{ConstraintLedger}

\HappenedSection{routing skew decides the hit rate}

Hermon's developers built an expert pager --- a cache of experts in memory,
misses read from an NVMe drive --- and measured it on Qwen3-30B-A3B in 4-bit
form, replaying routing traces against a 16.35~GiB container of its experts on
a Linux machine with an RTX~5060~Ti and a consumer NVMe drive (Hermon
\texttt{docs/KERNEL\_DESIGN.md}, section K6.1; the developers' measurement). With
uniform routing, an 8.75~GiB cache hit 44\% of expert reads; with routing drawn
from a power law over the experts --- a synthetic stand-in for the concentration
real routers show, generated by the benchmark itself
(\texttt{crates/hermon-kernels/csrc/bench\_experts.c}) --- the same cache hit
71.3\%. Because every miss was a read at the drive's sustained rate --- about
2.3~GB/s once \Chref{expert-streaming} recounts the bytes --- the ceiling on
generation speed followed the hit rate alone: 3.84 tokens per
second with uniform routing and 7.50 with skewed. The cache was never the
bottleneck; the bytes were, and the router decided how many there were. The
pager is a library that no request path in Hermon reaches yet at commit
\texttt{2a3fd52} (LIBRARY); \Chref{expert-streaming} follows the whole
investigation, including a hypothesis the measurements falsified.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-24}

Expert counts keep rising and the active fraction keeps falling: 8 of 128 in
Qwen3-30B-A3B, 10 of 512 plus a shared expert in Qwen3.5-397B, 16 of 896 in
Kimi~K3, which also stores its experts in MXFP4 \cite{moonshot2026kimik3}. Each
step down in active fraction raises the batch at which MoE decode becomes
compute-bound (Equation~\ref{eq:moe-ridge-batch}) and moves more of the
engineering into the network --- redundant and dynamically placed experts
\cite{deepseek2024v3}, communication kernels \cite{deepseek2025deepep} --- and
into memory hierarchies that keep only the hot experts close
(\Chref{offloading}, \Chref{expert-streaming}).
