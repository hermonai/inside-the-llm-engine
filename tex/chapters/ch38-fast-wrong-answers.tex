\chapter{Fast Wrong Answers: Oracles, Differential Testing and Determinism}\label{ch:fast-wrong-answers}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
Every optimization in this book is an opportunity to change the answer.
Some changes are bugs, some are rounding that should be tolerated, and some ---
results that depend on batch size or thread count --- are defects that no
single run reveals. This chapter builds the discipline that keeps speed
honest: independent oracles, differential tests across kernels, backends and
batch shapes, tolerances derived from numerics rather than chosen to pass,
deterministic reduction orders and batch-invariant kernels, and evaluation of
quantized models by their distributions rather than by anecdote.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{Different answers at temperature zero}

On the M1, llama.cpp decoded one prompt with Llama~3.2~3B greedily for 256
tokens, always choosing the most likely token. Run twice alone, the two answers
agreed to the last bit of every logit. Run beside one or two other requests
decoding at the same time, they still agreed exactly. Beside three or more,
the logits moved --- by 0.08 on a typical step and by up to 0.18 --- and at
token 220 the answer changed. The prompt's own cache was computed alone every
time, and the first generated token was identical in every run; only the
decode steps, which carried one token for each sequence in the batch, differed
(measured with a small C++ program against llama.cpp's library, build 8660; record
\texttt{2026-09-26-m1-part8}).

A mixture-of-experts model was more fragile. IBM's Granite 3.1 3B-A800M, which
routes each token to 8 of 40 experts per layer, changed its answer at token 32
when a single other request shared its steps, and beside three or more its
logits moved by up to 1.28 --- seven times the dense model's largest change ---
because a small change in the router's scores can send a token to a different
expert (Figure~\ref{fig:ch38-batch-regimes}).

Nothing in either run was a bug. Every kernel computed a correct matrix
product; they summed in different orders, and floating-point addition does not
care only about which numbers are added but also about the order
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch38-summation-order}). This chapter is about the engineering
that makes such differences visible, bounded and --- when it matters ---
impossible.

A kernel for one row accumulates in one running sum; a kernel for many rows

\Foundations{Choose the equivalence you intend to test}{
Bitwise equality, small numerical error, the same greedy token and the same sampling
distribution are different contracts. A seed fixes random choices only when the
surrounding computation and ordering also agree. Floating-point reductions can differ
with batch shape even when their real-arithmetic formulas are identical. An oracle
should be independently implemented enough to avoid sharing the optimized path's
likely bug. Chapter~\ref{app:numerics} explains rounding and reduction order. State
the expected equivalence before choosing tolerances; otherwise a permissive test can
certify the wrong property.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: how much rounding error to expect}

A 32-bit float carries 24 significant bits, so each addition may round by up to
$u = 2^{-24} \approx 6 \times 10^{-8}$ of its result. A dot product of length
$n$ summed in one running total accumulates at most about $n\,u \sum_i
\lvert a_i b_i \rvert$ of error, and in practice about $\sqrt{n}\,u \sum_i
\lvert a_i b_i\rvert$, because the roundings partly cancel. For one row of a
3,072-wide projection that is a few millionths of the row's scale
($\sqrt{3072}\,u \approx 3 \times 10^{-6}$) --- invisible. But a model applies 28 layers of such products
to a residual stream that each layer feeds forward, and normalization and
attention amplify small differences in some directions. The measured result is
the scale that matters: logits changed by up to 0.18 over 220 steps.

Whether that changes the answer depends on the margin between the best and
second-best token:
\begin{equation}
P(\text{greedy output changes within } T \text{ tokens}) \approx
1 - \prod_{t=1}^{T} \bigl(1 - P(m_t < \Delta)\bigr),
\label{eq:flip}
\end{equation}
with $m_t$ the top-1 minus top-2 logit at step $t$ and $\Delta$ the size of the
perturbation. In the Llama run, 4.3\% of steps had a margin below 0.1 and 9\% below
0.2; with $\Delta$ around 0.1, a 256-token answer almost surely contains a step
where a different batch can change the token (derived;
Figure~\ref{fig:ch38-margin}). Greedy decoding is deterministic only if the
logits are.

\EngineFigure{ch38-margin}{Llama~3.2~3B, the same request: the gap between the
largest and second-largest logit at every step of the batch-of-one run (log
scale), against the band of logit differences a batch of four produced
(measured). Most steps have gaps of several units and no batch can change
them; a few have gaps of hundredths. At step 220 the gap was 0.032 and the
answer changed.}{fig:ch38-margin}

\section{Kinds of wrong}

Wrong answers from an inference engine come in five kinds, and each needs a
different defence. A \emph{bug} gives a wrong answer every time: a mis-indexed
cache block, a quantization format read with the wrong stride, an expert cache
that returns another expert's weights (\Chref{expert-streaming}).
\emph{Nondeterminism} gives different answers to identical runs: atomic
additions whose order depends on thread timing, or a reduction split across
threads by availability. \emph{Batch variance} gives different answers
depending on what else is running --- the opening's case, and the most common
in serving, because every request's batch is an accident of traffic.
\emph{Tolerated rounding} changes bits but not the distribution the model
defines. And \emph{silent degradation} changes the distribution a little, for
every request: a lossy format, an approximate top-$k$, a cache stored at lower
precision. Only the first is always a defect; the rest are defects when a
system promises what they break.

\section{Oracles}

An oracle is a second way to compute the answer that is slow enough to be
obviously right. The best ones share as little code with the fast path as
possible. llama.cpp's \texttt{test-backend-ops} runs every operator on every
backend against the CPU implementation and fails if the normalized mean squared
error exceeds $10^{-7}$ by default (\texttt{tests/test-backend-ops.cpp} at
\texttt{d006858}). Hermon's quantized-matrix kernels were checked against a
dequantizer written independently in NumPy, which agreed to the last bit on
real weights (\Chref{expert-streaming}). The capstone engine of
\Chref{capstone} goes further: an oracle that recomputes every position from
scratch, with no cache, paging or batching, and that the engine must match bit
for bit. The price of an oracle is compute --- the capstone's is quadratic in
sequence length per token --- which is why oracles run in tests, not in
production.

\section{Differential testing}

A differential test runs one input through two paths that should agree and
compares the outputs. For an inference engine, the useful pairs are the ones
its optimizations create: two kernels for one operation; two backends; batch
sizes that select different kernels; one thread against many; one chunk against
several; cached against recomputed. The opening's probe is such a test, and it
cost one small program: decode a request alone, decode it again beside $B - 1$
others, and compare logits while the histories agree. The probe also contains
its own control --- the first token, computed before the batch forms, must match
exactly, and did --- without which a difference could come from anywhere.
Differential tests find defects that correctness tests cannot, because both
paths can be individually correct and still disagree. Hermon's thread-count bug
was found only by comparing a checksum across thread counts
(\Chref{expert-streaming}).

\section{Tolerances that mean something}

When two paths are allowed to differ, the tolerance must come from the
numerics, not from the last run that passed. An absolute tolerance ignores
scale; a relative tolerance per element fails wherever a result cancels to near
zero, which Hermon's developers met when a correct kernel appeared to fail with
a relative error of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ on one output that had cancelled to
$-2.06$ while its neighbours were $+232$ and $-261$. The
error lives in the accumulation, so the accumulation's scale --- $\sum_i
\lvert a_i b_i\rvert$ for a dot product --- is the honest denominator, and the
napkin bound above gives the size to expect. For whole tensors, normalized mean
squared error does the same job; for a model's output, the right measure is a
distance between distributions, below.

\section{Batch invariance}

A computation is batch-invariant when each request's result does not depend on
what else is in its batch. The opening shows why fast kernels are not: a
kernel tuned for one batch size tiles, splits and reduces differently from one
tuned for another, and llama.cpp switches among three matrix kernels by batch
rows on Metal. Thinking Machines showed the effect at production scale: 1,000
greedy completions of one prompt with Qwen3-235B-A22B produced 80 different
texts, diverging first at token 103, and they traced the cause to batch
variance rather than to concurrency as such. Their fix makes the reduction
strategies of RMSNorm, matrix multiplication and attention fixed --- no
split-K, attention split into chunks of a fixed size --- at a cost of 2.1 times
the time for their unoptimized version and 1.6 times with an improved attention
kernel \cite{he2025nondeterminism}.

Engines now offer it as a mode. vLLM's batch invariance, in beta at
\texttt{bcdacfc}, is switched on by \texttt{VLLM\_BATCH\_INVARIANT=1} on NVIDIA
GPUs of compute capability 8.0 or later; it also turns off TF32 and pins
collective communication to one channel and a tree algorithm
(\texttt{vllm/model\_executor/determinism/batch\_invariant.py}; PREVIEW).
SGLang's \texttt{--enable-deterministic-inference} reports an average slowdown of
34\% with CUDA graphs across its FlashInfer and FlashAttention~3 backends, and
seeds sampling so that temperature above zero is reproducible too
\cite{sglang2025deterministic}. The capstone engine is batch-invariant by
construction, because each output element is one fixed loop whatever else is
in the batch; the price is that it never tiles a reduction differently for
different shapes, which is exactly the freedom fast kernels use.

\section{Determinism by construction}

Batch invariance is one of several properties that make results reproducible,
and each is bought the same way: by fixing an order that would otherwise be
chosen at run time. Reductions split across threads combine their partial
results by index, not by completion (Hermon's fix). Split-K and split-KV kernels
choose their splits from the shape alone. Sampling draws from a generator seeded
per request, so a request's random choices do not depend on what ran before it
--- the capstone's rule, and SGLang's seeded Gumbel sampling. Atomics that add
floating-point values in arrival order are avoided or confined to integer counts.
None of this is free, and a system should buy only the determinism it promises:
bitwise reproducibility for reinforcement-learning rollouts and debugging,
distributional equivalence for everything else.

\section{Evaluating quantized models}

Quantization is silent degradation by design, so the question is how much. A
single perplexity number hides most of the answer. In llama.cpp's own
measurements of Llama~3 8B, the Q4\_K\_M format raised perplexity by only 2.8\%,
yet the most likely token differed from the 16-bit model's at 8.1\% of
positions, and at some positions a token's probability moved by 95 points
\cite{llamacpp2026perplexity}. The tool that produced those numbers compares
full output distributions: it saves the reference model's logits, then reports
the Kullback--Leibler divergence of another configuration from them and how
often both choose the same top token.

On the M1 the same tool puts engine settings and formats on one scale. The
reference is Llama~3.2~3B in its 4-bit Q4\_K\_M file with llama.cpp's defaults;
each other row changes one thing, and every row scores the same 2,040 tokens of
this book's text (measured; record \texttt{2026-09-26-m1-part8}):

The first row is the tool's own floor: it stores the reference logits in 16
bits, which alone moves perplexity by 0.05\% and leaves a largest divergence of
$5 \times 10^{-5}$. The next two rows are the opening's batch variance seen from
the other side: a different kernel shape changed the chosen token at one or two
positions in two thousand, while the distribution stayed indistinguishable. An
8-bit cache moves the distribution measurably and still changes only one token
in two hundred. A 4-bit cache changes one in fifteen, which perplexity reports
as 2.5\%; its largest single change of probability was 41 points. The 2-bit
weights --- requantized from the 4-bit file, so worse than a 2-bit file made
from the original --- change a third of the choices. Perplexity ranks the rows
correctly; only the distribution tells an engineer which of them a user would
notice.

\section{Testing the engine, not only the kernels}

Kernel tests do not test an engine. The capstone's tests run whole requests
through the scheduler --- queued behind others, prefilled in chunks, spread over
non-adjacent cache blocks, sampled with seeds, cancelled mid-flight --- and
require the same tokens as the oracle and exactly one terminal event per
request (\Chref{capstone}). Production adds what no test suite has: real
traffic. The incidents that matter most are the ones evaluations miss, and the
case below is one.

An oracle comparison is most informative before histories diverge. Hold inputs, positions and model identity fixed. On failure, shrink the case and find the first differing value instead of comparing two increasingly different generated stories.

Keeping an inference engine's answers honest:

\begin{ConstraintLedger}
Correctness & buys & Oracles and differential tests catch wrong answers that no single run shows; determinism makes every difference a signal. \\
Compute & spends & Oracles recompute what engines cache; differential tests run every input twice. \\

Cost & spends & Evaluation infrastructure that compares distributions, not anecdotes, on every change. \\

\HappenedSection{an approximate top-\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} that failed at some batch sizes}

In September 2025 Anthropic published a postmortem of three infrastructure bugs
that had intermittently degraded Claude's responses over several weeks. One
began as a fix. Their models computed next-token probabilities in bfloat16,
while the TPU compiler was free to promote some operations to float32, so two
operations meant to agree on the most probable token could run at different
precisions; a workaround deployed in December 2024 hid the resulting
problem. When the sampling code was rewritten in late August 2025 to fix the
precision issue properly, removing the workaround exposed a deeper one: the
approximate top-$k$ operation ``sometimes returned completely wrong results'',
and only for certain batch sizes and model configurations. The company switched to
an exact top-$k$ and standardized more of the computation in float32. Its
evaluations had not caught the degradation, in part because the model often
recovers from an isolated wrong token; the lessons it drew were evaluations
sensitive enough to separate working from broken implementations, run
continuously on production systems \cite{anthropic2025postmortem}. A kernel
that is wrong only at some batch sizes is invisible to a test that runs one.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-26}

Determinism has moved from a research demonstration to engine modes within a
year: vLLM and SGLang ship batch-invariant options, motivated largely by
reinforcement learning, where a mismatch between the sampler's and the
trainer's numerics corrupts training \cite{he2025nondeterminism}. The open
problems are cost and scope: invariance across tensor-parallel sizes and
hardware, not only batch sizes, and cheaper ways to detect when a difference
matters. On the evaluation side, distribution-level comparison ---
KL divergence and top-token agreement against a reference --- is becoming the
standard way to accept a quantization or a kernel change, replacing a single
perplexity number.
