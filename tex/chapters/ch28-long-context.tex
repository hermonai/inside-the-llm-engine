\chapter{Long Context: Windows, Sinks and Sparse Attention}\label{ch:long-context}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
Million-token contexts are served today, and not by a larger cache alone.
This chapter covers the techniques that make long context affordable and what
each risks: position scaling beyond the training length, sliding-window
attention, attention sinks, KV eviction by importance, trained sparse
attention that selects the relevant tokens per query, and compressed attention
that shrinks the cache along the sequence. It ends with how to measure whether
a long-context system still reads its context.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{A million tokens}

Put one million tokens in front of Llama~3 70B. Its cache, at 320~KiB per
token (\Chref{shrinking-kv}), is 320~GiB for that one sequence --- more than
four H100s' worth of memory before a single weight is loaded. Prefill costs about
$1.6\times10^{18}$ floating-point operations, nine-tenths of them in attention
rather than in the weights, which is 27 minutes of a single H100 at its full
BF16 peak (derived below). Decode then reads the whole cache at every step:
at a million tokens each generated token moves more than twice as many bytes of
history as of weights.

The same pressure shows at laptop scale. Served by Ollama on the M1 with a
16,384-token context, Llama~3.2~3B --- every layer attending to the whole
context --- slowed from 28.6 tokens per second after a short prompt to 16.5
after 11,700 tokens of document, and doubling its context from 8,192 to 16,384
tokens grew its memory by 915~MB, within 3\% of what 8,192 more tokens of its
112~KiB-per-token cache need. Gemma~4~E4B, which gives 35 of its 42 layers a
512-token window and lets its last 18 layers reuse earlier layers' caches,
slowed from 18.0 to 13.9 tokens per second over the same prompts, and the same
doubling grew it by \textbf{42~MB} (measured; record
\texttt{2026-09-24-m1-part5}). It lost about half as much of its speed and
under a twentieth of the memory. Its file's metadata implies 134~MB for those
8,192 tokens in 16-bit form; Ollama does not report how its engine stores the
cache, so the remaining difference is unexplained here.

DeepSeek-V4, released in 2026 with a one-million-token context, reports that
at that length its larger model needs 27\% of the per-token FLOPs and 10\% of
the KV cache of its predecessor V3.2 \cite{deepseek2026v4}. Nothing in this
chapter makes long context free. Each technique buys memory or arithmetic by
changing which tokens a query can see, and the price is paid in correctness,
which is why the chapter ends with how to measure it.

\Foundations{Addressable length versus usable evidence}{
A model's supported position range, an engine's allocation limit and a task's reliable
retrieval range are different quantities. Extending positions or allocating a larger
cache does not prove the model can use distant evidence. A sliding window changes
which historical positions are visible; a sparse selector chooses a subset by another
rule. Both differ from storing all positions in fewer bits. Chapter~\ref{app:rope} explains
position-dependent scores. Chapter~\ref{ch:kv-cache} explains their storage cost.
Evaluate a long-context optimization with tasks that require the information it might
discard, not only with an allocation test.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: attention's cost at a million tokens}

For a context of $S$ tokens, a model with $L$ layers and $H_q$ query heads of
width $d_h$ spends in causal attention during prefill about
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{attn}} \approx 2\,L\,S^{2}\,H_q\,d_h
\label{eq:attn-prefill-flops}
\end{equation}
FLOPs (scores and weighted values, each $S^2/2$ pairs of $H_q d_h$
multiply--adds), against $2PS$ for everything else. For Llama~3 70B
($L=80$, $H_q d_h = 8{,}192$) at $S = 2^{20}$ that is $1.44\times10^{18}$ for
attention and $1.5\times10^{17}$ for the rest (derived). Attention overtakes
the weights where $L\,S\,H_q\,d_h \approx P$: about 108,000 tokens for this
model.

Decode is the same story in bytes. A step reads the weights once and each
sequence's cache once: $W + B\cdot S\cdot k$ bytes for $B$ sequences of $S$
tokens and $k$ cache bytes per token. At a million tokens, Llama~3 70B's
343~GB of cache per sequence is 2.4 times its 141~GB of BF16 weights (derived).

Every technique in this chapter attacks one of the terms. A sliding window of
$w$ tokens caps a layer's cache at $w$ tokens and its attention at $S\cdot w$
pairs. Eviction caps the cache at a budget but keeps the full prefill. Sparse
selection keeps the full cache and reads $k_{\text{sel}}$ entries per query.
Compression divides the cache by the number of tokens folded into one entry.
Context parallelism divides the prefill across devices without changing the
total. Table~\ref{tab:ch28-long-context-costs} lists them.

\section{Position beyond the training length}

A model trained on sequences of 32K tokens has never seen rotary angles
beyond them, and nothing in its training constrains how its attention behaves
there (\Appref{rope}). Position scaling rescales the rotary frequencies so a longer
sequence maps into angles the model knows; Figure~\ref{fig:ch09-scaling} in
Chapter~\ref{app:rope} compares the schemes. YaRN, the most used, scales different
frequency bands differently and needs, in its authors' comparison, ten times
fewer tokens and two and a half times fewer training steps than earlier
methods to extend a context \cite{peng2024yarn}. It is how Qwen3-30B-A3B goes
from 32,768 native tokens to 131,072 and gpt-oss's dense layers reach 131,072
(model cards; \texttt{research/FRONTIER.md}).

For the engine, position scaling is nearly free: a change in how the rotary
tables are computed. Its cost is correctness at lengths the model was not
trained on, measured by the benchmarks at the end of this chapter, not by
perplexity on short text.

\section{Sliding windows}

A sliding-window layer attends only to the last $w$ tokens. Its cache stops
growing at $w$ tokens, its attention costs $S\cdot w$ pairs instead of
$S^{2}/2$, and it can see nothing older. No current model uses windows alone:
information from far back must still flow, so windowed layers are interleaved
with full ones. gpt-oss alternates banded layers with a 128-token window and
dense layers, ``following GPT-3'' (model card, section 2.2,
\cite{openai2025gptoss}). Gemma~4 uses windows of 512 or 1,024 tokens on most
layers; in the E4B file measured here, 35 of 42 layers are windowed at 512 and
the other 7, including the last, are global (GGUF metadata;
\texttt{research/FRONTIER.md}).

Engines implement the window either as a ring buffer of $w$ slots or as
ordinary pages freed once they fall out of the window. vLLM chose the second,
because a ring buffer cannot share prefixes: it allocates blocks per token
position and frees those outside the window, and its hybrid cache manager
intersects the prefix hits of windowed and full layers
\cite{vllm2026hybridkv}. llama.cpp keeps a window-sized cache by default and
offers \texttt{--swa-full} for a full-size one; because the windowed part
cannot be rolled back to an arbitrary earlier position, its server saves
checkpoints of it to reuse prompts (\Chref{hybrid-models}; \texttt{common/arg.cpp}
and \texttt{tools/server/server-context.cpp} at commit \texttt{d006858}).

\section{Attention sinks}

Xiao and colleagues observed that language models put large attention weight
on the first few tokens regardless of their content --- a place to put
probability mass when nothing else deserves it --- and that a sliding window
which evicts those tokens collapses \cite{xiao2024streamingllm}. Keeping the
first few tokens beside the window, StreamingLLM generated stably over up to
4 million tokens and ran up to 22.2 times faster than a window recomputed from
scratch at each step (their measurement). A sink is a correctness fix for
windows, not a way to remember: the model still cannot recall anything outside
the window.

Newer models build the sink in. gpt-oss gives each head a learned bias in the
softmax denominator, which ``enables the attention mechanism to pay no
attention to any tokens'' (model card, section 2.2, \cite{openai2025gptoss}),
and gated attention's output gate mitigates the sink
\cite{qiu2025gatedattention}. For an engine, a learned sink is one more term in
the softmax that every attention kernel must implement.

\section{Evicting cache entries}

Eviction keeps a fixed budget of cache entries per sequence and discards the
rest by an importance score. H2O keeps the ``heavy hitters'' that received the
most attention so far plus recent tokens; keeping 20\% of the cache as heavy
hitters, its authors report up to 29 times the throughput of DeepSpeed
Zero-Inference and Hugging Face Accelerate and up to 3 times that of FlexGen
\cite{zhang2023h2o}. SnapKV chooses, per head, the entries
that an observation window at the end of the prompt attends to, and reports 3.6
times faster generation and 8.2 times better memory efficiency at 16K tokens
\cite{li2024snapkv}. Both are training-free and both are bets: an entry
evicted because early queries ignored it cannot be recovered when a later
question needs it. Multi-turn conversations, where the next question is not in
the prompt that chose the evictions, are where the bet fails.

\section{Trained sparse attention}

If which tokens matter is the question, the model can be trained to answer it.
Native Sparse Attention combines compressed coarse-grained tokens with
fine-grained selected ones, is trained end to end, and reports substantial
speedups over full attention at 64K tokens in decoding and in both training
passes \cite{yuan2025nsa}. DeepSeek Sparse Attention, in DeepSeek-V3.2, adds a
``lightning indexer'' --- a few heads, run in FP8 --- that scores every earlier
token for each query; attention then reads only the top 2,048 entries, cutting
the core attention cost from $O(L^{2})$ to $O(Lk)$ in the report's notation
\cite{deepseek2025v32}. DSA runs in MLA's MQA mode (\Chref{shrinking-kv}), so
one selected latent serves all heads.

Sparse selection changes compute and bandwidth, not capacity: the full cache
is still resident, because a later query may select any entry. It moves the
engineering from the attention kernel to a gather --- read 2,048 scattered
entries from a paged cache per query --- and keeps a quadratic pass of its own:
the indexer still scores every earlier token for every query, made cheap by
few heads and FP8 rather than by selection.

\section{Compressing the cache along the sequence}

DeepSeek-V4 compresses the sequence itself \cite{deepseek2026v4}. Its
Compressed Sparse Attention folds the cache entries of every $m$ tokens into
one, with learned weights and positional biases, and then selects the top
entries per query, beside a small window of uncompressed recent tokens; its
Heavily Compressed Attention folds every $m' \gg m$ tokens into one and attends
to all of them densely. The two are interleaved across layers. The report defers the
exact values of $m$, $m'$ and the selection size to its open-source
implementation. At a million tokens, the combination brings V4-Pro to 27\% of
V3.2's per-token FLOPs and 10\% of its cache (report abstract).

Compression is the first technique here that shrinks the cache of every
layer without a window's blindness: every token still contributes to some
entry. What it gives up is detail inside a block, and as with every other
technique, the only way to know how much was lost is to measure retrieval.

\section{Context parallelism for prefill}

A million-token prefill does not fit in one device's time budget however the
attention is arranged. Context parallelism splits the sequence across devices.
Ring attention passes blocks of keys and values around a ring of devices,
overlapping each transfer with the attention computation on the previous
block, so the context scales with the number of devices without approximation
\cite{liu2023ringattention}. Meta's implementation for inference, with
variants that pass keys and values or pass queries, reported a one-million-token
prefill of Llama~3 405B in 77 seconds on 128 H100s across 16 nodes, at 93\%
parallelization efficiency and 63\% FLOPS utilization (their measurement,
\cite{yang2024contextparallel}). For decode, the cache itself is sharded along
the sequence: vLLM's decode context parallelism exists because the key/value
heads of GQA and MLA models run out before the tensor-parallel ranks do
(\Chref{shrinking-kv}; \Chref{model-parallelism}).

\section{Does the model still read its context?}

The cost of every technique above is paid in one currency: whether the answer
still depends on the right tokens. Needle-in-a-haystack tests --- find one
planted sentence --- probe only retrieval, which RULER's authors call a
superficial form of long-context understanding. RULER varies the number and
kind of needles and adds multi-hop tracing and aggregation at configurable
lengths, and found that of the models it tested that claimed
contexts of 32K tokens or more, only half remained satisfactory at 32K
\cite{hsieh2024ruler}. A long-context serving change --- a window, an eviction
policy, a compressed cache, a quantized one --- is a change to the model, and
deserves the same differential testing as any other (\Chref{fast-wrong-answers}).

Serving contexts far beyond a few thousand tokens:

\HappenedSection{compressed attention in a shipped model}

In 2026 DeepSeek shipped V4 with a context of one million tokens and an
attention design no earlier model family had used at that scale: layers that
compress the sequence before attending, some selecting sparsely over the
compressed entries and some attending densely to a much coarser summary
\cite{deepseek2026v4}. Engines had to follow. vLLM's attention-backend
documentation lists sparse DeepSeek-V4 variants beside its MLA backends
\cite{vllm2026attentionbackends}, because none of the existing kernels, cache
layouts or prefix-caching rules fitted a cache whose entries each summarize
many tokens. The lesson for engine builders repeats the one MLA taught
(\Chref{shrinking-kv}): a model's attention design is now part of the serving
stack's interface, and the stack that can absorb a new cache layout quickly
serves the new model first.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-24}

Long context is converging on mixtures of mechanisms per layer: windows and
global layers (Gemma~4, gpt-oss), recurrent layers beside a minority of
attention layers (\Chref{hybrid-models}), trained selection over compressed
caches (DeepSeek-V3.2 and V4). Native contexts of 256K to 1M tokens are now
common among open models (Qwen3.5: 262,144 native, extensible to about one
million; Kimi~K3: 1,048,576; \texttt{research/FRONTIER.md}). What lags is
evaluation: the benchmarks that show whether a million-token context is read,
rather than accepted, remain the limiting factor for trusting any of these
techniques in production.
