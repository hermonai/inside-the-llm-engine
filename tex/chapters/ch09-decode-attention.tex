\chapter{Decode Attention: FlashDecoding, Split-K and Paged Kernels}\label{ch:decode-attention}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
Ordinary decode has few new query rows but may have a long key/value history. A device
can have many workers and too little work per query to keep them useful. We split the
history into pieces, compute partial attention results and merge them correctly.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\section*{Give one query enough parallel work}
Do not average two partial attention outputs directly: their normalization masses can
differ. Carry the maximum and denominator alongside each partial result, as in the
previous chapter. First understand that merge, then ask whether extra workers save
enough time to pay for the merge and workspace. Paging changes where keys live, not
which logical keys the query is allowed to read.

\OpeningSection{Twice the bytes, twice the time}

On the M1 of \Chref{roofline}, llama.cpp decodes Llama~3.2~3B at 25.0 tokens
per second with an empty context. With 16,384 tokens already in the cache it
decodes at \textbf{12.4} (measured with flash attention enabled; record
\texttt{2026-09-23-m1-part2}). The extra work is attention over the cache, and
its bytes are easy to count: 16,384 tokens at 112~KiB is 1.88~GB of keys and
values --- nearly as much as the model's 2.01~GB of weights, and read at every
step beside them. The step reads 1.93 times as many bytes and takes 2.03 times
as long.

At every depth measured --- 0, 4,096, 8,192 and 16,384 tokens --- the step time
matches its bytes read at 48--52~GB/s: the rate at which the same engine reads
its weights, and about what a plain streaming kernel reaches on this GPU. A
long cache costs what its bytes cost, and no more. That is an achievement, not
a law. Each step asks the kernel to read 1.88~GB on behalf of a single query
vector per head, and the natural way to write that kernel leaves most of the
GPU idle. Another engine's kernel on the same M1 read its cache at about a
seventh of this rate (the section on occupancy, below). This chapter is about
the difference.

(Absolute rates on this laptop move with its load. A first run of these
measurements, taken while the machine was heavily loaded, recorded lower rates
that fell further at 16,384 tokens, and \Chref{roofline}'s 21 tokens per
second came from a busier session than this one. The record keeps every run
with its conditions; this chapter quotes the quietest.)

\Foundations{Parallelize the history, then reunite it}{
Decode attention has one new query per active sequence and head, but potentially many
historical keys. Splitting that history creates independent partial computations, not
independent final answers. Each split has its own normalization constant. Averaging
the splits' normalized outputs equally is wrong when their score masses differ. A
correct merge carries enough information to restore the full row's weights. This
distinction explains why a second reduction stage is necessary. Read
Chapter~\ref{ch:flashattention}'s online-softmax state before the split-kernel
derivation, and Chapter~\ref{app:gpu} if the terms block and occupancy are
unfamiliar.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: parallelism in one query}

For one sequence, one layer and one key/value head, decode attention reads
$S$ cached keys and values, $2Sd_hb_{kv}$ bytes, and performs about
$4Sd_h$ FLOPs for each of the $g=H_q/H_{kv}$ query heads that share them. Its
arithmetic intensity is therefore
\begin{equation}
I_{\text{attn}} \approx \frac{4\,g\,S\,d_h}{2\,S\,d_h\,b_{kv}} = \frac{2g}{b_{kv}}
\quad[\text{FLOP/byte}],
\label{eq:attn-intensity}
\end{equation}
one FLOP per byte for multi-head attention in 16-bit, four for a group of four,
independent of context length. The two models this chapter measures sit at
both ends of today's range: Llama~3.2~3B shares each of its 8 key/value heads
among 3 query heads, 3~FLOP/byte in a 16-bit cache, and Qwen2.5-Coder~3B shares
each of its 2 among 8, 8~FLOP/byte. Both are far left of the M1 GPU's ridge of
38 and of every data-center GPU's (\Chref{roofline}). Decode attention is a
bandwidth-bound workload, and batching does not change that: every sequence
reads its own cache. The first worked problem shows what that does to a
batched step, where attention can outweigh the weights.

The parallelism is the other number. A prefill kernel spreads work over query
tokens; at decode there is one. The natural decomposition --- one thread block
per (sequence, head) --- gives $B\,H_q$ blocks. For Llama~3.2~3B at batch one
that is 24 blocks, against a GPU that needs hundreds of concurrently resident
warps to hide memory latency. Each block then walks the entire cache alone:
at 8,192 tokens, 4~MiB of keys and values per head per layer, read by one
block while the rest of the chip waits. The Flash-Decoding authors put it
starkly: when the batch is smaller than the number of SMs --- 108 on an A100 ---
such a kernel uses only a small part of the GPU, and at batch one less than 1\%
of it \cite{dao2023flashdecoding}. The sequence dimension is the only place left
to find parallelism.

\section{The shape of decode attention}

For one head, decode attention is two matrix--vector products around a softmax:
scores $\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{K}\mathbf{q}/\sqrt{d_h}$ with
$\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times d_h}$, probabilities
$\mathbf{p}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{s})$, output
$\mathbf{o}=\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{p}$. No score matrix exists to be
avoided --- the scores are a single vector of length $S$ --- so the problem
\Chref{flashattention} solved is not this one. At 8,192 tokens the scores of
one head are 32~KiB of 32-bit floats, against 4~MiB of keys and values in
16-bit: the traffic is the cache, and nothing else is worth counting. The
problem here is reading $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ as fast as memory allows
with far too few independent tasks, and \Chref{prefill-decode} measured what
that costs on the M1: llama.cpp's unfused path, which runs decode attention as
ordinary matrix--vector products, read the cache at an effective 17--20~GB/s,
and its fused decode kernel at 52--57~GB/s. Same bytes, same GPU; the
difference is how many pieces the work was cut into.

\section{Splitting the sequence}

Flash-Decoding divides the cache into chunks and gives each chunk to its own
thread block \cite{dao2023flashdecoding}. It works in three steps: split the
keys and values into chunks; compute, in parallel, the query's attention over
each chunk, writing beside each partial output one extra number per row and
split, the log-sum-exp of its scores; then reduce over the splits, using the
log-sum-exps to weight each split's contribution. With 16 chunks, a batch-one
decode step has 16 times the parallelism. On an A100 with CodeLlama-34B, at
sequence lengths from 512 to 64K and batch one, its authors measured the
attention itself up to 50 times faster than FlashAttention and generation up to
8 times faster end to end for very long sequences; it shipped in the
FlashAttention package from version 2.2 and in xFormers from 0.0.22 (their
measurements, 2023). Figure~\ref{fig:ch09-split-merge} shows the scheme.

simdgroups instead, then merges the 32 workgroups' partials with SIMD

Engines choose the split differently, and the choice is visible in the source.
llama.cpp's Metal decode kernel always launches 32 workgroups per query and
head, each of one, two or four simdgroups: one while the attended cache is at
most 2,048 cells, two up to 4,096, four beyond. It does not cut the cache into
32 contiguous pieces. It cuts it into blocks of 32 cells and deals the blocks
round-robin, so that block $b$ goes to simdgroup $b \bmod (32 \cdot n_{sg})$,
and a block masked in full --- another sequence's cells, say --- is skipped
without reading its keys (\texttt{kernel\_flash\_attn\_ext\_vec} in
\texttt{ggml-metal.metal} and its launch in \texttt{ggml-metal-ops.cpp}). Hermon
cuts contiguous chunks of 256 tokens, between 1 and 64 of them, and runs one
simdgroup per (query token, query head, chunk) (Hermon
\texttt{docs/KERNEL\_DESIGN.md}, sections 6.2, 6.3 and K4, at commit
\texttt{2a3fd52}). The counts differ most where the cache is short. For the
decode shape of Llama-3-8B (32 query heads, 8 key/value heads) the two launch
(derived from the rules above):

\section{Merging partial softmaxes}

Every split returns what \Chref{flashattention}'s online softmax keeps for a
row: a maximum $m_i$, a denominator $\ell_i$ and an unnormalized output
$\mathbf{o}_i$. Equation~\eqref{eq:softmax-merge} combines them exactly, two at
a time or all at once. Flash-Decoding stores the same information in a
different form: the split's normalized output $\bar{\mathbf{o}}_i =
\mathbf{o}_i/\ell_i$ and one number, its log-sum-exp $\lambda_i = m_i + \ln
\ell_i$. Then
\begin{equation}
\lambda = \ln \sum_i e^{\lambda_i},\qquad
\mathbf{o} = \sum_i e^{\lambda_i - \lambda}\,\bar{\mathbf{o}}_i ,
\label{eq:lse-combine}
\end{equation}
a weighted average of the splits' answers, each weighted by its share of the
total softmax mass. (In practice $\lambda$ is computed as
$\max_i \lambda_i + \ln \sum_i e^{\lambda_i - \max_i \lambda_i}$, for the same
reason the softmax subtracts its maximum.)

\emph{A worked merge.} Take \Chref{flashattention}'s row --- scores 1, 3, 2, 5
against values 1, 2, 3, 4 --- and give its two halves to two splits. The first
sees scores 1 and 3: $m_1 = 3$, $\ell_1 = e^{-2} + 1 = 1.1353$, normalized output
$\bar{o}_1 = 1.8808$, and $\lambda_1 = 3 + \ln 1.1353 = 3.1269$. The second sees 2
and 5: $m_2 = 5$, $\ell_2 = e^{-3} + 1 = 1.0498$, $\bar{o}_2 = 3.9526$,
$\lambda_2 = 5.0486$. The merge gives $\lambda = 5.0486 + \ln(1 + e^{3.1269 -
5.0486}) = 5.1852$ and weights $e^{\lambda_1 - \lambda} = 0.1277$ and
$e^{\lambda_2 - \lambda} = 0.8723$, which sum to 1; the output is
$0.1277 \times 1.8808 + 0.8723 \times 3.9526 = 3.6881$, the full row's answer.
The second split's keys carry 87\% of the softmax mass because it holds the
largest score, and the merge needs nothing but two numbers per split to know
it. The lab's test checks the same arithmetic.

The two forms cost the same. llama.cpp keeps $(\ell_i, m_i)$ and the unnormalized
output --- 130 floats per workgroup at head width 128 --- and merges its 32
workgroups in a second kernel whose 32 SIMD lanes hold one partial each: a
SIMD maximum finds $m$, each lane scales its partial by $e^{m_i - m}$, and SIMD
sums produce $\ell$ and each element of $\mathbf{o}$
(\texttt{kernel\_flash\_attn\_ext\_vec\_reduce}). The partials are small beside
the cache they summarize: for Llama~3.2~3B at 8,192 tokens, one layer's 24 heads
$\times$ 32 workgroups $\times$ 130 floats come to 390~KiB, against 32~MiB of
keys and values read to produce them (derived). The merge is associative in
exact arithmetic, so the partials can be combined in any grouping --- but
floating-point rounding makes the grouping observable, and the grouping becomes
part of the engine's reproducibility contract (\Chref{fast-wrong-answers}).

\section{Grouped-query attention: what the heads share}

With grouped-query attention, the $g$ query heads of a group read the same keys
and values. A kernel can process the whole group in one pass over the cache,
reading each key once instead of $g$ times, and equation~\eqref{eq:attn-intensity}
says that multiplies the useful work per byte by $g$. Hermon's CPU kernel does
exactly that: it loops over key/value heads, loads each tile of keys once and
holds the group's query heads in registers, and its design notes expect the
key traffic to fall by the group size --- four for Llama-3-8B, eight for a model
with Qwen2.5-72B's grouping (Hermon \texttt{docs/KERNEL\_DESIGN.md}, section
6.4). llama.cpp's Metal decode kernel does not: it launches one threadgroup per
\emph{query} head, and each finds its group's cache by dividing its head index
by the group size (\texttt{ikv2 = iq2/(ne02/ne\_12\_2)} in
\texttt{kernel\_flash\_attn\_ext\_vec}). On paper, every key is read $g$ times.

So measure it. Qwen2.5-Coder~3B puts 8 query heads on each key/value head, and
its cache is 36~KiB per token. There are two predictions for the extra time a
decode step takes at depth: if the cache is read once at the
49~GB/s of \Chref{napkin-math}, 3.1, 6.2 and 12.3~ms at 4,096, 8,192 and
16,384 tokens; if each of a group's 8 threadgroups read it from memory, eight
times that --- 98.6~ms at 16,384. Measured with \texttt{llama-bench}, flash
attention on, two repetitions (record \texttt{2026-09-26-m1-pass3}):

\section{Reading through a block table}

A paged cache (\Chref{paged-kv}) stores each sequence's keys and values in
fixed-size blocks scattered through a pool, found through a per-sequence table.
The kernel's inner loop gains an indirection: for each block of tokens, look up
where it lives. vLLM's blocks hold 16 tokens unless the attention backend
prefers another size (\texttt{DEFAULT\_BLOCK\_SIZE} in
\texttt{vllm/config/cache.py}, and \texttt{\_preferred\_block\_size\_for\_backends}
in \texttt{vllm/platforms/interface.py}, at \texttt{bcdacfc}), and the table holds
32-bit block numbers (\texttt{vllm/v1/worker/block\_table.py}). For Llama-3-8B in
BF16, one such block of one key/value head is 4~KiB of keys and 4~KiB of values
behind a 4-byte table entry --- one byte of indirection per 2,048 of data
(derived) --- and the lookup costs one extra dependent load per block, which a
kernel hides by fetching the next entries while it reads the current block.
The table is small and the lookups are cheap compared with the bytes they
locate, provided blocks are large enough to be read in efficient bursts.

Not every engine pages. llama.cpp's cache is a pool of cells, each holding one
token of every layer, and a sequence's tokens may sit in any cells
(\Chref{kv-cache}). Its kernels read a contiguous window of the pool, the first
$n_\text{kv}$ cells, and rely on the mask to hide the cells that belong to other
sequences --- the decode kernel skips a 32-cell block whose mask is empty, so
the price of foreign cells is paid in mask reads, not key reads. Attention
libraries such as FlashInfer represent paged, ragged and sparse layouts in one
block-sparse format so that one kernel family serves them all
\cite{ye2025flashinfer}; DeepSeek's FlashMLA serves paged latent attention
\cite{deepseek2025flashmla}.

\section{Ragged batches: balancing the work}

A serving batch rarely holds sequences of one length. Suppose four sequences of
Llama-3-8B decode together with 32,768, 4,096, 2,048 and 512 tokens of context.
With one block per (sequence, key/value head), the step launches 32 blocks per
layer, and it lasts as long as its longest block: eight of them walk 32,768
tokens while the average block walks 9,856. The machine is busy for 30\% of the
block-time it pays for (derived: $9{,}856 / 32{,}768$), and adding sequences
does not help, because the step still waits for the longest one. Cut every
sequence into 256-token chunks and the same work becomes 1,232 blocks of equal
size --- 128, 16, 8 and 2 chunks per head --- which a GPU can spread evenly over
its SMs. Splitting, in other words, is not only for batch one. It turns a batch
of unequal sequences into a pool of equal pieces of work, which is what the
schedulers of attention libraries balance: FlashInfer describes its scheduling
as load-balanced and compatible with CUDA graphs \cite{ye2025flashinfer}, and
according to Thinking Machines it picks the largest split size that still
saturates the GPU \cite{he2025nondeterminism}. The other half of the balancing
problem --- which sequences to put in a step at all --- is the scheduler's
(\Chref{continuous-batching}).

\section{Occupancy, not bandwidth}

A GPU hides memory latency by switching among many resident warps; a kernel
that launches too few blocks cannot keep enough requests in flight to use the
memory's bandwidth, however few bytes it needs. That is the regime of decode
attention at small batch and moderate context, and it inverts the usual
optimization rule. Reducing bytes helps a kernel that is waiting on bandwidth.
It does nothing for a kernel that is waiting for work --- and if reducing bytes
also reduces the number of blocks, it makes the kernel slower.

The same GPU shows both kinds of kernel. llama.cpp's decode attention reads the
cache at the 48--52~GB/s of the opening measurement, the bandwidth roof in
practice. Hermon's Metal kernel, measured on the decode shape of Llama-3-8B,
took 19.2~ms to read one layer's 134~MB of 16-bit keys and values at 32,768
tokens: about 7~GB/s (derived from Hermon \texttt{docs/KERNEL\_DESIGN.md},
section K4). Both read the same bytes. Hermon's own measurements say where its
time goes. Every call costs about 0.94~ms at 512 tokens, whatever the data type
or the amount of work; a trivial kernel's round trip of commit and wait alone
is 0.19~ms; and at 512 tokens the grid is 64 threadgroups, which its developers
call far too few to fill the device (section K4.1). Against one CPU thread
running its NEON kernel on a 16-bit cache, the GPU lost below about 4,000 tokens
of context and won by less than a factor of two above it (section K4; PREVIEW,
opt-in native kernels). llama.cpp, at the same 512 tokens, launches 1,024
simdgroups. Occupancy is the difference the table in the section on splitting
predicts, and the case study below is its developers' account of learning it.

\section{Fewer bytes, more work: quantized caches at decode}

If decode attention were purely a matter of bytes, a smaller cache would be a
faster one. On the M1 it is not always. With the cache stored in llama.cpp's
8-bit format, \texttt{q8\_0} --- 34 bytes per 32 values, half the F16 cache's
bytes --- Llama~3.2~3B decoded at 9.48 tokens per second at 16,384 tokens of
context, against 12.35 with F16; the 4-bit \texttt{q4\_0}, a quarter of the
bytes, managed 11.44 (flash attention on; record \texttt{2026-09-23-m1-part2}).
Per step, over each format's own empty-cache time, the F16 cache added 41~ms
for 1.88~GB, \texttt{q8\_0} 63~ms for 1.00~GB and \texttt{q4\_0} 39~ms for
0.53~GB (derived): 46, 16 and 14~GB/s of effective cache reads. The quantized
kernels read fewer bytes and then spend longer on each one, turning blocks of
integers and scales back into floating point before the dot products. That is
the chapter's lesson from another side: once a kernel's time is set by
something other than bytes --- launch, occupancy, or the work done per byte ---
saving bytes buys nothing. \Chref{kv-quantization} takes up when the trade pays.

\section{Fixed split count or fixed split size}

How many chunks should a sequence be split into? A fixed \emph{count} gives
every sequence the same parallelism, but the chunk boundaries --- and so the
floating-point order of the merge --- move with the length. A fixed
\emph{size}, say 256 tokens per chunk, keeps the boundaries in place: the
split, and so the result, depends only on the tokens. Thinking Machines showed
that fixed-size splits are one of the changes needed to make attention's
result independent of which other requests share the batch
\cite{he2025nondeterminism}; Hermon uses a fixed size of 256 tokens, clamped to
between 1 and 64 splits, so that its plans depend only on the shape of the
request (Hermon \texttt{docs/KERNEL\_DESIGN.md}, section 6.3).

llama.cpp's Metal kernel is a fixed count with a twist, and the twist shows how
easily batch dependence creeps in. Its simdgroups per workgroup --- one, two or
four --- are chosen from $n_\text{kv}$, the number of cells the step attends, and
$n_\text{kv}$ is the highest used cell of the cache plus one, rounded up to a
multiple of 256 (\texttt{get\_n\_kv} in \texttt{src/llama-kv-cache.cpp}). In a
cache shared by several sequences, that is set by whichever sequence reaches
furthest. A short request decoded beside a long one can therefore have its keys
dealt to four simdgroups instead of one, and its partials merged in a different
grouping, than the same request decoded alone (derived from the source; the
bits were not measured). The results agree to rounding either way. Whether they
agree to the bit is exactly what an engine must decide to guarantee or not.

The lab measures the other half of the argument on the CPU. With chunks of a
fixed 256 tokens, merged in index order, every plan gives the same bits on 1, 2,
4 or 8 threads, whichever thread finishes first. Changing the split changes the
bits: at 32,768 tokens only 17 of the 2,048 outputs of the split plans match
the unsplit plan's to the bit, though none differs by more than
$10^{-7}$. And the split plans are the more accurate, by a factor of about nine
against float64 ($6.4 \times 10^{-7}$ against $5.5 \times 10^{-6}$): summing a
long row in chunks and merging the chunks accumulates less rounding than one
long running sum, the same reason pairwise summation beats a simple loop.
Splitting buys parallelism and, as a side effect, accuracy; what it costs is
agreement with any other split.

\emph{Parallelism:} on one thread the four plans take 12.2 to 15.5~ms ---
packing saves an eighth, splitting costs up to a tenth for its merges and
tasks --- because a core running this loop computes at about 22~GFLOP/s and is
busy with arithmetic, not memory. On more threads the \texttt{packed} plan, with
only 2 tasks, uses two cores and stays at 6.3~ms; the split plans spread 256 or
2,048 tasks over every core, and \texttt{packed+split} reaches 3.6~ms on four,
3.4 times one thread. \emph{Sharing:} the \texttt{heads} plan asks for each key
and value 8 times, 537~MB per step. Had those reads gone to memory, four threads
would have needed 98--113~GB/s, well beyond the 60~GB/s four cores draw
(\Chref{roofline}); the four threads, working on heads of the same group at the
same moment, found much of it in the shared cache, just as the GPU's
threadgroups did. Packing still pays once the work is split and the cores are
busy: on four threads \texttt{packed+split} takes 27--53\% less time than
\texttt{split}.
\emph{Determinism:} every plan returns the same bits on every thread count; the
split plans differ from the unsplit ones in the last bits and are nine times
closer to float64. The third worked problem places the times on
\Chref{roofline}'s roofline.

\emph{How big a chunk?} A sweep of the chunk size at 32,768 tokens on four
threads (\texttt{packed+split}; record \texttt{2026-09-26-m1-pass3}) is flat from
256 to 4,096 tokens, at 3.7~ms. Chunks of 64 take 4.3~ms and chunks of 16 take
6.1~ms, because 4,096 tasks of 16 tokens each spend as much time being queued,
started and merged as computing. A single chunk --- no split --- takes 6.4~ms,
because two tasks leave two cores idle. The error against float64 has a
minimum too, at 256 tokens ($6.4\times10^{-7}$), rising to $2.2\times10^{-6}$ at
both 16 and 4,096 and to $5.5\times10^{-6}$ unsplit. That is the pattern of
blocked summation: rounding grows with the length of each chunk's running sum
plus the number of partials merged, a total smallest when the chunk is near the
square root of the length --- 181 tokens here (derived). A chunk of a few hundred
tokens is not an accident of one engine; it is where both curves bottom out.

\emph{Predict} before you run: on your machine, how many threads can the
\texttt{packed} plan use at the default shape, and what does that cap its speed
at? \emph{Explain} why \texttt{split} and \texttt{packed+split} produce the same
bits as each other but not as \texttt{heads}. \emph{Break} it: set
\texttt{--chunk 16} on your machine and find where the task overhead starts to
swamp the gain; run
\texttt{--contexts 256} with four threads, where starting the threads costs
more than the attention (the lab's analogue of a GPU launch); and try
\texttt{--q-heads 8 --kv-heads 8}, multi-head attention, where packing has
nothing to share.

\HappenedSection{the GQA fusion that lost in every shape}

Hermon's attention kernel for Apple GPUs gave each query head its own thread
group. Under grouped-query attention that meant the heads of a group each read
the same keys and values --- four times over at 32 query heads and 8
key/value heads. On the CPU, packing a group to read the cache once was a real
win, so the GPU analogue looked certain to help. Hermon's developers built the
fused kernel, verified that it was bit-identical to the per-head kernel, and
measured it on an M1. It was \textbf{slower in every shape measured}:
0.19--1.00 times the per-head kernel's speed at decode across four head
configurations, and 0.68--0.71 times at prefill (Hermon
\texttt{docs/KERNEL\_DESIGN.md}, section K4.1).

Both reasons invalidated the premise. Fusing divided the number of thread
groups by the group size, and at decode the grid was already too small to fill
the device. And the ``redundant'' reads had never cost bandwidth: the thread
groups of a group ran concurrently and read the same cache lines, so the
hardware had already removed the traffic the fusion set out to save. The
llama.cpp measurement above is the same fact seen from outside: a per-head
kernel whose 8 threadgroups per group decode Qwen2.5-Coder at close to the
read-once cost. The developers' conclusion generalizes: on that device, decode
attention is occupancy-bound, not bandwidth-bound, and an optimization that
trades parallelism for bytes will lose. They recorded the failure so that it
would not be repeated --- which is the only reason we can learn from it.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-27}

Decode attention kernels are now chosen per hardware and per model family. At
the vLLM commit this book pins, causal attention on Hopper goes to the
FlashAttention backend first and on Blackwell to FlashInfer first, with separate
backend lists for latent attention and for DeepSeek's sparse attention
(\texttt{vllm/platforms/cuda.py} at \texttt{bcdacfc})
\cite{vllm2026attentionbackends}; FlashMLA's dense decode kernel reports up to
3,000~GB/s on an H800, a vendor number \cite{deepseek2025flashmla}. Sparse decode
attention, which reads only selected parts of the cache, changes this chapter's
arithmetic (\Chref{long-context}), and attention over quantized caches changes
the bytes (\Chref{kv-quantization}).
