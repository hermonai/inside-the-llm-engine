\chapter{Benchmarking Without Lying to Yourself}\label{ch:benchmarking}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
A benchmark is an experiment, and most inference benchmarks are poorly
controlled ones. This chapter defines the metrics that matter --- time to
first token, inter-token latency, throughput and goodput --- and how to
measure them: open- and closed-loop load, realistic arrival and length
distributions, warm-up and cache state, percentiles rather than means, and
comparisons that hold model, quantization and stopping rules fixed. It ends
with a record format that makes a number reproducible, and the common ways
results mislead.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{The same engine, three times apart}

This book's first measurement was Llama~3.2~3B decoding 128 tokens on the M1:
21.02 tokens per second, the mean of five repetitions with a standard deviation
of 0.30 (\Chref{why-inference}). Three days later the same binary decoded the
same file --- byte for byte, by its SHA-256 --- at 23.6 to 25.6 tokens per
second in twelve runs made over six minutes. The day in between, beside one
process copying memory on the CPU, it ran at 13.5 (\Chref{unified-memory}).
Each number was correctly measured, and they disagree by a factor of nearly two
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch39-three-days}).

The first run had flash attention off and the later ones had it on, which looks
like an explanation. It is not. Four interleaved pairs on the third day, one
with the flag off and one with it on, averaged 24.1 and 24.7 tokens per second:
the flag is worth about 2\%, and in one pair of the four it lost. With the flag
off on both days, the two days differ by 15\%. What changed was the machine
--- what else was running, how much memory was compressed or swapped, how warm
it was --- and nothing in the first record could have predicted it (records
\texttt{2026-09-23-m1-llama32-3b}, \texttt{2026-09-24-m1-part7} and
\texttt{2026-09-26-m1-part8}).

\EngineFigure{ch39-three-days}{One model file, one binary, one command: the
decode speed of Llama~3.2~3B on the M1, each mark one repetition, on three days
(measured). The flash-attention flag moves the result by about 2\%, less than

\Foundations{A timer needs a population and a boundary}{
Latency describes an observation interval for one request; throughput describes
completed work over an interval for a population. Neither says which requests were
rejected, timed out or silently omitted. Percentiles describe a declared sample
population and do not automatically compose across stages. A client that waits for
each response before sending another reduces offered load during stalls.
Chapter~\ref{app:reproducing} develops these distinctions with a worked trace. Before
running the benchmark, specify arrivals, warm-up, stopping rules, error handling and
whether the timer begins before or after client-side queueing.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: how many runs are enough}

Three questions decide whether a benchmark can answer the question asked of it.

\emph{How many runs resolve a difference?} The twelve runs of the third day had
a standard deviation of $\sigma = 0.59$ tokens per second, 2.4\% of their mean.
To tell apart two configurations whose true difference is $\delta$, with each
measured $n$ times, the difference of the means has a standard error of $\sigma
\sqrt{2/n}$; asking for a difference of two standard errors gives
\begin{equation}
n \ge 2 \left( \frac{2\sigma}{\delta} \right)^2 .
\label{eq:runs}
\end{equation}
For $\delta = 2\%$ that is 12 runs per configuration; for 1\%, 46; for 5\%, 2
(derived). The four pairs above could not have resolved the flag's 2\%, which is
why one of them came out backwards.

\emph{How many arrivals did the load generator actually send?} A Poisson
process at rate $\lambda$ for $T$ seconds sends $\lambda T$ requests on average,
with a standard deviation of $\sqrt{\lambda T}$. The load sweep of
\Chref{production} ran each rate for 120 seconds. At 0.2 requests per second that
is $24 \pm 4.9$ arrivals and at 0.3 it is $36 \pm 6.0$; the sweep drew 32 and
28, so the run that asked for less load offered more. Open-loop runs must be
reported by the rate they realized, not the rate they requested.

\emph{Can the percentile be estimated from the sample?} The $p$th percentile of
$n$ values, by nearest rank, is the $\lceil pn/100 \rceil$th smallest. The 99th
percentile of 50 requests is the 50th: the maximum, one request. A p99 worth
reporting needs at least a few hundred requests, and ten samples beyond it
need a thousand.

\section{What to measure}

A streamed request has a few instants that matter
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch39-request-timeline}): when it should arrive, when it is
sent, when the server admits it, when its first token arrives, and when its last
does. The metrics are differences between them. \emph{Time to first token}
(TTFT) is dominated by queueing and prefill. \emph{Inter-token latency} (ITL) is
each gap between consecutive tokens; its tail shows every stall a request met,
including other requests' prefill chunks and preemptions. \emph{Time per output
token} (TPOT) averages one request's gaps: vLLM's benchmark defines it as
$(\text{E2E} - \text{TTFT})/(n - 1)$ for a request of $n$ tokens, and reports
the 99th percentile of TTFT, TPOT and ITL by default
(\texttt{vllm/benchmarks/serve.py} at \texttt{bcdacfc}). \emph{End-to-end
latency} (E2E) is what a non-streaming client waits.

\EngineFigure{ch39-request-timeline}{One streamed request and the metrics
defined on it (illustrative). The clock a benchmark starts decides what it
measures: from the scheduled arrival, TTFT includes every queue the request met;
from the send, it omits the client's queue, which is where a closed-loop load
generator hides the server's slowness.}{fig:ch39-request-timeline}

Throughput has two units and needs a condition. Generated tokens per second
and completed requests per second are both meaningful; neither means anything
without the latency at which it was achieved, because any engine can raise
throughput by batching more and making each user wait. \emph{Goodput} joins the
two: the rate of requests that met every latency objective. DistServe defined it
as the rate a system can sustain while a target fraction of requests meet their
TTFT and TPOT objectives \cite{zhong2024distserve}, and vLLM's benchmark counts
it directly --- \texttt{--goodput ttft:500 tpot:50} counts the completed requests
whose TTFT, TPOT and end-to-end latency (as given) are all within their
limits. Server-side counters complement client measurements: llama.cpp's
\texttt{/metrics} exposes prompt and generation speeds, requests being processed,
requests deferred for lack of a slot and busy slots per decode step, all of which
\Chref{production} uses.

\section{Open and closed loops}

A load generator decides when each request is sent, and the two ways of
deciding measure different things. A \emph{closed loop} has a fixed number of
users, each sending its next request when its last one ends. An \emph{open loop}
draws arrival times in advance, as independent users would, and sends each
request when its time comes whether or not the server has kept up.

The difference is largest exactly when it matters. The same llama-server, with
four slots and the same prompts, was driven for 90 seconds by each, and stopped
with \texttt{SIGSTOP} for five seconds at $t = 40$ s in both runs
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch39-stall}). The closed loop of four users completed 52
requests; its first-token times had a median of 3.2~s and a 99th percentile of
4.9~s. The open loop, asked for 0.45 Poisson arrivals per second, drew 50 ---
0.56 per second, close to the closed loop's own 0.54 --- and its first-token times
had a median of 9.9~s and a 99th percentile of 16.5~s. The server and the average
load were nearly the same; the distribution of latency was three times worse
(measured; record \texttt{2026-09-26-m1-part8}). The sweep of \Chref{production}
explains why: under random arrivals this server completes at most about 0.52
requests per second, so the open loop's queue grew for the whole run, while the
closed loop's users, arriving in lockstep, filled the four slots exactly and
never queued.

\EngineFigure{ch39-stall}{The same server driven by a closed and an open loop,
with the server stopped for 5~s in each run (measured). The closed loop's four
users synchronized into waves: they start together, prefill their four prompts
together --- 3.2~s at about 250 prompt tokens per second --- and finish together.
None of them was waiting to send while the server was stopped, so the stop
appears in no first-token time at all; it only made the four requests in flight
finish 5~s late. The open loop's users arrived at random, queued behind one
another at the same average rate, and five of them arrived during the stop.}{fig:ch39-stall}

Two things hide in the closed loop's numbers. Its users never arrive while the
server is busy, because they are waiting for it; the queue that random arrivals
would build never forms. And a stall stops the load generator along with the
server, so the requests that would have arrived during it are never sent and
never measured. Gil Tene named the effect \emph{coordinated omission}. His
\texttt{wrk2} sends at a constant rate and measures from when each request
should have been sent; in his example, a web server paused for 1.4~s during a
30-second run showed a 99th percentile of 6.04~ms measured the traditional way
and 1.27~s measured correctly, a factor of 200 \cite{tene2014wrk2}.

Inference benchmarks inherit the problem through concurrency limits. vLLM's
benchmark sends requests on an open-loop schedule, but \texttt{--max-concurrency}
makes it wait for a free slot on the client; its TTFT and end-to-end times start
when the request is actually sent, and the waiting is recorded separately as
\texttt{client\_queue\_time}, reported only when selected with
\texttt{--percentile-metrics} (\texttt{bcdacfc}). A run with a concurrency cap is
a closed loop in disguise unless that column is read.

\section{Arrivals and workloads}

What arrives matters as much as when. Poisson arrivals --- exponential gaps ---
are the standard model of independent users, and real traffic is burstier.
vLLM's benchmark draws gaps from a gamma distribution whose shape is its
\texttt{--burstiness}: 1 gives Poisson, smaller values give bursts, and infinity
gives a constant rate. It then rescales the gaps so that the whole run lasts
exactly the number of requests divided by the rate, which removes the sampling
error of the napkin section at the price of not quite being Poisson
(\texttt{bcdacfc}).

Lengths decide cost. Prefill is paid per prompt token and decode per generated
token, so a benchmark must state both distributions. Fixed output lengths ---
\texttt{ignore\_eos}, used throughout this book's load tests --- make
configurations comparable; natural lengths measure what a service will see,
including the long tail that fills the cache. Datasets encode these choices:
vLLM's benchmark ships ShareGPT conversations, the BurstGPT trace, synthetic
random prompts and a \texttt{prefix\_repetition} generator for prefix caching,
among others. A benchmark that reuses one prompt measures a prefix cache, not an
engine.

\section{Warm-up, caches and steady state}

The first request to a new server pays costs no later request pays: pages of
the model file faulted in from storage, GPU pipelines compiled, allocators
growing. llama.cpp's server was warmed with one request before every load test
here; the first of Hermon's four runs in \Chref{real-engines} was discarded
because it paid for the page cache and Metal pipelines. vLLM's benchmark sends no
warm-up requests unless asked (\texttt{--num-warmups} defaults to 0 at
\texttt{bcdacfc}).

Caches outlive warm-up. In the three-engine comparison of \Chref{real-engines},
llama.cpp's server, told not to reuse its prompt cache, spent 526~ms on a
129-token prompt; Ollama, on the same file and the same prompt, reported 41--44~ms,
because it kept the prompt cached from its warm-up request. The twelvefold
difference is a cache hit, not an engine. A long run adds slower drift: caches
fill, fragmentation grows and the machine heats. Reporting the first minute of a
service as its steady state is the load-test version of the opening's mistake.

\section{Percentiles and variance}

Latency objectives are percentiles because users meet tails, not means. A
request that waits 16~s is not made acceptable by forty that wait 1~s. Every
percentile should be reported with its sample count, for the reason in the
napkin section; the stall experiment's ``p99'' of 50 requests is simply its
slowest request.

Two practices cancel what cannot be controlled. Interleaving --- A, B, A, B
rather than all A then all B --- spreads slow drift in the machine over both
arms; the flash-attention pairs of the opening were interleaved for that reason.
And the estimator should fit the question. Hermon's microbenchmarks report the
fastest of several trials with the spread beside it, arguing that on a loaded
machine noise only adds time, so the minimum is the least contaminated
observation (\texttt{docs/KERNEL\_DESIGN.md} at \texttt{2a3fd52}). That is right
for the speed of a kernel and wrong for a service: a user's latency cannot be
the best of five.

\section{Comparing engines fairly}

A comparison of two engines is a comparison of two complete configurations,
and fairness means holding fixed everything but the variable of interest: the
same weights in the same format, the same prompts and output lengths, the same
sampling, the same cache state, the same hardware in the same condition. In
\Chref{real-engines}, llama.cpp's server, Ollama and Hermon ran one GGUF file
greedily on one prompt and produced the same text; their decode speeds of
20--26 tokens per second differ by less than the opening's day-to-day spread on
one engine.

Public comparisons rarely hold so much fixed, and each side can be right about
its own numbers. In December 2023, AMD launched the MI300X claiming a 1.4 times
lead over NVIDIA's H100 in Llama~2~70B latency, both running vLLM in FP16. NVIDIA
answered that with its own TensorRT-LLM and FP8 the H100 was twice as fast, and
that under a fixed response-time budget a server processes several times more
requests than one at a time \cite{nvidia2023h100trtllm}. AMD replied that
NVIDIA had compared FP8 on its hardware with FP16 on AMD's, converted relative
latency into absolute throughput, and answered figures AMD had recorded in
November; with newer software its lead was 2.1 times on vLLM, and 1.3 times in
latency even against TensorRT-LLM in FP8 \cite{amd2023mi300x}. Four numbers, four configurations,
and not one of them a measurement of the chip alone.

\section{Industry benchmarks}

Two public efforts show what a controlled comparison takes. MLPerf Inference
fixes the model, the dataset, an accuracy target and a scenario --- offline, or
a server scenario with Poisson arrivals and latency bounds --- and lets
submitters vary everything else, with results reviewed by the other submitters
\cite{reddi2020mlperf}. Its LLM bounds are percentiles: in version 5.0, the
interactive Llama~2~70B test requires a 99th-percentile TTFT of 450~ms and TPOT of
40~ms, and Llama~3.1~405B requires 6~s and 175~ms, each while keeping 99\% of
a 16-bit reference's accuracy \cite{mlcommons2025llmv5}. Version 6.1, published
on 16 September 2026, drew results from 30 organizations, added a retrieval
pipeline and an agentic edge workload, and included a system of 512 accelerators
\cite{mlcommons2026v61}.

SemiAnalysis's InferenceMAX answers a different weakness: a benchmark run once
goes stale as software improves. It reruns its suite every night on hundreds of
accelerators from NVIDIA and AMD, with vLLM, SGLang and TensorRT-LLM, and plots
throughput per GPU against tokens per second per user, with cost per million
tokens derived from total cost of ownership \cite{semianalysis2025inferencemax}.
Both efforts report curves, not points --- throughput as a function of the
latency paid for it --- which is the shape of every honest answer in this chapter.

\section{A benchmark record}

Every number in this book comes from a record in \texttt{research/measurements/},
and the record's format is the chapter's advice in one place:

\begin{verbatim}
# <what was measured>, <hardware>              Date: <UTC range>
Questions      what the measurement must answer, stated before running it
Setup          hardware, OS, engine build and commit, model file and its SHA-256,
               every non-default flag
Conditions     load average, memory pressure, power, other work on the machine
Commands       exactly as run, with the scripts in the raw directory
Results        tables with sample counts and spreads; raw outputs alongside
Derived        each derived number with its arithmetic
Anomalies      failed runs, surprises and re-runs, kept rather than deleted
\end{verbatim}

The conditions line is the one most often missing and the one the opening
needed: with it, the first day's 21.02 and the third day's 24.1 are two honest
measurements of two different machines that happened to share a name.

\HappenedSection{measurements that compared a system with itself}

Hermon's development records keep three measurements that measured something
other than their subject. The first run of its benchmark for streaming experts
from an SSD reported up to 8,331~MiB/s, several times what the drive can
deliver; the machine's page cache was serving most reads, and the benchmark was
``measuring memory bandwidth wearing a storage costume'' until reads bypassed the
cache with \texttt{O\_DIRECT}. Its first report of a NEON kernel's speedup came
from one timed run; the same scalar baseline then timed 12.8, 12.9 and 21.6~ms on
three consecutive runs while a build competed for the cores, which put the
speedup anywhere from 6.0 to 11.9 times (\texttt{docs/KERNEL\_DESIGN.md} at
\texttt{2a3fd52}). And a table of that SSD benchmark counted every missed expert
at the size of the largest one, overstating the bytes read by 7\%, until a
replay of its trace recounted them (Hermon \texttt{5459c84}; \Chref{expert-streaming}).

This book's own capstone did the same. Its first roofline showed every batch
size at the same speed, because the ``memory ceiling'' was measured with the
engine's own dot product --- and that kernel's arithmetic, not memory, set the
rate. Both ceilings were the kernel's, and the plot compared the engine with
itself (\Chref{capstone}). The defence in every case was the same: a second
measurement by an independent route, and a number that could not be true when
the two disagreed.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-26}

Benchmarking is moving from single numbers to continuously refreshed curves:
InferenceMAX reruns nightly, MLPerf adds workloads within a year of their
appearance --- retrieval pipelines and agents in version 6.1 --- and tools now
record the queueing that closed loops used to hide, as vLLM's client-side queue
time does. Goodput under stated latency objectives is becoming the unit of
comparison. The open problems are workloads that no fixed dataset captures ---
multi-turn agents whose prompts grow with each call and whose value depends on
cache reuse --- and measurements across a fleet rather than one server, the
subject of \Chref{production}.

\ExercisesSection

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Derivation.} Using Equation~\ref{eq:runs} and $\sigma = 2.4\%$, how
  many interleaved runs per arm are needed to detect a 1\% improvement with two
  standard errors? How does the answer change if the runs are paired and the
  pairs' differences have a standard deviation of 4\%?
\item \emph{Prediction.} A closed loop of $C$ users drives a server with $S$
  slots. For $C \le S$ and $C > S$, predict what the loop's TTFT distribution
  shows and what it cannot show, then test it with the lab's generator.
\item \emph{Measurement.} Run the lab's open loop at five rates for 60 and for
  600 seconds each. Plot TTFT against the requested and the realized rates, and
  explain which plot is honest.
\item \emph{Falsification.} ``The fastest of five runs is the best estimate of
  an engine's speed.'' Find a measurement in this chapter for which that is the
  right estimator and one for which it is wrong, and say why.
\end{enumerate}
