\chapter{Matrix Multiplication on Real Hardware}\label{ch:matmul-hardware}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
The same multiply--adds can run forty times faster or slower in the same
library on the same GPU, and a thousand times across the ladder from scalar
loops to matrix units, depending on how they are ordered. This chapter follows
matrix multiplication from the scalar loops of Appendix~A onto real hardware,
climbing the ladder rung by rung in a lab on the reader's own CPU: loop order,
cache blocks, register tiles, threads. It then moves to the GPU --- tiles that
live in shared memory, tensor cores that multiply small matrices per
instruction, Hopper's asynchronous warpgroup MMA and Blackwell's tensor memory
--- and shows why the shape of a product, especially the skinny products of
decode, decides which roof binds and how much of the machine a kernel can use,
and when split-K or a specialized matrix--vector kernel beats a general GEMM.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{Same FLOPs, forty times apart}

Multiply two $4{,}096\times4{,}096$ matrices of 16-bit floats on the M1's GPU
and the product --- 137 billion floating-point operations --- takes 62~ms
through PyTorch's MPS (Metal) backend: 2.2~TFLOP/s, about 85\% of the 2.6~TFLOP/s
Apple quotes for the chip (record \texttt{2026-09-23-m1-part2}). Compute the
same product as 4,096 matrix--vector products, one column of the right-hand
matrix at a time, and it takes about 2.5~s (512 of them timed, then scaled):
the same multiply--adds, in the same library, on the same GPU,
\textbf{41 times} slower. Each matrix--vector product reads the whole 32~MiB
left-hand matrix to perform 34 million FLOPs; the single product reads it once
and uses each element 4,096 times.

The ladder below the GPU is longer. The chapter's lab climbs it on the M1's CPU
with $512 \times 512$ matrices in 32-bit floats: the textbook $i,j,k$ loops run at
2.2~GFLOP/s, the same loops reordered at 26, a register-tiled kernel at 46 and
the tiled kernel on four cores at 160 (measured, record
\texttt{2026-09-26-m1-pass3}). Apple's Accelerate library runs the $4{,}096$
product on the same CPU at 873~GFLOP/s --- more than twice the 377~GFLOP/s that
\Chref{roofline}'s lab measured for the performance cores' vector units, so
Apple's library evidently uses hardware beyond them, which Apple does not
document. The GPU runs it at 1,604~GFLOP/s in 32-bit floats and 2,213 in 16-bit.
A thousand-fold separates the bottom rung from the top, and none of it comes from
doing less arithmetic. It comes from where the operands are when they are
needed, how many times each byte is used once it is there, and whether the
arithmetic runs on units built for matrices.

One more measurement shows why an engine cannot choose one kernel and stop.
With the same $4{,}096\times4{,}096$ matrix and $N$ right-hand columns, the GPU
library takes 1.9~ms at $N = 9$ and 1.2~ms at $N = 10$ in 16-bit floats --- more
work in less time --- and 3.2~ms at $N = 96$ against 2.4 at $N = 128$ (measured,
best of two sweeps). Arithmetic cannot explain a product that gets faster when it
is given more to do; a change of kernel inside the library can, and Apple does
not publish which kernel it runs for which shape. Decode runs at exactly these
shapes.

\Foundations{An output tile owns a reduction}{
Matrix multiplication forms each output by summing products over the shared dimension.
Splitting output rows or columns gives independent results; splitting the reduction
dimension creates partial sums that must be combined. A tile is a small rectangular
region assigned to a cooperating group of workers. Keeping its operands nearby can
reduce repeated transfers, but consumes local storage and limits how many groups can
run together. More workers therefore need not mean more throughput. Appendix A.5
derives the scalar loop; Appendices~\ref{app:gpu} and~\ref{app:cpu} connect those
loops to GPU and CPU storage.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: the work and the bytes of a GEMM}

A product $\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ with
$\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times K}$ and $\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times N}$
costs $2MNK$ FLOPs and must move at least $(MK+KN+MN)\,b$ bytes. In a decode
step, $\mathbf{A}$ is a weight matrix and $N$ is the batch: the weights
dominate the bytes and the intensity is about $2N/b$ (\Chref{roofline}). That is
the compulsory traffic. What a kernel actually moves depends on how it reuses
data on chip. A kernel that computes an output tile of $T_M\times T_N$ loads
$T_M$ rows of $\mathbf{A}$ and $T_N$ columns of $\mathbf{B}$ for each step
along $K$, and performs $2T_MT_N$ FLOPs per $K$-element for
$(T_M+T_N)\,b$ bytes loaded:
\begin{equation}
I_{\text{tile}} = \frac{2\,T_M\,T_N}{(T_M+T_N)\,b}\quad[\text{FLOP/byte}].
\label{eq:tile-intensity}
\end{equation}
A $128\times128$ tile in BF16 reaches 64~FLOP/byte from slow memory --- short
of an H100's BF16 ridge of about 300 (\Chref{roofline}), which is why fast
kernels also reuse tiles through the L2 cache and share them between
neighbouring blocks. The tile size is bounded by on-chip capacity: registers
for the accumulators, shared memory for the operands, 228~KB per SM on an H100
\cite{nvidia2026hoppertuning}. And at decode, $N$ is the batch: a
$128\times128$ tile with $N=8$ does $8/128$ of the useful work its shape
promises.

Work the numbers once. A $128 \times 128$ output tile stepping along $K$ in
slices of 64 needs, per slice, $128 \times 64$ elements of $\mathbf{A}$ and
$64 \times 128$ of $\mathbf{B}$: 16~KB each in BF16, 32~KB per stage. Four stages
in flight --- so that the next slices are loading while the current one is
multiplied --- take 128~KB of the 228~KB, and the tile's $128 \times 128$
accumulators take 64~KB in 32-bit floats wherever they are kept. Doubling the
tile to $256 \times 128$ raises its intensity by a third, to 85~FLOP/byte, and
doubles the accumulators. Every choice of tile is a trade between reuse and
the scarcest capacity on the chip.

each rung computing the same multiply--adds in a different order, each checked

shows the result beside the rungs above it.

\emph{Naive loops, 2.2~GFLOP/s.} The textbook order $i, j, k$ keeps a running sum
for one element of $\mathbf{C}$ and walks a row of $\mathbf{A}$ against a
\emph{column} of $\mathbf{B}$. Consecutive elements of that column are 512 floats
apart, so every multiply--add touches a different cache line, and each addition
waits for the previous one to finish.

\emph{Reordered loops, 26~GFLOP/s, twelve times faster.} Swapping the inner two
loops, to $i, k, j$, makes the innermost loop add a multiple of one row of
$\mathbf{B}$ to one row of $\mathbf{C}$. Both rows are contiguous, the additions
are independent, and the compiler turns the loop into vector instructions. Not
one operation changed.

\emph{Cache blocks of 64, 20~GFLOP/s --- slower.} Blocking the three loops so
that a $64 \times 64$ block of each matrix stays in cache is the textbook next
step, and at this size it loses: the reordered loop already streams its rows
through the 128~KiB first-level cache efficiently, and the block loops add
bookkeeping. The first edition measured the same surprise on the same machine
(the ``What actually happened'' section below). Blocking pays when the matrices
outgrow the caches; an optimization is a hypothesis until it is measured.

\emph{A register tile of $4 \times 16$, 46~GFLOP/s.} The decisive rung keeps a
$4 \times 16$ block of $\mathbf{C}$ in 64 accumulators --- sixteen 4-wide vector
registers --- for the whole length of $K$:

\CodeLines{../code/labs/ch07-gemm/src/main.rs}{93}{115}

\noindent Each step along $k$ loads 16 values of $\mathbf{B}$ and 4 of
$\mathbf{A}$, twenty loads, and performs 64 multiply--adds: every loaded value
of $\mathbf{B}$ is used four times and every value of $\mathbf{A}$ sixteen times,
equation~\eqref{eq:tile-intensity} at the scale of a register file. The compiled
loop is sixteen vector multiplies and sixteen vector adds per step (checked in
the generated assembly); the accumulators never leave the registers until the
tile is done.

\emph{Four cores, 160~GFLOP/s, 72--74 times the naive loop.} Splitting the rows of
$\mathbf{A}$ among four threads gives each core its own tiles, and the product
now runs at 42\% of the four cores' multiply--add peak --- about the rate
\Chref{roofline}'s lab found for the capstone engine's kernel on data already in
cache. The remaining gap to the peak is loads, loop overhead and the separate
multiply and add that Rust emits without fused multiply--adds; closing it is the
job of hand-written micro-kernels and of the matrix hardware the next rungs use.

\section{The memory hierarchy a tile lives in}

A GPU matrix kernel spans five levels of storage, each much smaller and faster
than the one below: HBM or GDDR (tens to hundreds of gigabytes, a few
terabytes per second), a shared L2 cache (50~MB on an H100), per-SM shared
memory (228~KB on an H100), registers, and --- on Blackwell --- tensor memory
dedicated to matrix accumulators \cite{nvidia2026hoppertuning,nvidia2026cutlassblackwell}.
A well-written kernel stages each operand tile down this hierarchy once and
reuses it as many times as the output tile allows. The CPU lab has the same
shape at smaller scale: the 12~MiB shared second-level cache, the 128~KiB
first-level data cache of each performance core, and the vector registers that
hold the $4 \times 16$ tile. Appendix~\ref{app:gpu} gives the numbers for each
GPU generation.

\section{Tensor cores: matrix units}

A tensor core executes a small matrix multiply--accumulate, $\mathbf{D}=
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{C}$ on tiles of a few dozen to a few hundred
elements, as a single instruction. It is why a GPU's matrix throughput is an
order of magnitude above its ordinary floating-point throughput, and why
low-precision formats matter: each generation's tensor cores double their rate
at each halving of operand width (\Chref{low-precision-float}). A kernel that
does not feed the tensor cores --- because its operands are the wrong shape,
type or layout --- runs at a fraction of the GPU's advertised peak.

The instruction's shape is also a tile, and the same arithmetic applies to it.
A tensor-core instruction that multiplies a $64 \times 16$ block of $\mathbf{A}$ by
a $16 \times N$ block of $\mathbf{B}$ does $2 \times 64 \times 16 \times N$ FLOPs,
however many of the $N$ columns are real. When a decode step brings eight
sequences, an instruction built for 64 rows of weights and a few dozen columns
spends most of its lanes on padding --- which is why decode kernels are often
written without tensor cores at all, as streaming matrix--vector kernels, and
why the precision of the \emph{weights} (\Chref{weight-quantization}) matters more
to decode than the tensor cores' peak.

\section{Hopper: asynchronous warpgroup MMA and TMA}

Hopper changed how kernels feed the tensor cores. Its warpgroup MMA
instruction, \texttt{wgmma}, is issued by a warpgroup of 128 threads, reads its
$\mathbf{B}$ operand (and optionally $\mathbf{A}$) directly from shared memory,
keeps the accumulator in registers, and runs asynchronously: a kernel issues
it, does other work, and waits for it with explicit fences. Each instruction
computes a $64\times N\times K$ product with $N$ from 8 to 256
\cite{nvidia2026cutlasswgmma}. Its partner is the Tensor Memory Accelerator,
which copies multi-dimensional tiles between global and shared memory on the
request of a single thread, without spending registers or instruction issue on
address arithmetic \cite{nvidia2026hoppertuning}. Together they let a kernel
dedicate some warps to loading and others to computing, the
\emph{warp specialization} that FlashAttention-3 uses (\Chref{flashattention}).
Figure~\ref{fig:ch07-tile-journey} follows one output tile through such a
kernel.

\EngineFigure{ch07-tile-journey}{One output tile's journey through a
warp-specialized GEMM. On Hopper, a producer warp has the Tensor Memory
Accelerator copy operand tiles from HBM into a ring of shared-memory stages
while consumer warpgroups multiply them with \texttt{wgmma}, accumulating in
registers. On Blackwell, \texttt{tcgen05} instructions on one SM or a pair of
SMs accumulate in tensor memory instead. Both finish with an epilogue that

\section{Blackwell: tensor memory and two-SM MMA}

Blackwell's \texttt{tcgen05} instructions are 2--4 times faster than Hopper's
\texttt{wgmma}, depending on data type; they write accumulators into tensor
memory, a new on-chip store, instead of registers; a pair of SMs can execute
one larger MMA together; and block-scaled variants consume FP8, FP6 and FP4
operands with their scale factors directly \cite{nvidia2026cutlassblackwell}.
Freeing registers from accumulators changes kernel design: tiles can be larger,
and the registers go to the non-matrix work --- softmax, dequantization ---
that \Chref{flashattention} found becoming the bottleneck.

\section{Occupancy, waves and tails}

A GEMM's output is divided into tiles, and the tiles are handed to SMs in
waves. If the number of tiles is not a multiple of the number of SMs, the
last wave runs partly empty. This \Term{wave quantization} is invisible in FLOP
counts and can cost a large fraction of a kernel's speed on unlucky shapes. It
is also why the same model can run at different efficiencies on GPUs with
different SM counts.

Take an H100 with 132 SMs, one tile per SM at a time, and a product whose output
divides into 150 tiles of $128 \times 128$ --- for instance $M = 1{,}920$ rows by
$N = 1{,}280$ columns. The first wave runs 132 tiles, the second 18: the product
takes two tile-times, as long as 264 tiles would, and the GPU does useful work
in $150/264 = 57\%$ of its tile slots (derived). A product with 132 tiles takes
one tile-time; the 150-tile product, with 14\% more work, takes twice as long.
The arithmetic depends on the machine as much as on the product: on a GPU with 150
or more SMs the same product finishes in one wave, and on one with 76 SMs it fills
two waves almost exactly. Moving a model to a different GPU can therefore change a
kernel's efficiency when nothing in the model changed, which is one reason
libraries tune their tile shapes per GPU. The worked problems show two ways to get
the lost time back.

\section{Skinny GEMMs at decode}

At decode, a linear layer multiplies a large weight matrix by $N$ columns,
where $N$ is the number of sequences in the batch: one, eight, sixty-four. A
kernel tuned for square products wastes most of its tile on such a shape,
exactly the tile-quantization effect the M1's GPU showed at 9--32 sequences
(\Chref{roofline}). The lab measures the effect in isolation. It multiplies a
$4{,}096 \times 4{,}096$ weight matrix of 32-bit floats --- 64~MiB, far larger than
any cache --- by $N$ vectors with two kernels: the register-tiled GEMM above,
whose tile is 16 columns wide, and the capstone engine's decode kernel, which
streams each weight row once and dots it with every vector.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch07-skinny} shows the two.

The streaming kernel takes 1.2~ms for one, two or four vectors: the step reads
67~MB at about 57~GB/s and the arithmetic hides inside the read. At eight vectors
it takes 2.2~ms, and at sixteen 6--7~ms, because each weight row now has to be
dotted with sixteen vectors that no longer fit beside it in the first-level
cache. The tiled kernel takes 3.3~ms from one vector to sixteen: it does the
arithmetic of sixteen columns whatever $N$ is, at about 164~GFLOP/s of padded
work, so at $N = 1$ fifteen sixteenths of its multiply--adds are thrown away.
From 16 up, every column is real and it runs at 164--171~GFLOP/s, one and a
half to two times the streaming kernel's rate. Neither kernel is right for both regimes, and an engine
that must serve batches from one to hundreds carries both and switches between
them --- exactly what llama.cpp's Metal backend does at eight rows
(\Chref{roofline}).

The GPU library shows the same effect with less regularity.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch07-mps-cliff} sweeps $N$ from 1 to 32 one column at a time,
then on to 512.

In 16-bit floats one to three columns take 0.84--0.91~ms --- the matrix read at
about 38~GB/s --- and the time climbs to 1.9~ms at nine columns, then drops to
1.0--1.2~ms from ten to sixteen, climbs again past sixteen, falls at 64, jumps
at 96 and falls at 128. The 32-bit curve has its own steps in similar places.
Nothing in the roofline predicts a product that gets faster with more work; a
library that selects kernels by shape does, and both sweeps reproduced the
pattern. For an engine the lesson is that the batch sizes it can choose are not
equally cheap: a scheduler that knows its kernels' steps can add a sequence at
nine for free, or avoid adding one at 96.

\section{A decode product on an H100, by the numbers}

The same arithmetic at data-center scale shows how short a decode kernel is.
Multiply a $4{,}096 \times 4{,}096$ BF16 weight matrix --- 32~MiB, one of the four
attention matrices of an 8-billion-parameter model's layer --- by one vector on an
H100. At 3.35~TB/s the read takes 10~$\mu$s; the 34~MFLOP take 0.03~$\mu$s at the
tensor cores' peak, and the product is bandwidth-bound by a factor of three
hundred (derived). Launching a GPU kernel costs about 2~$\mu$s on an H100, or about
1.3~$\mu$s with CUDA graphs, by one published measurement
\cite{spector2025megakernel}: a fifth of this kernel's whole run.

A decode step of such a model runs a few hundred kernels --- seven matrix products
per layer, attention, norms, activations, over 36 layers --- and most are shorter
than this one. The fixed cost of starting them, not the bytes they move, is then
a large share of the step: at a hundred launches of 2~$\mu$s each, 0.2~ms of a step
that must also stream 15~GB of weights in 4.5~ms, and far more for a small model
whose whole step lasts a millisecond. That is why decode is where engines capture
CUDA graphs (\Chref{prefill-decode}), fuse operators into fewer kernels
(\Chref{kernel-languages}), and --- at the extreme --- run the whole forward pass as
one persistent ``megakernel'' that never returns to the host between layers. The
measurement behind the 2~$\mu$s also reported that such a megakernel moved weights
at 78\% of the H100's bandwidth for a 1-billion-parameter model at batch one,
against at most half for general-purpose engines \cite{spector2025megakernel}. For
skinny products, how a kernel starts and ends matters as much as how it tiles.

\section{Quantized weights inside the kernel}

The weights a decode kernel multiplies on a laptop are rarely floats. In the
\texttt{Q4\_K\_M} file of Llama~3.2~3B most matrices are stored in blocks of 256
weights: 4-bit values plus scales and minimums for each group, 144 bytes per
block, 4.5 bits per weight (\Chref{forward-pass}). A decode kernel reads the
blocks, expands each weight to a floating-point value in registers, and
multiplies --- the fused dequantization that the worked problems of
\Chref{roofline} showed must happen inside the product, not before it, if the
bytes saved are to be saved at all.

Expansion is arithmetic, and at low enough precision it stops being free. The
book measured the same model in six formats on the M1's GPU with llama.cpp
(record \texttt{2026-09-23-m1-part2}): decode moved its weights at an effective
51~GB/s in \texttt{Q8\_0}, 48 in \texttt{Q6\_K}, 47 in \texttt{Q5\_K\_M}, 46 in
\texttt{Q4\_K\_M}, and only 39 in \texttt{Q3\_K\_M} and \texttt{Q2\_K}. Going from
\texttt{Q8\_0} to \texttt{Q4\_K\_M} cut the bytes 1.70 times and raised decode speed
1.52 times; going from \texttt{Q4\_K\_M} to \texttt{Q3\_K\_M} cut the bytes by a
further 17\% and raised the speed by 3\%. Below about four bits per weight the
kernel spends so much of its time unpacking --- shifting and masking bits, applying
two levels of scales --- that it no longer keeps the memory busy, and the
roofline's promise that fewer bytes mean proportionally more speed stops holding.
\Chref{weight-quantization} takes the formats apart; the point for this chapter is
that a quantized decode kernel has two roofs of its own, memory for the packed
bytes and arithmetic for the unpacking, and the format decides which one binds.

\section{The order of the sums}

The ladder's five rungs perform the same multiply--adds, and the lab checks more
than that: it compares every output of every rung with the naive loop's, bit for
bit. All five agree on all 262,144 elements (measured). Reordering the loops,
blocking them and tiling them into registers changed which values sat near the
arithmetic and when, but every output was still the sum of its $K$ products added
in the same order, $k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$, one at a time. Tiling rearranges
\emph{loads}; it does not rearrange \emph{additions}, and floating-point results
depend only on the additions.

Part two of the lab compares its two decode-shape kernels the same way, and there
the answer is different: they agree on only about 2.5\% of the outputs, and differ
elsewhere by up to $3 \times 10^{-6}$ of the largest output (measured). Both are
correct to within the oracle's tolerance. They differ because the capstone's
streaming kernel computes each dot product with sixteen running sums --- four
vector registers of four lanes, combined at the end in a fixed order --- so that
the additions can overlap, while the tiled kernel adds each output's products one
at a time. Every technique that makes a reduction faster changes its order:
several running sums, vector lanes, split-K and stream-K, a tree of partial sums
across threads, a fused multiply--add that skips a rounding. Production GPU
libraries choose among such orders per shape, which is why the same model can
give slightly different logits at batch 1 and batch 16 on the same GPU
(\Chref{fast-wrong-answers}). The capstone engine avoids it by fixing one order for
every shape, and pays for that in speed; this lab shows both sides of the trade.

\section{How an engine picks a kernel}

Every measurement in this chapter says the same thing: no single kernel is best
for every shape a model presents. A decode step asks for products of a weight
matrix and one to a few hundred vectors; a prefill step asks for the same weights
against hundreds or thousands; the attention in each asks for something else
again. An engine therefore carries several kernels per operation and a
dispatcher that chooses among them by shape at run time.

llama.cpp's Metal dispatcher is small enough to read in full: for K-quant weights
it runs a matrix--vector kernel for one to three rows, a small-batch kernel for four
to eight, and a tiled matrix--matrix kernel above eight, with 32-column tiles
(\Chref{roofline}). The thresholds are constants in its source; the lab's
crossover between 8 and 16 on a different kernel pair lands in the same place for
the same reason, and the GPU library's steps at 9 and 17 columns suggest Apple's
own thresholds sit nearby. Larger engines generate more of the choice: CUTLASS
kernels are template instances fixed at compile time, each with its tile shape and
scheduler, so an engine built on them ships a set of instances and picks one per
shape; cuBLAS chooses inside the library by heuristics it does not publish. The
engineering consequence is the same everywhere. The threshold between two kernels
is a measured crossover on one machine, and a threshold copied from another
machine, or from another kernel pair, is a guess; the chapter's lab and its third
worked problem show how to measure and predict one.

\section{Split-K and stream-K}

When $M\times N$ yields too few tiles to fill the GPU --- a skinny product, or
a small layer --- the $K$ dimension can be split instead: several blocks compute
partial sums over different ranges of $K$ for the same output tile, and a final
reduction adds them. This is the same idea as Flash-Decoding's split of the
sequence (\Chref{decode-attention}), and it has the same cost: a reduction pass
and a workspace. Stream-K generalizes it by giving every SM an equal share of
the product's total inner-loop iterations, whatever the tiling, which removes
the wave-quantization loss; its authors reported gains of up to 14 times on some
of 32,824 shapes with a single tile configuration per precision
\cite{osama2023streamk}.

The decode case makes split-K concrete. A $4{,}096 \times 4{,}096$ weight matrix
times one vector, tiled 128 rows at a time, has only 32 output tiles: on 132 SMs,
100 would sit idle while 32 each streamed 128 rows of weights. Splitting $K$ four
ways turns 32 tiles into 128 work units, 97\% of the SMs, for the price of
adding four partial vectors of 4,096 numbers --- 64~KB of workspace, trivial
beside the 32~MiB of weights (derived). Decode's matrix--vector kernels split
this way, or split rows finer than any GEMM tile, precisely because a skinny
product has too few tiles to occupy the machine.

\HappenedSection{the first edition's loop-order and tiling measurements}

This book's own first edition measured the bottom of the ladder on the M1 and
kept the losses (records in \texttt{research/benchmarks/}, 2026-09-03). Changing
the loop order from $i,j,k$ to $i,k,j$ made a single-threaded scalar product
3.4 to 9.2 times faster at sizes 64 to 256 --- identical arithmetic, contiguous
memory. The blocked kernel's teaching default of $32\times32\times32$ tiles was
not the best tile on that machine: 64 was, at 4.3 times the unblocked loop. The
blocked kernel \emph{lost} at sizes 8 and 16, where its bookkeeping outweighed
its reuse. And with a fixed $512\times512$ weight matrix, eight columns ran at
fewer FLOPs per second through the blocked kernel than one column through the
matrix--vector loop, while sixty-four columns ran fastest of all --- the skinny
product's cliff, measured in scalar code before any GPU was involved.

The second edition's lab, written from scratch three weeks later, found the same
two things at a different size: at 512 the blocked kernel ran slower than the
reordered loop it was meant to improve, and the decode kernel and the GEMM
crossed between 8 and 16 columns. None of those ratios is a portable constant;
all of them are the reason engines choose kernels by shape, and the reason this
book re-measures instead of quoting.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-27}

Each GPU generation changes the instruction that kernels are built around:
Hopper's asynchronous warpgroup MMA and TMA \cite{nvidia2026cutlasswgmma}, then
Blackwell's tensor memory, paired-SM MMA and native block-scaled low-precision
operands \cite{nvidia2026cutlassblackwell}. Kernel authoring is following with
Python-level tile languages (\Chref{kernel-languages}). Work decomposition is a
choice libraries make differently. CUTLASS, NVIDIA's open template library, uses
a persistent tile scheduler by default on both Hopper and Blackwell; stream-K
must be requested, and once requested its decomposition defaults to a heuristic
that chooses per problem among data-parallel, split-K and stream-K
(\texttt{tile\_scheduler.hpp} and \texttt{sm90\_tile\_scheduler\_stream\_k.hpp}
at release v4.8.0). cuBLAS makes the same choices inside a closed library and
does not document them per shape; on the M1, Apple's library does not either,
which is why the book measures its steps rather than naming them.
