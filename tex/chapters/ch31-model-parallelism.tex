\chapter{Tensor, Pipeline and Context Parallelism}\label{ch:model-parallelism}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
When a model does not fit on one device, or one device is too slow, the
model must be split. Tensor parallelism splits each matrix and pays collective
communication in every layer; pipeline parallelism splits the layers and pays
in bubbles; context parallelism splits the sequence for long prefill. This
chapter derives each, prices its communication in bytes against the
interconnect's bandwidth, and shows how to choose a layout for a given model,
context and latency target.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{A model split across two processes}

Split Llama~3.2~3B between two llama.cpp processes on the M1: 15 of its 29
layers behind llama.cpp's RPC server, reached over a loopback TCP connection,
and 14 in the main process, both running on the same GPU. Generation slowed
from 25.4 to 24.4 tokens per second, about 1.7~milliseconds more per token, and
prefill of 512 tokens from 289 to 285 tokens per second (measured,
configurations interleaved, two rounds of two runs; record
\texttt{2026-09-24-m1-part6}). Only 12~KiB of activations crossed the boundary
per token. What each token paid for was the round trip itself: copy the
activations in, run the remote half of the graph, copy the result back, one
command after another. In a first pass, with the machine heavily loaded by
other work, the same split cost 13\% for this model and 35\% for an
8-billion-parameter one; a boundary that is cheap on an idle machine is the
first thing contention finds.

That was one boundary per token. Tensor parallelism, the layout that makes
large models fast on a single node, puts a synchronization in every layer ---
two per layer, 56 per token for this model. At the laptop's 1.7~milliseconds
each, those would take nearly 100~milliseconds per token, more than twice the
whole 39-millisecond step (illustrative). Splitting a model is only worth it on links whose
per-exchange latency is measured in microseconds, and this chapter is about
pricing each way of splitting against the links that carry it.

\Foundations{Partitioning creates a communication contract}{
Splitting a matrix by output columns produces pieces of an output that can be
concatenated. Splitting its reduction dimension produces partial sums that must be
added. An all-gather makes distributed pieces available together; an all-reduce
combines corresponding values and distributes the result. These operations have
different traffic and synchronization costs. Pipeline parallelism instead passes
activations between stages holding different layers. Chapter~\ref{app:notation} gives
the matrix convention. Before applying bandwidth numbers, identify which tensor
crosses which link, in which direction, and how often per generated token.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: collective bytes against interconnect bandwidth}

Split a model's matrices across $N$ devices and each device holds $1/N$ of the
weights, reads $1/N$ of them per step, and must exchange partial results. The
exchange is an all-reduce: every device contributes a vector and every device
receives the sum. A ring all-reduce of $S$ bytes moves $2(N-1)/N \cdot S$
bytes through each device's link, the factor NVIDIA's benchmarks call bus
bandwidth \cite{nccltests2026performance}, so its time is about
\begin{equation}
t_{\text{AR}} \approx \alpha + \frac{2(N-1)}{N}\cdot\frac{S}{\beta},
\label{eq:allreduce-time}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha$ a fixed latency per collective and $\beta$ the link's bandwidth in
one direction.

Megatron-LM's layout needs two all-reduces per transformer layer in the forward
pass \cite{shoeybi2019megatron}, each of $B \cdot d \cdot b$ bytes for $B$ tokens
in the step. For Llama~3 70B ($d = 8{,}192$, 80 layers) in BF16 on 8 GPUs, a
decode step of one token all-reduces 16~KiB 160 times; each moves 28~KiB
through each GPU's link, 64~ns at NVLink~4's 450~GB/s per direction (derived) ---
negligible against $\alpha$, which for decode is the whole cost
\todo{measure: NCCL all-reduce latency for 16~KiB to 1~MiB messages on an 8-GPU
NVLink node}. Prefill is the opposite. For an 8,192-token prompt each
all-reduce is 128~MiB, 235~MB through each link: 0.52~ms at 450~GB/s, 84~ms for
the 160 of them, against 157~ms of arithmetic per GPU at full BF16 peak and
about 315 at a more typical half of it (derived). On PCIe Gen5 instead of
NVLink, the same all-reduces take 590~ms: communication would exceed the
compute it serves (derived, at 64~GB/s per direction).

Pipeline parallelism, which gives each device a contiguous block of layers,
sends only the activations at each boundary: 16~KiB per token for this model,
0.3~$\mu$s at the 50~GB/s of a 400~Gb/s network port, 2.7~ms for the whole
8,192-token prompt (derived). Its cost is not bytes but idle time, which the
section on bubbles prices.

\section{Why split a model}

Two reasons, and they pull toward different layouts. \emph{Capacity:} Llama~3
70B in BF16 is 141~GB, more than an 80~GB H100, and a model that does not fit
must be split somehow. \emph{Speed:} decode is bandwidth-bound
(\Chref{roofline}), so eight GPUs reading one-eighth of the weights each produce
a token about eight times sooner --- 5.3~ms per token for this model on eight
H100s at batch 1, against 17.7~ms on one B200 that can hold it (derived from
3.35 and 8~TB/s) --- as long as the communication does not eat the gain.
Capacity cares only that the pieces fit; speed cares about every microsecond the
pieces spend talking.

\section{Tensor parallelism}

Tensor parallelism splits each layer's matrices across devices so that every
device takes part in every layer. Megatron-LM's arrangement pairs the splits so
that most communication cancels: the feed-forward block's first matrix is split
by columns and its second by rows, so each device computes a slice of the
hidden activation and a partial output with no exchange in between; attention
is split by heads, each device computing whole heads, and its output
projection by rows. That leaves one all-reduce after attention and one after
the feed-forward block --- ``only two all-reduces in the forward path'' per
layer, in the paper's words \cite{shoeybi2019megatron}. vLLM implements this
algorithm for its tensor parallelism (\texttt{docs/serving/parallelism\_scaling.md}
at commit \texttt{bcdacfc}).

The split has limits set by the model. Heads divide among devices only if there
are at least as many key/value heads as devices; beyond that, key/value heads
and their cache are replicated (\Chref{shrinking-kv}). And every layer now waits
for the slowest device and the collective, so tensor parallelism wants
identical devices on the fastest link available.

\section{The collectives each layer pays}

An all-reduce is a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather: each device ends
the first phase owning the sum of one slice, and the second phase shares the
slices. The ring algorithm does each phase in $N-1$ steps over neighbouring
links, which is where the factor $2(N-1)/N$ of
Equation~\eqref{eq:allreduce-time} comes from; the all-gather and
reduce-scatter alone each cost $(N-1)/N$ \cite{nccltests2026performance}. Two
regimes follow. Decode moves kilobytes per collective and pays latency: 160
collectives per token, each with its fixed $\alpha$, set a floor on time per
token that no bandwidth removes. Prefill moves hundreds of megabytes and pays
bandwidth, so it can hide communication behind the next layer's arithmetic if
the engine overlaps them. Engines differ mainly in how much of each regime they
manage to hide.

\section{Pipeline parallelism and its bubbles}

Pipeline parallelism gives each of $p$ devices a contiguous block of layers
and passes activations from one to the next. It needs little bandwidth and no
collective at all, so it crosses slow links well, and it splits a model of any
shape, evenly or not. Its cost is time: one sequence's token must pass through
the stages one after another, so pipeline parallelism shortens nothing for a
single request --- it only lets several requests occupy different stages at
once. GPipe introduced batch-splitting pipelining for training, dividing
batches into micro-batches to keep the stages busy \cite{huang2019gpipe}; with $m$
micro-batches in flight on $p$ stages, the fraction of time a stage sits idle
waiting for work is $(p-1)/(m+p-1)$ (derived). Inference serving meets the same
arithmetic with requests instead of micro-batches: pipeline stages stay busy
only if enough independent batches are in flight.

That is why vLLM's guidance is to use tensor parallelism within a node and
pipeline parallelism across nodes, and pipeline parallelism even within a node
when its GPUs lack NVLink, ``for higher throughput and lower communication
overhead'' (\texttt{docs/serving/parallelism\_scaling.md} at \texttt{bcdacfc}).
The opening's split was a pipeline in miniature: two stages, one request, and
the whole cost of the boundary with none of the concurrency that would pay for
it.

\section{Context parallelism and ring attention}

The third axis is the sequence. For a million-token prefill, splitting weights
does not help enough, because attention's cost grows with the square of the
context (\Chref{long-context}). Context parallelism gives each device a slice of
the tokens; attention then needs every earlier token's keys and values. Ring
attention passes key/value blocks around a ring of devices and overlaps each
transfer with the attention computed on the block before
\cite{liu2023ringattention}, and Meta's inference variants, passing keys and
values or passing queries, prefilled a million tokens of Llama~3 405B in 77
seconds on 128 H100s (their measurement, \cite{yang2024contextparallel}). For
decode, the cache itself is split along the sequence; vLLM's decode context
parallelism exists because tensor parallelism beyond the number of key/value
heads only duplicates the cache (\Chref{shrinking-kv}). NVIDIA's Helix
generalizes the idea: shard the cache across GPUs during attention, then reuse
the same GPUs for tensor or expert parallelism in the feed-forward layers; its
authors report up to 1.5 times lower token-to-token latency at fixed batch, and
up to 32 times larger batches within the same latency budget for DeepSeek-R1
on Blackwell \cite{bhatia2025helix}.

\section{Choosing a layout}

A layout is chosen by working through the constraints in order. First, fit: the
weights plus the cache the target batch needs must fit across the devices.
Second, the link: tensor parallelism only where the interconnect makes
Equation~\eqref{eq:allreduce-time} small against the step --- NVLink within a
node --- and pipeline parallelism across slower links. Third, the latency
target: decode's per-token floor rises with every collective, so interactive
services keep tensor groups small and replicate the model instead. Fourth, the
workload: long prompts argue for context parallelism in prefill, mixtures of
experts for expert parallelism (\Chref{expert-parallelism}), and a service with
both prefill- and decode-heavy traffic may run the phases on different layouts
altogether (\Chref{disaggregated-serving}). Pope and colleagues turned this
reasoning into an analytical model and chose partitionings of PaLM for the
application's latency or throughput target (\cite{pope2022scaling}; the case below).

\section{Interconnects}

The numbers that decide all of this are the links. An H100 SXM's NVLink moves
900~GB/s and its PCIe Gen5 port 128~GB/s \cite{nvidia2026h100}; NVIDIA's
figures count both directions together (its NVLink page says so; a PCIe Gen5
$\times$16 link carries about 63~GB/s each way), and the NVL variant's NVLink
moves 600~GB/s.
Blackwell's NVLink doubles to 1.8~TB/s per GPU, 14.4~TB/s across a DGX B200's
eight GPUs \cite{nvidia2026nvlink,nvidia2026dgxb200}, and a GB300 NVL72 rack
connects 72 GPUs with 130~TB/s of NVLink in total and gives each GPU an
800~Gb/s network port \cite{nvidia2026gb300}. Between nodes, 400~Gb/s
InfiniBand ports, 50~GB/s each, are typical of the last generation
\cite{nvidia2026dgxb200}. About a factor of ten separates NVLink from the
network, per direction. Every layout decision is a decision about which of these
links each byte crosses.

Start by naming the object being divided. Tensor-parallel ranks must combine partial layer results. Pipeline stages send activations between different layers and keep the state for their own layers. In the illustrated context-parallel arrangement, each rank owns query rows but needs keys and values from other sequence partitions. These communication edges explain why adding devices does not by itself predict a speedup.

\LedgerSection

Splitting one model across $N$ devices:

\begin{ConstraintLedger}
Memory capacity & buys & Each device holds about $1/N$ of the weights (and, if key/value heads allow, of the cache). \\
Memory bandwidth & buys & Aggregate bandwidth: decode reads $1/N$ of the weights per device per token. \\
Latency & spends & Two all-reduces per layer for tensor parallelism, a fixed cost per collective that dominates decode; boundaries and bubbles for pipelines. \\
Cost & spends & The interconnect: NVLink-class links for tensor parallelism, many in-flight batches for pipelines. \\
\end{ConstraintLedger}

\HappenedSection{partitioning PaLM for inference}

In 2022 a Google team faced the problem this chapter describes at the largest
scale then served: PaLM, 540 billion parameters, on TPU v4 slices. Rather than
choose one layout, they built a simple analytical model of inference
efficiency and used it to pick multi-dimensional partitionings of the weights
and activations for each application's requirements, reaching a new Pareto
frontier of latency against model FLOPS utilization. With 8-bit weights, PaLM
generated at 29~ms per token at small batch; processing input tokens at large
batch it reached 76\% utilization; and multi-query attention let it serve
contexts up to 32 times longer (the authors' measurements,
\cite{pope2022scaling}). This chapter's napkin math says why the choice depends
on the target: decode pays per-collective latency and prefill pays bandwidth,
so a layout tuned for one is a compromise for the other, and a service that
needs both must accept the compromise or run the two phases separately
(\Chref{disaggregated-serving}).

\FrontierSection{2026-09-24}

The fabric is growing faster than the model: rack-scale NVLink domains of 72
GPUs make tensor and expert groups of dozens of devices practical inside one
fast network \cite{nvidia2026gb300}, and designs such as Helix split each layer
differently for attention and for the feed-forward block
\cite{bhatia2025helix}. Context parallelism is moving from research into
engines for prefill and decode (\texttt{docs/serving/context\_parallel\_deployment.md}
in vLLM). The open question is less which axis to split than how to switch
between splits from one layer, or one phase, to the next.
