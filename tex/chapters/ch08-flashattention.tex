\chapter{Attention as a Memory Problem: From Online Softmax to FlashAttention}\label{ch:flashattention}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
Standard attention materializes a score matrix that grows with the square of
the sequence length and moves it through slow memory several times.
FlashAttention's insight is that attention's cost is memory traffic: with an
online softmax, attention can be computed tile by tile in on-chip memory
without ever writing the score matrix. This chapter derives the online
softmax by hand, builds a tiled attention and checks it against a naive
oracle, measures what the fused kernel saves inside llama.cpp, then follows
FlashAttention's four generations as each adapted to a new GPU --- better
parallelism, asynchrony and FP8 on Hopper, and on Blackwell a software
exponential because the tensor cores outran everything else.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{Attention that runs out of memory before it runs out of time}

Take eight heads of causal attention with head width 128 --- a quarter of
one layer of an 8-billion-parameter model --- and run them on the M1's GPU
with PyTorch, three ways, at longer and longer sequences. The inputs and output
are small: at 8,192 tokens, queries, keys, values and output together are
64~MiB in 16-bit floats. So that failures would be clean rather than a
swapping laptop, we capped PyTorch's GPU allocator at 6~GiB, and every run that
completed was checked against a float64 computation of three query rows,
including the last, which attends to every key (record
\texttt{2026-09-23-m1-part2}).

\toprule

\midrule

\bottomrule

The textbook version --- compute every score, mask, softmax, multiply by the
values --- ran at 4,096 tokens and failed at 8,192. Its 16-bit score matrix is
1~GiB there, and a softmax computed in 32-bit floats needs two more copies at
twice that size; the allocator refused the last one. PyTorch's fused
\texttt{scaled\_dot\_product\_attention} did not avoid the matrix on this GPU.
Its internal buffers, which the cap does not govern, took 4.1~GiB at 8,192
tokens and 16.5~GiB at 16,384 --- more than the machine's 16~GB of memory ---
and at 32,768 it asked Metal for a single 16~GiB buffer and was refused. The
third version, two dozen lines of PyTorch that walk the keys in blocks of
1,024 while keeping a running maximum and a running sum for each query row,
ran at every length. Its temporaries are a few 32~MiB blocks of scores,
however long the sequence (derived), and it agreed with float64 to within
$3\times10^{-5}$. At 4,096 tokens, where all three ran, it was also the fastest.

The arithmetic was never the problem: all three versions do the same
multiplications. The problem is a matrix, quadratic in the sequence length,
that exists only to be summed away. This chapter is about never writing it
down.

\Foundations{Normalize a row, not the whole matrix}{
Attention first scores eligible keys for each query, then normalizes that query's
score row into nonnegative weights summing to one. Multiplying those weights by values
gives a weighted average, not a lookup of a single winner. Subtracting the row maximum
before exponentiation changes neither the exact probabilities nor the weighted
average, while avoiding overflow. A causal mask excludes future positions from that
normalization. Online softmax must preserve the same row result while processing only
a tile at a time. Appendix~\ref{app:numerics} supplies the stable-softmax example;
Chapter~\ref{ch:forward-pass} supplies the attention shapes.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: counting attention's bytes}

For one head, a sequence of $S$ tokens and head width $d_h$, causal attention
computes a score matrix $\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}/\sqrt{d_h}$ of
shape $S\times S$, applies a row-wise softmax, and multiplies by
$\mathbf{V}$. The arithmetic is about $4S^{2}d_h$ FLOPs per head (two matrix
products), halved by causal masking. The inputs and output --- $\mathbf{Q}$,
$\mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{V}$, $\mathbf{O}$ --- are $4Sd_h$ numbers.

A naive implementation writes the $S^{2}$ scores to memory, reads them back
to compute the softmax, writes the probabilities, and reads them again for the
second product: about $4S^{2}$ numbers of traffic against $4Sd_h$ for the
inputs and output. With $d_h=128$ the score traffic is $S/128$ times the
input traffic --- 64 times at 8,192 tokens, 256 times at 32,768. Capacity
suffers first: at 32,768 tokens the scores of one 32-head layer are
$32\times32{,}768^{2}\times2$ bytes, 64~GiB in 16-bit floats (derived) ---
four times the 16~GiB buffer that Metal refused for the opening's eight
heads --- for a matrix whose only purpose is to be summed away. The napkin math says the
problem is not arithmetic. It is a matrix that never needed to exist in slow
memory.

Capacity is the first cost. Time is the second, and it depends on the machine.
Every score costs $4d_h$ FLOPs: $2d_h$ for its dot product and $2d_h$ for its
share of the second product. In the three-kernel version it also costs
16~bytes if the scores are 32-bit floats --- written by the first product, read
and rewritten by the softmax, read by the second product. That is an arithmetic
intensity of $d_h/4$ FLOP/byte: 32 at $d_h = 128$, 16 at the lab's $d_h = 64$
(derived). Set against the ridges of equation~\eqref{eq:ridge}:

\begin{center}\small

\noindent One algorithm, three predictions. On a CPU core, fusing attention
should save memory and no time. On the M1's GPU it should save a little time,
more as the sequence grows. On a data-center GPU, the unfused kernel should
starve the tensor cores. FlashAttention's authors measured that last case on an
A100 for GPT-2 medium at 1,024 tokens, forward and backward passes together:
standard attention did 66.6~GFLOPs of work, moved 40.3~GB through HBM and took
41.7~ms; FlashAttention did \emph{more} arithmetic, 75.2~GFLOPs --- its
backward pass recomputes the scores rather than storing them --- moved 4.4~GB
and took 7.3~ms \cite[Figure~2]{dao2022flashattention}. A ninth of the traffic,
a sixth of the time. This chapter's lab tests the first prediction and
llama.cpp the second.

\section{Softmax needs the whole row --- or does it?}

The softmax of a row of scores $x_1,\dots,x_n$ is
$e^{x_j-m}/\sum_i e^{x_i-m}$, where subtracting the row maximum $m$ keeps the
exponentials from overflowing (Appendix~\ref{app:sampling}). Written that way
it seems to need the whole row before any output can be produced, and the
textbook implementation takes three passes over it: one to find $m$, one to
compute and store the exponentials and their sum, and one to divide every
stored value by the sum. llama.cpp's Metal softmax kernel is exactly that ---
one threadgroup per row, three loops over the row (\texttt{kernel\_soft\_max}
in \texttt{ggml-metal.metal} at commit \texttt{d006858}) --- and it sits between
a kernel that writes the row to memory and one that reads it back.

Milakov and Gimelshein noticed that the first two passes can be one
\cite{milakov2018online}. Keep a running maximum $m$ and a running sum $d$ of
exponentials taken against it. When a new score $x$ arrives,
\begin{equation}
m' = \max(m, x), \qquad d' = d\,e^{m-m'} + e^{x-m'}.
\label{eq:online-normalizer}
\end{equation}
If $x$ does not raise the maximum, the factor $e^{m-m'}$ is 1 and the update is
an ordinary sum. If it does, every term already in $d$ was taken against a
maximum that is now too small, and multiplying by $e^{m-m'}$ moves them all to
the new one at once. After the last score, $d$ is the softmax denominator, found
in one pass. Attention can go one step further, because it never needs the
probabilities themselves --- only their weighted sum of value vectors --- and
that sum can be carried along in the same way.

\section{The online softmax}

Process the row in pieces --- one score at a time, or a block of scores. Keep a
running maximum $m$, a running denominator $\ell=\sum e^{x_i-m}$ and a running
output $\mathbf{o}=\sum e^{x_i-m}\mathbf{v}_i$. When a new piece raises the
maximum from $m$ to $m'$, rescale:
\begin{equation}
\ell \leftarrow \ell\,e^{m-m'} + \sum_{\text{new}} e^{x_i-m'},\qquad
\mathbf{o} \leftarrow \mathbf{o}\,e^{m-m'} + \sum_{\text{new}} e^{x_i-m'}\mathbf{v}_i,
\label{eq:online-softmax}
\end{equation}
and at the end divide: the attention output is $\mathbf{o}/\ell$.

\emph{Why it is exact.} After any prefix of the row, $\ell$ and $\mathbf{o}$
are sums of $e^{x_i-m}$ and $e^{x_i-m}\mathbf{v}_i$ over the scores seen so far,
all taken against the current $m$. Multiplying both by $e^{m-m'}$ moves the
reference point from $m$ to $m'$, because $e^{x_i-m}\,e^{m-m'} = e^{x_i-m'}$,
and the new terms are added against $m'$ too. At the end
\[
\frac{\mathbf{o}}{\ell}
= \frac{\sum_i e^{x_i-m}\,\mathbf{v}_i}{\sum_k e^{x_k-m}}
= \sum_i \frac{e^{x_i}}{\sum_k e^{x_k}}\,\mathbf{v}_i ,
\]
because the common factor $e^{-m}$ cancels: the softmax-weighted sum, computed
in one pass with a constant amount of state per query row. Notice what the
argument did not use: that $m$ is the maximum. Any reference point gives the
same quotient in exact arithmetic. The maximum is chosen only so that no
exponential exceeds 1 --- for floating point's sake, not for correctness.
FlashAttention-4 turns that freedom into speed.

\emph{A worked example.} Take one query row whose four scores are 1, 3, 2 and 5,
with scalar values 1, 2, 3 and 4 (a head of width 1), and process it in two
blocks of two. The direct computation first. Against the maximum, 5, the
exponentials are $e^{-4}, e^{-2}, e^{-3}, e^{0}$ = 0.0183, 0.1353, 0.0498 and 1;
their sum is 1.2034; the softmax weights are 0.0152, 0.1125, 0.0414 and 0.8310;
and the output is $\sum_j w_j v_j = 3.6881$. Now the online version:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
Step & $m$ & $e^{m-m'}$ & $\ell$ & $o$ & $o/\ell$ \\
\midrule
Start & $-\infty$ & & 0 & 0 & \\
Block 1: scores 1 and 3 & 3 & & $e^{-2} + e^{0} = 1.1353$ & $1e^{-2} + 2e^{0} = 2.1353$ & 1.8808 \\
Block 2: rescale & 5 & 0.1353 & 0.1537 & 0.2890 & \\
Block 2: add scores 2 and 5 & 5 & & $+\,e^{-3} + e^{0} = 1.2034$ & $+\,3e^{-3} + 4e^{0} = 4.4383$ & 3.6881 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent After the first block the running answer, 1.8808, is exactly the
attention over the first two keys alone. The second block raises the maximum
from 3 to 5, the old sums are multiplied by $e^{-2}$, and the final quotient
matches the direct computation in every printed digit; the lab's test checks it
to $10^{-12}$ in 64-bit arithmetic. Two variations show what the rescale is for.
\emph{Forget it}, and block 1's terms stay weighted against the old maximum:
$\ell = 2.1851$, $o = 6.2847$, and the output is 2.8761 --- 22\% low, because
the first two keys now count $e^{2} = 7.4$ times too much against the last two.
\emph{Keep the old maximum on purpose}, adding block 2's terms against 3 rather
than 5: $\ell = 1.1353 + e^{-1} + e^{2} = 8.8923$,
$o = 2.1353 + 3e^{-1} + 4e^{2} = 32.7952$, and the output is 3.6881 again. The
answer is right; only the sizes of the intermediate numbers changed, and
$e^{2}$ is now the largest term instead of 1. That is the whole of
FlashAttention-4's conditional rescaling.

\emph{Merging partial results.} Nothing forces the pieces to be processed in
order by one worker. Two workers that each summarize a disjoint set of keys as
$(m_a, \ell_a, \mathbf{o}_a)$ and $(m_b, \ell_b, \mathbf{o}_b)$ can be combined:
\begin{equation}
m = \max(m_a, m_b),\qquad
\ell = \ell_a e^{m_a-m} + \ell_b e^{m_b-m},\qquad
\mathbf{o} = \mathbf{o}_a e^{m_a-m} + \mathbf{o}_b e^{m_b-m}.
\label{eq:softmax-merge}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:online-softmax} is the special case in which the second
summary is one new block. The merge is associative and commutative in exact
arithmetic, so the keys of one row can be divided among processors in any way
and the pieces combined in any order; \Chref{decode-attention} uses exactly this
to spread one decode step's attention over a whole GPU. The pair $(m, \ell)$ is
often stored as one number, the log-sum-exp $m + \ln \ell$, which is what
FlashAttention-2 keeps for its backward pass \cite{dao2023flashattention2}. In
floating point the merge is \emph{not} associative: a different division of the
keys, or a different order of merging, changes the last bits of the result.
The ``What actually happened'' section returns to that.

Milakov and Gimelshein published the running normalizer in 2018
\cite{milakov2018online}; applied to attention, with the output carried
alongside it, it is the core of FlashAttention.

\section{Tiling attention}

FlashAttention \cite{dao2022flashattention} applies the online softmax to
blocks. Split the queries into blocks of $B_r$ rows and the keys and values
into blocks of $B_c$. A query block is loaded into on-chip memory and the key
and value blocks stream past it; for each, the kernel computes a
$B_r \times B_c$ tile of scores on chip, updates every row's
$(m, \ell, \mathbf{o})$ with equation~\eqref{eq:online-softmax}, and moves on.
In outline, with the loop order of FlashAttention-2, which the lab also uses:

\begin{verbatim}
for each query block Q_i (B_r rows), in parallel:
    load Q_i; for each of its rows set m = -inf, l = 0, o = 0     (on chip)
    for each key block j at or left of the diagonal:
        load K_j and V_j
        S = Q_i K_j^T / sqrt(d_h)           a B_r x B_c tile, on chip
        if j == i: mask the scores above the diagonal
        m' = max(m, rowmax(S));  P = exp(S - m')
        l = l * exp(m - m') + rowsum(P)
        o = o * exp(m - m') + P V_j
        m = m'
    write o / l, and m + ln(l) for the backward pass
\end{verbatim}

\noindent The score matrix exists one tile at a time, in shared memory and
registers. The result is exact --- the same mathematics in a different order of
floating-point operations --- and the only memory beyond the inputs and the
output is the per-row state, linear in $S$; the paper proves both
\cite[Theorem~1]{dao2022flashattention}. The first FlashAttention ran the loops
the other way round, with the key blocks outside and the query blocks inside,
reading and writing each query block's partial output in HBM once per key block
\cite[Algorithm~1]{dao2022flashattention}; FlashAttention-2's order keeps it on
chip until it is finished. Figure~\ref{fig:ch08-tiling} follows one query block
and one row's state through the worked example.

\EngineFigure{ch08-tiling}{Tiled causal attention. Left: the score matrix as
$8 \times 8$ tiles; query block 5 stays on chip while key and value blocks 0 to
5 stream past, the diagonal tile is masked inside, and the tiles above the
diagonal are never computed. Right: one row's running maximum, denominator and
output through the worked example of the online softmax
(derived).}{fig:ch08-tiling}

\emph{Counting tiles.} At $S = 8{,}192$ with $128 \times 128$ tiles, the score
matrix has $64 \times 64 = 4{,}096$ tiles. Causal masking leaves
$64 \times 65/2 = 2{,}080$ of them to compute --- 64 on the diagonal, masked
inside --- and skips the other 2,016 without loading their keys (derived).
Causal attention does half the work of full attention, plus half a diagonal.

\emph{Counting bytes.} $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{O}$ cross slow memory once;
$\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ are read once per query block, because every query
block must meet every key block to its left. For one head at 8,192 tokens with
$d_h = 128$ in BF16 that is 4~MiB for $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{O}$ and, if
every re-read went to HBM, 130~MiB of keys and values: 2,080 tiles, each
loading a 32~KiB block of keys and a 32~KiB block of values. An unfused kernel
that computes the full square and masks it moves 512~MiB of 16-bit scores in
its four passes (derived). Larger query blocks mean fewer passes over the keys,
and the size of on-chip memory bounds the block. That is the paper's IO result:
standard attention needs $\Theta(Nd + N^2)$ accesses to HBM and FlashAttention
$\Theta(N^2 d^2 M^{-1})$, for $N$ tokens, head width $d$ and an on-chip memory
of $M$ elements, and no exact attention algorithm can do asymptotically better
over all $M$ \cite[Theorem~2 and Proposition~3]{dao2022flashattention}. In
practice the re-reads need not reach HBM at all. The query blocks of one head
read the same key blocks, and one head's keys and values at 8,192 tokens, 4~MiB,
fit many times over in an H100's 50~MB L2 cache (\Chref{matmul-hardware}); the
first worked problem shows how much depends on it. The ridge, again, is a
property of the whole chip's reuse.

\emph{Budgeting on-chip memory.} The block sizes come from the scarcest
capacity. FlashAttention-2 typically uses blocks of 64 or 128 rows by 64 or 128
columns, ``depending on the head dimension $d$ and the device shared memory
size'' \cite{dao2023flashattention2}. With $B_r = B_c = 128$ and $d_h = 128$ in
BF16, the query block is 32~KiB, a block of keys and a block of values 32~KiB
each, and a second stage of keys and values --- loaded while the first is being
multiplied --- another 64~KiB: 160~KiB of an H100's 228~KB of shared memory per
SM (derived). The $128 \times 128$ tile of 32-bit scores (64~KiB) and the
64~KiB output accumulator do not go to shared memory at all. On Hopper they
live in the registers of the warps that compute them; on Blackwell the matrix
units write them to tensor memory, 256~KB per SM
\cite{zadouri2026flashattention4}. The kernel's shape is the shape of the chip.

\section{Attention in llama.cpp, fused and unfused}

llama.cpp builds both versions from the same model graph, and one flag chooses.
Paraphrasing \texttt{build\_attn\_mha} in \texttt{src/llama-graph.cpp} at
\texttt{d006858}, for each layer:

\begin{verbatim}
flash attention on:   out = flash_attn_ext(q, k, v, mask, scale)     one kernel
flash attention off:  kq  = mul_mat(k, q)          32-bit scores, every head
                      kq  = soft_max_ext(kq, mask, scale)
                      out = mul_mat(v_transposed, kq)
\end{verbatim}

\noindent The unfused path forces the score product to 32-bit precision --- a
comment in the source warns that the operation ``tends to require high floating
point range'' --- and stores the value cache transposed, so that its second
product reads contiguous rows (\Chref{kv-cache}). The fused path is one
\texttt{flash\_attn\_ext} node per layer, with its accumulation also pinned to
32 bits. On the M1 its prefill kernel takes 8 queries per threadgroup and walks
the keys 64 at a time (\texttt{ggml-metal-impl.h}); with fewer than 20 queries
it switches to the decode kernel of \Chref{prefill-decode}.

The difference in memory is plain in the buffers llama.cpp reserves when it
creates a context. Loading Llama~3.2~3B with 4,096, 8,192 and 16,384 cells of
cache, flash attention off and then on, with the default micro-batch of 512
tokens (measured, record \texttt{2026-09-26-m1-pass3}):

\noindent With flash attention on, the buffer does not move. Its size is set
by the largest tensor left in the graph, the logits of a full micro-batch:
$128{,}256 \times 512 \times 4$~bytes = 250.5~MiB (derived). With it off, the
buffer grows by 400~MiB for every 8,192 more cells. That is one layer's scores
for 8,192 more keys --- 8,192 keys $\times$ 512 queries $\times$ 24 heads
$\times$ 4 bytes = 384~MiB --- plus 16~MiB, the size of a 32-bit mask for the
same keys (derived). One layer's worth, because the layers run one after
another and the graph allocator reuses the same memory for each. At the model's
full context of 131,072 cells, the unfused buffer would pass 6~GiB (derived),
for the scores of a single layer. The defaults choose for you:
\texttt{llama-completion} and \texttt{llama-server} default to \texttt{auto},
which turns the fused kernel on when every layer's attention can run on the
device that holds that layer's cache (\texttt{src/llama-context.cpp}), and
\Chref{kv-cache}'s run at 8,192 cells, with no flag, reported the fused
path's 256.50~MiB. \texttt{llama-bench} defaults to off in this build.

\section{FlashAttention-2: parallelism and work partitioning}

The first version was IO-aware but left parallelism on the table, in three
places \cite{dao2023flashattention2}. \emph{Across the chip:} FlashAttention
parallelized over batch and heads only. With long sequences and small batches
that gives fewer thread blocks than an A100 has SMs, so FlashAttention-2 also
parallelizes over the sequence, one thread block per query block --- the loop
order above. \emph{Within a thread block:} FlashAttention divided the keys and
values among its four warps, which then had to write their partial results to
shared memory, synchronize and add them, a scheme the paper calls ``split-K''.
FlashAttention-2 divides the queries instead and keeps $\mathbf{K}$ and
$\mathbf{V}$ visible to all four warps, and ``there is no need for
communication between warps''. \emph{In the arithmetic:} it cut the operations
that are not matrix multiplications, because they are the expensive ones. An
A100 does ``312 TFLOPs/s of FP16/BF16 matmul, but only 19.5 TFLOPs/s of
non-matmul FP32'', so ``each non-matmul FLOP is 16$\times$ more expensive than a
matmul FLOP''. The division by $\ell$ moves out of the inner loop to the end,
and the backward pass keeps one log-sum-exp per row instead of both $m$ and
$\ell$.

The result was about twice FlashAttention's speed and 50--73\% of the A100's
theoretical peak in the forward pass, and training at up to 225~TFLOP/s per
A100, 72\% model FLOPs utilization \cite{dao2023flashattention2}. The lab shows
the non-matmul lesson at small scale: its one-row-at-a-time online softmax
rescales the 64-element output after every key and takes 1.5--1.6 times as long
as the tiled version, which rescales once per block of 64. The lesson recurs in
every later chapter: an algorithm that moves the minimum bytes is only fast if
it also keeps every unit of the machine busy.

\section{FlashAttention-3: asynchrony and FP8 on Hopper}

On an H100, FlashAttention-2 reached only 35\% utilization
\cite{shah2024flashattention3}. Hopper added hardware that a synchronous kernel
cannot use: TMA copies issued by a single thread and warpgroup matrix
instructions that run asynchronously (\Chref{matmul-hardware}).
FlashAttention-3 uses it three ways. \emph{Warp specialization:} the warps of a
thread block become producers, which only issue TMA copies, and consumers,
which only compute, and Hopper's \texttt{setmaxnreg} instruction moves
registers from the first to the second, which need them for their
accumulators. \emph{Pingpong scheduling:} two consumer warpgroups take turns,
synchronized so that one computes its softmax while the other's matrix products
run. \emph{Pipelining within a warpgroup:} the second product of one block
overlaps the softmax of the next.

Why so much machinery to hide a softmax? The exponential. An H100 SXM5 has
``989 TFLOPS of FP16 matmul but only 3.9 TFLOPS of special functions such as
exponential''. At head width 128 there are 512 times more matrix FLOPs than
exponentials, but the exponential has 256 times lower throughput, so the
exponentials alone can take half as many cycles as the matrix products
\cite{shah2024flashattention3}. Overlapped with the matrix products, they cost
nothing; in series with them, a third of the kernel's time.

For FP8, FlashAttention-3 quantizes in blocks, with one scale per block, and
applies what the paper calls incoherent processing: it multiplies $\mathbf{Q}$
and $\mathbf{K}$ by the same random orthogonal matrix --- a Hadamard matrix
times random signs --- which spreads outliers across all the elements before
quantization and leaves $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$ unchanged, since
$(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^{\top} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$
for orthogonal $\mathbf{M}$. \Chref{kv-cache} met the same kind of rotation in
llama.cpp's quantized caches. The paper reports 1.5--2.0 times FlashAttention-2's
speed, 740~TFLOP/s in FP16 (75\% of peak), close to 1.2~PFLOP/s in FP8, and FP8
error 2.6 times lower than a baseline FP8 attention
\cite{shah2024flashattention3}.

\section{FlashAttention-4: when the exponential becomes the bottleneck}

Blackwell doubled the matrix units and nothing around them. Per SM per clock, a
B200's BF16 matrix instructions do 8,192 operations against Hopper's 4,096,
while the multifunction unit that evaluates exponentials still does 16 and
shared memory still delivers 128 bytes
\cite[section~2.2]{zadouri2026flashattention4}. For a $128 \times 128$ tile at
head width 128 the authors count 1,024 cycles of matrix work, 1,024 cycles of
exponentials and 768 of shared-memory traffic
\cite[section~3.1.1]{zadouri2026flashattention4}: balanced on a knife edge. The
balance has a simple form. Each score costs $4d_h$ matrix operations and one
exponential, so on a B200 the exponentials take
$(1/16)\,/\,(4d_h/8{,}192) = 128/d_h$ times as long as the matrix work, and on an
H100 $64/d_h$ (derived). At $d_h = 128$ that is 1 on Blackwell against Hopper's
one half; at $d_h = 64$ the exponential unit alone would take twice as long as
the matrices.

FlashAttention-4 attacks the non-matrix work from three sides
\cite{zadouri2026flashattention4}. \emph{A software exponential.} It computes
$2^x$ as $2^{\lfloor x \rfloor} \cdot 2^{x - \lfloor x \rfloor}$ --- a
Cody--Waite range reduction --- and evaluates the fractional power with a
degree-3 polynomial in Horner form on the ordinary fused multiply--add units,
with a maximum relative error of $8.8 \times 10^{-5}$ in 32 bits. Only 10--25\%
of each row's exponentials are emulated; the rest still go to the hardware
unit, so the two work side by side (section~3.1.3). \emph{Conditional
rescaling.} It keeps the old maximum unless a block raises it by more than a
threshold $\tau$, ``typically set to $\log_2(256) = 8.0$'' --- the kernel works
in base 2, so the exponentials may grow to 256 before a rescale is forced
(section~3.1.4). The worked example showed why that is exact: the reference
point cancels in $\mathbf{o}/\ell$. \emph{Asynchrony and tensor memory.}
Larger tiles and fully asynchronous matrix instructions keep the tensor cores
fed, and in the backward pass tensor memory and the two-SM matrix mode cut
shared-memory traffic and atomic additions.

The authors report up to 1,613~TFLOP/s in BF16 on a B200, 71\% of peak, up to
1.3 times cuDNN~9.13 and 2.7 times Triton, in a kernel written in CuTe DSL, a
language embedded in Python, that compiles 20--30 times faster than its C++
template equivalent. The lab reproduces the rescaling half of the story in
miniature. At 2,048 tokens, with the threshold at $\ln 256$ (the lab works in
base $e$), its tiled kernel performs no rescalings at all instead of 4,499 on
ordinary scores, and 1,604 instead of 12,456 on ``ramp'' keys whose scores grow
along the sequence, while agreeing with float64 to $5\times10^{-7}$ and
$1.5\times10^{-6}$. The second worked problem explains both counts.

\section{Masks, variable lengths and packed batches}

A serving engine rarely attends over one tidy square. Prompts in a batch have
different lengths; engines pack them end to end rather than padding them to a
common length, and attention kernels take the offsets as input.
FlashAttention's variable-length interface takes all the queries of a batch as
one tensor of shape (total queries, heads, head width), the keys likewise, and
a vector \texttt{cu\_seqlens} of $b + 1$ cumulative lengths that marks where
each of the $b$ sequences starts. With \texttt{causal=True} the mask is aligned
to the bottom-right corner of each sequence's score matrix, so that a request's
new queries see all of its earlier keys; \texttt{window\_size} turns on
sliding-window attention (\texttt{flash\_attn\_varlen\_func} in
\texttt{flash\_attn/flash\_attn\_interface.py}) \cite{daoailab2026flashattnrepo}.
The mask is never stored: it is a comparison of two indices inside the kernel.

llama.cpp does it differently. For each micro-batch it builds one mask, a
32-bit tensor of (cache cells) $\times$ (tokens), filled on the CPU with 0 or
$-\infty$ from each cell's sequence and position (\texttt{set\_input\_kq\_mask} in
\texttt{src/llama-kv-cache.cpp}; the cells are \Chref{kv-cache}'s) and cast to 16
bits for the fused kernel, and every head reads it. Sliding windows and ALiBi's
position biases go into the same tensor. So llama.cpp does materialize something
quadratic --- the mask, though not the scores. Shared by all the heads, it is
1/24 of one layer's scores for Llama~3.2~3B, and it is what lets one kernel
serve any mix of sequences in the cache without knowing their structure.

Every other variation --- sliding windows (\Chref{long-context}), attention
sinks, soft-capping of the scores, per-request masks for structured prompts
--- is a branch in the inner loop of the most performance-critical kernel in
the engine; llama.cpp's fused kernel already takes attention sinks and a
soft-capping value as arguments. That is why engines ship several attention backends and choose
among them by request shape and hardware (\Chref{decode-attention}).

exponentials overflow.

\HappenedSection{making the reduction order a design decision}

An online softmax reorders floating-point additions, and a parallel one
reorders them differently depending on how the work is split. Most engines
accept that results differ in the last bits from run to run. Hermon made the
opposite choice: its planned release gate is bit-identical output over a
thousand prompts at temperature zero (Hermon \texttt{docs/KERNEL\_DESIGN.md},
section 6.3; the gate itself has not yet been run, because it needs the
real-weights path). Its attention kernels follow four rules. The number of
splits is a function of the request's shape alone, never of core count or
load. Split results are combined in index order, not completion order.
Accumulators are 32-bit whatever the storage type. And the exponential is
vendored --- a range reduction and a degree-5 polynomial in a source file of
its own, compiled so that the compiler may not fuse its multiply--adds ---
because the system math libraries of different platforms disagree in the last
bit. The rules are tested, not just stated: the kernels produce identical bits
at 1 to 64 threads and with their task order reversed, and one test checks
directly that the split count depends on the shape and not on the hardware
(\texttt{crates/hermon-kernels/tests/differential.rs} at commit
\texttt{2a3fd52}; the native kernels are opt-in, so PREVIEW). The last rule is
the mirror image of FlashAttention-4's: both replace the library exponential
with a polynomial, one for speed and one for reproducibility. \Chref{fast-wrong-answers} returns to why this matters beyond
one engine.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-27}

FlashAttention-4 went from a paper in March 2026 to the kernel a major engine
runs on Blackwell by September. At the vLLM commit this book pins, the
FlashAttention backend runs version 4 on data-center Blackwell GPUs (compute
capability 10) and version 3 on Hopper; on Blackwell, though, vLLM's first
choice for causal attention is FlashInfer, with FlashAttention second, while
for non-causal attention FlashAttention comes first
(\texttt{vllm/platforms/cuda.py} and
\texttt{vllm/v1/attention/backends/fa\_utils.py} at \texttt{bcdacfc})
\cite{vllm2026attentionbackends}. Attention kernels are specializing by
architecture: DeepSeek's FlashMLA serves latent attention and, since September
2025, token-sparse attention for DeepSeek-V3.2 \cite{deepseek2025flashmla};
sparse and compressed attention appear in \Chref{long-context}. The direction
is set by the asymmetry FlashAttention-4 documents: matrix units keep outrunning
everything around them, so the work beside the matrix products ---
exponentials, rescaling, masking --- is where the next kernels will be won.

\item \emph{Prediction.} Estimate the shared memory a kernel with 128-query and

\item \emph{Implementation.} Add a sliding-window mask to the lab's tiled

outside the window. How many tiles does a window of 1,024 leave at 8,192 tokens?
\item \emph{Measurement.} Run \texttt{llama-bench -fa 0,1} on your machine at
prompt lengths from 512 to 8,192 and fit the 20-bytes-per-score model to the

your machine's streaming bandwidth from \Chref{roofline}?
\item \emph{Falsification.} The conditional rescaling is exact in real
arithmetic. Find where it stops being exact in floating point: the largest
threshold for which a block's exponentials still fit in FP16 (whose largest
value is 65,504) when they are converted for the second product, and construct
scores that overflow a kernel storing them in FP16 with a threshold of 12. Does
BF16 have the same limit?
\end{enumerate}
