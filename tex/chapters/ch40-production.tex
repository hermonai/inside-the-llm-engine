\chapter{Operating Inference in Production}\label{ch:production}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
An engine that is fast on one GPU is not yet a service. This chapter
covers what changes for a fleet: model loading and cold starts, autoscaling
on signals that track user-visible latency, routing that is aware of load,
cached prefixes and adapters, isolation between tenants, observability,
handling of timeouts and partial output, safe rollouts of new models, and the
cost controls that decide whether a deployment makes money.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{Past the knee}

One llama-server on the M1, four slots, Llama~3.2~3B, requests of about 200
prompt tokens and 48 generated tokens arriving at random. At 0.09 requests per
second the median user saw the first token after 0.78~s and the slowest after
1.5~s. At 0.36 per second --- 70\% of what this server can complete --- the
median had barely moved, to 0.93~s, but the 99th percentile had quadrupled to
6.3~s. At 0.43 it was 11.9~s. Above 0.52 per second the server could not keep
up at all: it completed at most 0.52 requests per second whatever arrived, and
at 0.63 the median user waited 12.7~s and the slowest 29~s, with the queue
still growing when the run ended (Figure~\ref{fig:ch40-knee}; measured, 120~s
per rate; record \texttt{2026-09-26-m1-part8}).

Nothing in the engine changed between those rows. The same kernels ran at the
same speed; only the queue in front of them grew. Operating inference is the
work of keeping every server on the left of that curve --- by adding capacity
before the knee, routing each request where it will wait least, and knowing
from the server's own counters which side of the knee it is on.

\Foundations{A component target is not an end-to-end promise}{
A service-level indicator is a measured quantity; an objective is a target for it over
a population and interval. A fast kernel cannot guarantee a request objective if
routing, queueing, loading or streaming dominates. Increasing replicas also changes
cache locality and warm-up behaviour, so capacity does not always scale immediately
with process count. Chapter~\ref{ch:request-lifecycle} defines completion and failure;
Chapter~\ref{ch:benchmarking} defines useful measurement. Build operational decisions
around the request path and explicit failure modes, not only around device
utilization.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: cold-start time and headroom}

Queueing theory explains the shape. For the simplest queue, one server with
random arrivals and service times, the mean time in the system is the service
time divided by $1 - \rho$, where $\rho$ is the utilization: 70\% utilization
multiplies it by 3.3, 90\% by 10. Real servers batch, which moves the curve but
not its form, and the tail diverges before the mean, as the opening showed.

Capacity arrives late. A new replica is useless until its weights are loaded
and its engine is warm, so a fleet must carry enough headroom to absorb the
traffic that grows while it waits:
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\text{target}} \le \frac{1}{1 + g\,(t_{\text{detect}} + t_{\text{cold}})},
\label{eq:headroom}
\end{equation}
where $g$ is the fractional growth of traffic per unit time. With traffic
growing 10\% per minute, one minute to notice and three to start a replica, a
fleet must run below $1/1.4 = 71\%$ utilization to stay under capacity through
the scale-up (derived, illustrative values) --- and the opening says that at
70\% the tail is already four times its unloaded value.

Cold-start time is mostly bytes. On the M1, with the model file in the page
cache, llama-server answered its health check 1.1--2.0~s after starting and its
first token 0.11~s later; Ollama loaded the same model in 1.1~s (measured; three
runs each). A data-center replica moves far more bytes over slower paths. A
70-billion-parameter model in 16 bits is 140~GB: 140~s from storage at 1~GB/s,
14~s at 10~GB/s (derived), and then the engine compiles kernels and captures
graphs before it serves. vLLM's developers put a full reload at 30 to more than
100~seconds per model switch \cite{vllm2025sleepmode}.

\section{Loading models and cold starts}

A cold start has more parts than the copy. vLLM's developers list them: weights
copied to the GPU, the memory allocator set up, CUDA graphs captured, kernels
compiled just in time, and caches warmed by the first requests
\cite{vllm2025sleepmode}. Faster loaders attack the first. vLLM at
\texttt{bcdacfc} accepts fifteen load formats, among them streaming loaders
(\texttt{runai\_streamer}, \texttt{fastsafetensors}), pre-sharded and serialized
states (\texttt{sharded\_state}, \texttt{tensorizer}) and a dummy format that
skips weights for testing (\texttt{vllm/model\_executor/model\_loader/\_\_init\_\_.py}).
Keeping the process alive attacks the rest. vLLM's sleep mode puts a model to
sleep by moving its weights to CPU memory (level 1) or discarding them (level
2) while keeping the process, its allocator, graphs and compiled kernels. Its
developers measured waking at 18 to 200 times the speed of a full reload, and
the first request after waking 61--88\% faster than the first after a cold start
(A100 GPUs; developers' measurements) \cite{vllm2025sleepmode}. On the M1 the
llama.cpp server warms itself during loading: its first request was no slower
than its second.

\section{Autoscaling on the right signal}

The signal must lead the latency users see, and utilization of the GPU does not.
A decoding GPU shows busy whenever a kernel runs, whether it serves one
sequence or sixty; the opening's server was ``busy'' at every rate. The signals
that lead are the queue and the cache. llama.cpp exports
\texttt{requests\_deferred}, the requests waiting for a slot: 0 or 1 below 70\%
of capacity, 2 at 70\%, 6 at 83\% and 24 when overloaded in the opening's sweep.
vLLM exports \texttt{vllm:num\_requests\_waiting}, split by reason into waiting for
capacity and deferred by transient constraints, with
\texttt{vllm:kv\_cache\_usage\_perc}, preemption counts and histograms of queue
time, first-token time and inter-token latency (\texttt{vllm/v1/metrics/loggers.py}
at \texttt{bcdacfc}). Scaling on queue length or on a windowed 90th-percentile
TTFT acts before users notice; scaling on utilization acts after.

Scaling down needs the same care. A replica holds cached prefixes, and removing
one throws away work that a router would have reused; a replica that is draining
must stop receiving new requests but finish the ones it holds, including long
generations.

\section{Routing: load, prefixes and adapters}

A router in front of several replicas decides which one serves each request,
and the best choice is rarely the least loaded. A request whose prefix is cached
on one replica costs only its new tokens there and its whole prompt anywhere
else (\Chref{prefix-caching}). SGLang's cache-aware load balancer keeps an
approximate radix tree of each worker's cache and routes by expected hit; its
developers reported the hit rate rising from 20\% to 75\% and throughput by 1.9
times \cite{sglang2024v04}. llm-d and NVIDIA Dynamo route by the overlap of a
request's blocks with each worker's cache and by load, and report up to twice
the speed to first token with such routing (vendor measurements)
\cite{llmd2026,nvidia2026dynamo}. Routing by adapter follows the same logic: a
request for a LoRA adapter is cheapest where that adapter is loaded, and
llama.cpp batches together only requests with equal adapter sets
(\Chref{multi-model-serving}).

Affinity has a cost when the routing rule is wrong. In August 2025, Anthropic
sent some short-context requests for Claude Sonnet~4 to servers configured for
an upcoming 1-million-token context window, and those servers gave degraded
answers. The bug affected 0.8\% of that model's requests at first; a routine
load-balancing change on 29 August raised the share, to 16\% in the worst hour on
31 August. Because routing was sticky, a user whose request reached a wrong
server was likely to stay on it; about 30\% of Claude Code users who made
requests in the period had at least one message misrouted. The fix, deployed on
4 September, sent short- and long-context requests to the correct pools
\cite{anthropic2025postmortem}. A router is part of the model's correctness, not
only of its speed.

\section{Isolation between tenants}

Sharing is how inference becomes cheap --- one batch, one cache, one set of
weights for many users --- and every shared resource is a channel between
tenants. The one specific to inference is the prefix cache: a cached prompt is
answered faster, so a user who can measure time can test whether someone else
sent a prompt. An audit of commercial APIs detected prompt caches shared across
users at seven providers, including OpenAI, and recovered a fact about a model's
architecture from timing alone \cite{gu2025auditing}. The defence is to key the
cache by tenant. vLLM lets a request carry a \texttt{cache\_salt}, mixed into the
hash of its first block so that only requests with the same salt can share
cached blocks; its own description is that the salt prevents an attacker from
guessing prompts in multi-user environments (\texttt{bcdacfc}). The same
reasoning applies to batch composition, which \Chref{fast-wrong-answers} showed
can change a request's answer: a tenant who needs reproducibility needs batch
invariance, or a replica of its own.

Noisy neighbours are the older problem. A tenant with long prompts consumes the
prefill budget of every batch it joins, and one with long generations holds
cache blocks for minutes. Admission control per tenant, and the scheduling
policies of \Chref{scheduling}, keep one customer's traffic from setting
another's latency.

\section{Observability}

Four families of measurements describe a serving system, and all four are
needed. Request metrics --- first-token time, inter-token latency, end-to-end
latency and goodput, by percentile and by tenant --- say what users see. Queue
and cache metrics --- waiting requests, deferred requests, cache usage,
preemptions, prefix hit rate --- say why. Engine metrics --- batch sizes, tokens
per step, prefill and decode rates --- say where the time went. And correctness
metrics say whether the answers are still right: vLLM counts requests whose
logits contained NaNs, and continuous evaluations sample live traffic
(\Chref{fast-wrong-answers}). Traces that follow one request through router,
queue, prefill and decode join the four, and they are how a regression in one
tenant's p99 is traced to a change in another's traffic.

\section{Timeouts, retries and partial output}

A streamed response can fail halfway, and every layer must agree on what that
means. A client that disconnects should cancel its request so that its cache
blocks are freed and its batch slot reused (\Chref{request-lifecycle}); a
server that keeps generating for a closed connection spends capacity on nobody.
A slow reader is the opposite case: its unread tokens must not stall the others,
which is the defect \Chref{real-engines} found in Hermon's batched runtime. A
timeout must say whether the partial output is valid. And a retry of a
generation is not idempotent: it doubles the cost, returns a different sample
unless the seed is fixed, and under overload turns a queue into a storm, which
is why retries need budgets and backoff that respect the server's own signals.

\section{Rolling out a new model}

A new model, quantization, kernel or engine version changes answers, and
\Chref{fast-wrong-answers} shows that a single benchmark cannot tell how much.
Rollouts therefore run in stages: the new version is evaluated offline against
the old on distributions, not anecdotes; it receives shadow traffic whose
answers are compared but not returned; then a small share of real traffic, with
automatic rollback on latency or quality alarms. Anthropic's postmortem gives
the reason in its own terms: its evaluations had not captured the degradation
users reported, and one of the three bugs, a misconfiguration deployed to its TPU
servers on 25 August 2025, occasionally gave high probability to tokens that
should rarely appear --- Thai or Chinese characters in English answers --- until it
was rolled back on 2 September. It added tests for unexpected characters to its
deployment process \cite{anthropic2025postmortem}.

\section{Security: untrusted models and inputs}

A model file is code-adjacent. Weights stored with Python's pickle can run code
when loaded, which is why formats that store only tensors --- safetensors and
GGUF --- became the norm, and why vLLM will not run a model's own Python code from
a hub unless \texttt{trust\_remote\_code} is set; it defaults to false
(\texttt{vllm/config/model.py} at \texttt{bcdacfc}). Parsers of those formats
still read attacker-controlled lengths and offsets, and belong in the threat
model. Administrative endpoints are a second surface: vLLM's sleep and wake
endpoints require a development-mode variable and, in its developers' words,
should be exposed only in trusted networks \cite{vllm2025sleepmode}. Inputs are
the third: prompts can be crafted to exhaust a context window, to maximize
generation length, or to probe shared caches, and limits on each belong at the
edge.

\section{Cost control}

The unit of cost is the token served within its latency objective. A replica's
cost per million generated tokens is its price per hour divided by the tokens
it generates per hour, in millions. At \$2 per hour and 2,000 tokens per second,
that is $2 / 7.2 \approx \$0.28$ (illustrative values). Every lever in this book
moves the denominator: batching, quantization, speculation, prefix caching,
disaggregation. The opening moves it too. The M1's server generated 17 tokens
per second at 70\% of capacity and 25 when saturated: running it saturated buys
44\% more tokens per hour and multiplies the 99th-percentile wait by five to
seven. That trade is the cost decision in production: how much latency headroom
a service buys, and how much it pays for each unit.

\LedgerSection

What operating a fleet buys and spends:

\begin{ConstraintLedger}
Latency & buys & Headroom below the knee keeps tail latency near its unloaded value; routing to cached prefixes shortens prefill. \\
Cost & spends & Headroom is idle capacity: the rule of Equation~\ref{eq:headroom} keeps a fleet at 70\% or less. Sleep mode and fast loaders trade memory and complexity for faster scaling. \\
Correctness & buys & Staged rollouts, correctness metrics and routing that respects model configuration; the incidents in this chapter were routing and deployment faults, not kernel bugs. \\
Memory capacity & spends & Per-tenant cache isolation gives up sharing; sleeping models occupy host memory. \\
\end{ConstraintLedger}

\HappenedSection{incidents and their lessons}

The incidents in this chapter share a pattern: none began in a kernel. A load
balancer's routine change widened a routing bug from 0.8\% to 16\% of a model's
traffic; a deployment configuration corrupted sampling on some servers; an
approximate top-$k$ that failed only at some batch sizes surfaced when a
workaround was removed (\Chref{fast-wrong-answers}). All three were intermittent,
platform-specific and hard to reproduce, and evaluations run before deployment
missed them. Anthropic's lessons were more sensitive evaluations, run
continuously on production systems, and faster tools for debugging from
community reports \cite{anthropic2025postmortem}. For an engine, the lesson is
that correctness is a property of the whole path --- router, configuration,
batching, sampling --- and has to be measured there.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-26}

The serving stack above the engine is where most change is happening:
orchestration layers such as llm-d and NVIDIA Dynamo route by cache contents,
disaggregate prefill from decode and move key--value blocks between machines
(\Chref{disaggregated-serving}, \Chref{kv-storage-tier}); engines add sleep modes
and streaming loaders so that capacity follows traffic in seconds rather than
minutes. The open problems are autoscaling for agentic traffic, whose sessions
last minutes and whose value depends on keeping their caches warm; isolation
that does not give up sharing; and continuous measurement of correctness in
production, which the incidents above show is still largely manual.
