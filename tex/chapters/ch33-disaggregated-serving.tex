\chapter{Disaggregated Serving}\label{ch:disaggregated-serving}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
Prefill and decode interfere when they share a GPU, and want different
batch sizes and parallelism when they do not. Disaggregated serving runs them
on separate pools and moves each request's KV cache from one to the other.
This chapter measures the interference, prices the KV transfer against the
network, derives when disaggregation raises goodput and when it does not, and
surveys the scheduling and transfer machinery production systems use.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{Prefill here, decode there}

Disaggregation can be emulated on a laptop. One llama.cpp server prefills a
4,013-token prompt for Llama~3.2~3B and saves its slot's cache to a file ---
439~MiB. A second server, which has never seen the prompt, restores the file
and generates. In three runs the second server processed one token of the
prompt instead of 4,013, and its 64-token answer matched, character for
character, the answer a single server produced from scratch. Prefill took 16.1
to 17.1 seconds; saving and restoring the cache took 0.5 to 0.6 seconds, after
1.8 in the first run; the second server's first token came 0.07 seconds after
the restore (measured; record \texttt{2026-09-24-m1-part6}).

Nothing about a KV cache ties it to the device that computed it. It is data,
and it can be moved --- which is what lets a service compute prompts on one
pool of machines and generate on another, each sized and configured for its
own phase. This chapter prices that move against the prefill it replaces,
derives when separating the phases pays, and follows the systems that do it
over networks rather than through a file.

\Foundations{Handoff moves state and responsibility}{
Separating prefill from decode means the second worker must receive the state needed
to continue the same history. A tensor transfer alone is insufficient if its model
version, positions, layout or request ownership disagree. Transfer also introduces
queueing and a failure boundary: who owns a request if the sender finishes but the
receiver has not acknowledged it? Chapter~\ref{ch:kv-cache} defines the state, while
Chapter~\ref{ch:request-lifecycle} defines terminal ownership. Price the handoff
against both recomputation and local continuation before interpreting a faster prefill
worker as a faster service.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: KV transfer time against prefill time}

Prefill of $S$ tokens on hardware delivering $\pi$ FLOP/s at utilization $u$
costs about $2PS/(\pi u)$ seconds for a model of $P$ parameters; shipping the
resulting cache of $k$ bytes per token over a link of $\beta$ bytes per second
costs $Sk/\beta$. Their ratio,
\begin{equation}
\frac{t_{\text{transfer}}}{t_{\text{prefill}}} \approx \frac{k\,\pi\,u}{2\,P\,\beta},
\label{eq:pd-ratio}
\end{equation}
does not depend on the prompt's length, ignoring attention's own growth, which
only makes prefill costlier at long contexts. For Llama~3 70B on eight H100s
($k = 320$~KiB, $\pi = 8 \times 989$~TFLOP/s, $u = 0.5$) the cache takes 18\% of
the prefill time to cross one 400~Gb/s port and 2.3\% if each GPU sends its own
share over its own port; for Qwen3-8B on one H100 over one port, 9\% (derived).
An 8,192-token prompt of the 70B model makes 2.7~GB of cache, 54~ms on one
port, against 290~ms of prefill (derived). Moving the cache is cheaper than
making it, by a margin set by the model's cache bytes per FLOP --- which is why
the cache-shrinking designs of \Chref{shrinking-kv} and \Chref{hybrid-models}
make disaggregation easier, and why the transfer can be streamed layer by layer
while prefill continues.

\section{Interference, revisited}

\Chref{chunked-prefill} measured what a long prompt does to the requests
decoding beside it: every decode step that shares a batch with a prefill chunk
waits for it. Chunked prefill bounds the damage by capping each step's tokens,
but the two phases still compete for one budget, and the budget that is right
for time to first token is wrong for time per output token. vLLM's
documentation gives the same two reasons for its experimental disaggregated
mode --- tuning the two latencies separately, and controlling tail latency
between tokens --- and adds, emphatically, that disaggregated prefill ``DOES
NOT improve throughput'' (\texttt{docs/features/disagg\_prefill.md} at commit
\texttt{bcdacfc}). Disaggregation is a latency tool first.

\section{Separate pools}

Give prefill and decode their own devices and each can be configured for its
phase. Prefill is compute-bound (\Chref{prefill-decode}) and wants tensor or
context parallelism to finish long prompts quickly; decode is bandwidth- and
capacity-bound and wants large batches, wide expert parallelism and all the
memory it can get for cache. DistServe made this the design principle: assign
prefill and decoding to different GPUs, choose resources and parallelism per
phase, and place the phases with the cluster's bandwidth in mind; its authors
report serving 7.4 times more requests, or meeting 12.6 times tighter latency
objectives, with more than 90\% of requests within their constraints
(\cite{zhong2024distserve}; their measurement). Splitwise argued the same from
the hardware side, since the two phases stress different resources, and
reported 1.4 times the throughput at 20\% lower cost, or 2.35 times the
throughput at the same cost and power (\cite{patel2023splitwise}; their
measurement).

The pools need not even be the same hardware. A prefill pool wants arithmetic;
a decode pool wants memory bandwidth and capacity per dollar. Separating them
lets each be bought for what it does.

\section{Moving the KV cache}

Disaggregation lives or dies on the transfer. The prefill pool must hand each
request's cache to a decode device quickly, without stalling either, and the
decode side must receive it into its own paged layout (\Chref{paged-kv}).
Engines have converged on connectors: vLLM's documentation lists nine kinds of
KV connector, including NVIDIA's NIXL, Moonshot's Mooncake transfer engine and
LMCache (\texttt{docs/features/disagg\_prefill.md} at \texttt{bcdacfc}). NIXL
abstracts ``various types of memory (e.g., CPU and GPU) and storage'' behind one
point-to-point transfer interface \cite{nvidia2026nixl}, with plug-in backends
such as UCX for RDMA networks. Because a layer's cache is final as soon as that
layer finishes, the transfer can start before the prefill ends, which can hide
most of Equation~\ref{eq:pd-ratio}.

Correctness rides on the transfer too. The decode side must end up with the
cache the prefill side computed, for the same tokens, in the layout its kernels
expect; any mismatch in precision, block size or tokenization silently changes
the continuation. The opening's measurement checks the only thing that matters:
the text is the same.

\section{When disaggregation pays}

Disaggregation pays when its latency control is worth the pools' rigidity. It
wins when prompts are long enough that prefill would stall decoding, when the
latency objectives for the two phases differ, and when the traffic is large
enough to keep two pools busy. It loses when prompts are short (the transfer
and scheduling overhead is then a large share of a small prefill), when load is
small or bursty (a fixed split leaves one pool idle), and when prefix caching
already removes most prefill (\Chref{prefix-caching}). And it does not raise the
throughput a well-tuned colocated engine achieves, in vLLM's own assessment. Its
wins are in goodput --- requests served within their objectives --- which is
the metric DistServe optimized.

\section{Scheduling across pools}

With two pools, scheduling gains a dimension. A request must be routed to a
prefill instance, likely one that already holds part of its prefix in cache,
then to a decode instance with room for its cache to grow; and the ratio of
prefill to decode capacity must follow the traffic, because a reasoning
workload is decode-heavy (\Chref{reasoning-workloads}) where a
retrieval-augmented one is prefill-heavy. NVIDIA's Dynamo routes by worker load
and KV-cache overlap and resizes its pools with a planner \cite{nvidia2026dynamo};
llm-d offers prefix-cache-aware routing and prefill/decode disaggregation on
Kubernetes over vLLM \cite{llmd2026}; Mooncake's scheduler places requests to
maximize throughput within latency objectives \cite{qin2024mooncake}.

\section{Production architectures}

Two of the largest open-model services run this way. DeepSeek serves
V3 and R1 with prefill and decode on separate groups --- prefill units of 4
nodes and decode units of 18, with different expert-parallel widths in each
(\cite{deepseek2025inference}; \Chref{expert-parallelism}), and Moonshot's
Kimi runs on Mooncake, described below. SGLang's team
reproduced a comparable system on 96 H100 GPUs with prefill--decode
disaggregation and large-scale expert parallelism (\cite{sglang2025largeep};
the developers' measurement), and both NVIDIA's Dynamo and the llm-d project
package the pattern for others, each reporting large gains on its own
benchmarks (vendor claims, \cite{nvidia2026dynamo,llmd2026}).

\section{Failure and rebalancing}

Two pools double the ways to fail. A prefill instance can die after computing a
cache and before sending it; the request must be prefilled again elsewhere. A
decode instance can die holding hundreds of requests' caches; each must be
rebuilt, by recomputation or from a copy in a storage tier
(\Chref{kv-storage-tier}). And the balance between pools drifts with the
traffic, so a system must move capacity between them, which takes minutes if
it means loading models. Overload is the hardest case: accepting a request the
decode pool cannot absorb wastes the prefill spent on it, which is why Mooncake
rejects some requests at arrival, before prefill, on a prediction of decode
load (\cite{qin2024mooncake}; the case below).

The handoff is a state transfer, not just a byte copy. The destination must know what the bytes mean and when they are safe to read. The source must know when it may release its copy. The successful-path sequence makes those two obligations visible before failure handling is added.

\LedgerSection

Running prefill and decode on separate pools:

\begin{ConstraintLedger}
Latency & buys & Time to first token and time per output token tuned separately; decode no longer waits for prefill chunks. \\
Memory bandwidth & spends & Every request's cache crosses the network once: 2.7~GB for an 8,192-token prompt of Llama~3 70B, about a fifth of its prefill time on one 400~Gb/s port unless streamed or spread over ports. \\
Cost & risks & Two fixed pools idle when the traffic mix changes; throughput is not higher than a well-tuned colocated engine. \\
Correctness & risks & The decode side must receive exactly the cache prefill computed, in its own layout. \\
\end{ConstraintLedger}

\HappenedSection{a KV-centric serving platform}

Moonshot AI's Mooncake, which serves its Kimi assistant, is built around the
cache rather than around the model \cite{qin2024mooncake}. Prefill and decode
run in separate clusters, and the otherwise idle CPU memory and SSDs of the GPU
servers form a disaggregated cache of KV blocks that any instance can read, so
a prompt's prefix computed once can serve later requests anywhere. A
KV-centric scheduler balances throughput against latency objectives, and under
overload a prediction-based policy rejects requests early. The authors report
that it let Kimi handle 75\% more requests on real workloads, and ``up to a
525\% increase in throughput'' in simulated scenarios (their measurement). The
early rejection took two tries, a story \Chref{scheduling} tells in full:
rejecting on the decode pool's current load made the two pools' loads oscillate,
and only a prediction of decode load at the time a request would arrive there
settled it. Separating the phases had turned one feedback loop into two coupled
ones.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-24}

Disaggregation is spreading along both axes of this Part: expert-parallel
decode pools (\Chref{expert-parallelism}), encoder pools for images
(\Chref{multimodal}), and cache tiers shared by all of them
(\Chref{kv-storage-tier}). The machinery has become a product category ---
transfer libraries, KV-aware routers, planners that resize pools --- and the
open problems have moved from whether to split the phases to how to keep the
pools balanced as the mix of chat, retrieval, agents and reasoning shifts under
them.

\item \emph{Prediction.} For a decode pool of 8 H100s serving Qwen3-8B with

  the incoming caches need.
\item \emph{Implementation.} Stream the cache layer by layer in the lab and
  measure how much of the transfer the prefill hides.
\item \emph{Falsification.} Find a traffic mix for which a colocated engine
  with chunked prefill beats disaggregation on goodput at the same GPU count.
\end{enumerate}
