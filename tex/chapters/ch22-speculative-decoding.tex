\chapter{Speculative Decoding: The Draft--Verify Contract}\label{ch:speculative-decoding}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
Decode leaves compute idle, so verifying several guessed tokens in one
forward pass costs about as much as generating one. Speculative decoding
exploits this: a cheap drafter proposes tokens, the target model checks them in
parallel, and a modified rejection rule accepts them so that the output
distribution is exactly the target's. This chapter proves that exactness,
derives the expected speedup from the acceptance rate and the draft cost, and
builds drafting from n-grams, the prompt and a smaller model, including rolling
back the KV cache after a rejection.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{Several tokens for the price of one step}

Ask Llama~3.2~3B, through llama.cpp's server on the M1, to repeat a
218-word passage from its prompt. Without speculation it produced the 245
tokens of its answer at 15 to 16 tokens per second, one forward pass per token.
With prompt-lookup speculation --- drafts copied from the prompt wherever the
last dozen tokens of output match it --- the drafter proposed 224 tokens and
the model accepted every one. The answer took about 21 forward passes instead
of 245, arrived at 60 to 65 tokens per second, \textbf{3.7 to 4.3 times faster},
and was identical, character for character, to the answer without speculation
in all three runs (measured; record \texttt{2026-09-24-m1-part4}).

Change the task and the gain changes with it. Asked to rewrite the passage
with one word replaced, the model accepted 193 of 208 drafts and ran 2.5 to 3.1
times faster. Asked to summarize the passage, or to write a story, it produced
no run of a dozen tokens that the prompt contained, the drafter proposed
nothing, and the speed was unchanged. Across ten greedy prompts, the outputs
with and without speculation were identical wherever both ran; the one
difference was a speculative run that ended 45 characters early, as expected
from a server that counts a pass's accepted drafts against the length limit
before emitting them (\texttt{tools/server/server-context.cpp} at commit
\texttt{d006858}).

Eleven or twelve tokens per forward pass, then, but a four-fold speedup.
The gap is the price of verification: each pass checked 17 tokens, which on
this laptop costs about 2.3 ordinary steps. This chapter derives the contract
that made those outputs identical, the arithmetic that predicts the speedup,
and the drafters that make it possible.

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: acceptance rate and expected speedup}

Suppose a drafter proposes $\gamma$ tokens and the target accepts each one,
independently, with probability $\alpha$, stopping at the first rejection.
Every verification pass also yields one token from the target itself --- the
correction after a rejection, or a bonus token after a full acceptance --- so
the expected number of tokens per pass is
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per pass}] = \frac{1-\alpha^{\gamma+1}}{1-\alpha},
\label{eq:spec-tokens}
\end{equation}
Leviathan and colleagues' equation \cite{leviathan2023speculative}. If a
drafted token costs $c$ target steps and a verification pass costs $v$ target
steps, the speedup over ordinary decoding is
\begin{equation}
S = \frac{1-\alpha^{\gamma+1}}{(1-\alpha)\,(\gamma c + v)}.
\label{eq:spec-speedup}
\end{equation}
With $v = 1$ this is their walltime improvement factor. That assumption ---
verifying $\gamma+1$ tokens costs one step --- holds where decode leaves the
arithmetic idle (\Chref{roofline}). On the M1 it does not quite: processing 16
tokens at once cost 2.3 single-token steps for Llama~3.2~3B and 2.7 for an
8-billion-parameter model (measured with \texttt{llama-bench}; record
\texttt{2026-09-24-m1-part4}; Figure~\ref{fig:ch22-verify-cost}).

\EngineFigure{ch22-verify-cost}{The time of one forward pass over $k$ tokens

With $\alpha = 0.8$, $\gamma = 4$ and a free
drafter, equation~\eqref{eq:spec-tokens} gives 3.4 tokens per pass; at $v = 1$
that is a 3.4-fold speedup, and at $v = 1.6$, the M1's measured cost of a
five-token pass for the 3B model, about 2.1 (derived).

\section{Verification is cheap at decode}

A decode step reads every weight to produce one token per sequence
(\Chref{roofline}). Feeding the model $k$ tokens of the same sequence instead ---
the last accepted token followed by $k-1$ guesses --- reads the same weights
once and produces $k$ sets of logits, one per position, exactly as a short
prefill does. Where decode is bandwidth-bound, the extra arithmetic fills
capacity that was idle, and $k$ tokens cost nearly the same as one. The cache
reads grow by the $k-1$ extra positions' attention, a small addition at short
context. Verification is therefore nearly free on hardware with idle arithmetic
and becomes expensive exactly when the arithmetic is already in use ---
\Chref{when-speculation-loses}.

\section{Draft, verify, accept}

The loop has three steps. The drafter proposes $\gamma$ tokens
$x_1,\dots,x_\gamma$ continuing the accepted text. The target runs once on the
last accepted token and the $\gamma$ drafts, producing distributions
$p_1,\dots,p_{\gamma+1}$ for every position. The engine then walks the drafts
in order, accepting each by the rule of the next section; at the first
rejection it replaces the rejected token with one drawn from the target and
discards the rest. If every draft is accepted, $p_{\gamma+1}$ supplies one more
token for free. Each pass therefore emits between one and $\gamma+1$ tokens, and
never fewer than ordinary decoding would.

\section{The acceptance rule that preserves the distribution}

Let the drafter sample $x$ from its distribution $q$ and let the target's
distribution at that position be $p$. Accept $x$ with probability
$\min\!\bigl(1, p(x)/q(x)\bigr)$; on rejection, sample a replacement from the
residual distribution
\begin{equation}
p'(y) = \frac{\max\bigl(0,\,p(y)-q(y)\bigr)}{\sum_{z}\max\bigl(0,\,p(z)-q(z)\bigr)}.
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
The emitted token then has exactly the distribution $p$
\cite{leviathan2023speculative,chen2023speculative}. The proof is two lines.
Let $\beta = \sum_y \min(p(y), q(y))$ be the probability of accepting. The
emitted token is $y$ either because $y$ was drafted and accepted, with
probability $q(y)\min(1, p(y)/q(y)) = \min(p(y), q(y))$, or because a
rejection occurred and $y$ was resampled, with probability $(1-\beta)\,p'(y) =
p(y) - \min(p(y), q(y))$. The two add to $p(y)$. Applied position by
position, the whole output is distributed exactly as the target would have
generated it, however poor the drafter: a bad drafter costs speed, never
correctness.

Two special cases matter in practice. Under greedy decoding, a draft is
accepted if and only if it equals the target's most likely token. And when the
drafter is deterministic --- a lookup table, or a small model taking its own
most likely token --- $q$ puts all its mass on $x$, and the rule reduces to
\emph{sample from $p$ and compare}: accept if the target's sample equals $x$.
That is what llama.cpp's server does at every drafted position
(\texttt{common\_sampler\_sample\_and\_accept\_n} in \texttt{common/sampling.cpp}
at commit \texttt{d006858}); since its drafts are deterministic proposals, the
rule is exact.

\section{Expected speedup}

Equation~\eqref{eq:spec-speedup} has three levers. Acceptance $\alpha$ depends
on how well the drafter predicts the target on this text; it is high where the
output is predictable and low where the model is choosing freely. Draft length
$\gamma$ has an optimum: each extra draft adds cost $c$ and helps only if all
drafts before it were accepted, so the marginal draft is worth $\alpha^{\gamma}$
tokens. And the costs $c$ and $v$ belong to the hardware and the batch. Real
acceptance is not independent from token to token: inside a quoted passage
every draft is right, inside a fresh sentence most are wrong, and engines adapt
$\gamma$ to the recent acceptance for that reason
(\Chref{when-speculation-loses}).

\section{Drafts from n-grams and the prompt}

The cheapest drafter is the text already present. Prompt lookup searches the
prompt and the output so far for the most recent earlier occurrence of the last
few tokens and proposes what followed them. llama.cpp's simple n-gram drafter
matches the last 12 tokens and proposes up to 48 that followed, capped by the
server's draft limit of 16 (\texttt{common/ngram-map.cpp} and the server's
\texttt{--spec-type} option at commit \texttt{d006858}); vLLM offers the same
idea with configurable match lengths \cite{vllm2026specdecode}. Its cost is a
search, $c \approx 0$, and its acceptance is high exactly when the output
repeats the context: quoting, editing, code that reuses identifiers, structured
data that echoes a schema. SuffixDecoding extends the table to earlier requests'
outputs, which pays off in agent loops that repeat themselves; its authors
reported up to 5.3 times on agentic benchmarks \cite{oliaro2025suffixdecoding}.
Lookahead decoding needs no drafter at all: it generates n-grams from Jacobi
iterations of the model itself and verifies them in the same pass, reporting up
to 1.8 times on MT-bench \cite{fu2024lookahead} (their measurements).

\section{Drafts from a smaller model}

A smaller model of the same family, sharing the tokenizer, predicts the target
well on ordinary text. Its cost $c$ is its step time over the target's: on the
M1, a 3-billion-parameter draft for an 8-billion-parameter target measured
0.38, close to the ratio of their bytes. That is expensive:
equation~\eqref{eq:spec-speedup} with $c = 0.38$ and $\gamma = 4$ needs high
acceptance just to break even. Drafters are therefore made much smaller than
their targets, or built from the target itself (\Chref{modern-speculation}).
llama.cpp adds two refinements. It stops drafting when the drafter's own
probability for its next token falls below a threshold, 0.75 by default, so
that uncertain drafts are not paid for; and it can pair a drafter and target
whose vocabularies differ by translating tokens through text, at extra cost
(server options at commit \texttt{d006858}).

\section{Rolling back the cache}

Verification writes the cache entries of every drafted position. After a
rejection, the entries past the accepted prefix are wrong --- they encode tokens
that will never be part of the output --- and must be discarded before the next
pass. With a contiguous or paged cache this is a truncation of the sequence's
length and costs nothing; llama.cpp's server keeps the accepted tokens and cuts
the rest from the slot's token list before the next step
(\texttt{tools/server/server-context.cpp} at commit \texttt{d006858}). The
drafter's own cache must be rolled back the same way. Models whose state is not
a list of per-token entries --- recurrent and state-space layers
(\Chref{hybrid-models}) --- cannot truncate: they must checkpoint their state
before a speculative pass and restore it after a rejection.

\LedgerSection

Drafting cheaply and verifying $\gamma$ tokens per target pass:

\begin{ConstraintLedger}
Latency & buys & Up to $\gamma+1$ tokens per target pass (equation~\eqref{eq:spec-tokens}); several-fold where the output is predictable. \\
Compute & spends & The drafter's work, and the target's work on rejected drafts: $\gamma c + v$ steps per pass (equation~\eqref{eq:spec-speedup}). \\
Memory capacity & spends & A drafter's weights and cache; cache entries for drafts that will be discarded. \\
Correctness & risks & Exact by construction; batched verification changes floating-point order, so greedy outputs can differ in rare places. \\
\end{ConstraintLedger}

\HappenedSection{prompt lookup on retrieval workloads}

Hermon added prompt-lookup speculation to its batched runtime and measured it
where it should win: on an Apple M3 Pro, Llama-3-3B quantized to four bits,
four concurrent requests asking the model to quote a passage from their
prompts. Speculation cut the decode steps from 64 to 40 and raised aggregate
throughput from 51.3 to 68.1 tokens per second, 1.33 times, with 96 of 160
drafted tokens accepted (Hermon \texttt{docs/PERFORMANCE.md}, 2026-05-12; the
developers' measurement). Their notes list where they expected it to win ---
retrieval, code completion, summarization, tool calls that echo their inputs
--- and where not: open-ended writing, arithmetic, translation, whose tokens
are new. The feature is off unless two environment variables set its n-gram
and draft lengths (\texttt{crates/hermon-runtime/src/batched.rs} at commit
\texttt{2a3fd52}; PREVIEW). Measured on a second machine and workload, the same
feature gained only 1.09 times when prefill dominated (\Chref{prefill-decode})
and lost 6\% at higher concurrency (\Chref{when-speculation-loses}): the
technique was the same; the ledger was not.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-24}

Speculative decoding is standard in every major engine, and the menu of
drafters keeps growing: vLLM ships model-based drafters, n-gram and suffix
lookup \cite{vllm2026specdecode}; SGLang ships tree-based EAGLE drafting and
n-gram lookup \cite{sglang2026specdecode}; llama.cpp ships draft models and
five kinds of n-gram lookup. Model-free drafting is being extended to the
repetitive traffic of agents \cite{oliaro2025suffixdecoding}. The drafters
built from the target itself are \Chref{modern-speculation}'s subject; when
speculation stops paying is \Chref{when-speculation-loses}'s.

\ExercisesSection

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Derivation.} Show that under greedy decoding the rule of
equation~\eqref{eq:residual} accepts a draft exactly when it equals the
target's most likely token, and find the probability of accepting when both
distributions are uniform over $n$ tokens.
\item \emph{Prediction.} Using equation~\eqref{eq:spec-speedup}, find the
draft length $\gamma$ that maximizes speedup for $\alpha = 0.6$, $c = 0.05$ and
$v = 1$, then for $v = 1 + 0.1\gamma$.
\item \emph{Implementation.} Add a smaller-model drafter to the lab and measure
its cost ratio $c$ on your machine.
\item \emph{Falsification.} Find a drafter and text for which speculation with
exact verification is slower than ordinary decoding on your machine. Which term
of equation~\eqref{eq:spec-speedup} is responsible?
\end{enumerate}
