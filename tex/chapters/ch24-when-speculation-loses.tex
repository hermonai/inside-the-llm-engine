\chapter{When Speculation Loses}\label{ch:when-speculation-loses}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
Speculation spends idle compute to buy latency. When there is no idle
compute --- at large batch, on compute-bound hardware, or when acceptance is
low because of temperature or domain --- the same technique makes a system
slower. This chapter builds a break-even model from the roofline and the
acceptance rate, measures cases on both sides of it, and covers the
interactions with chunked prefill, structured output and sampling settings that
production engines must handle.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{Slower with speculation}

The drafter of \Chref{modern-speculation}'s opening --- a 3-billion-parameter
model speculating for an 8-billion-parameter target on the M1 --- did more than
disappoint. Without speculation, the target decoded at 9.8 to 10.6 tokens per
second; with the drafter, at \textbf{4.3 to 5.6}. Speculation made decoding
about twice as slow, with 46 to 47\% of the drafts accepted (measured, two runs
of two workloads each, alternating with the baseline; record
\texttt{2026-09-24-m1-part4}).

Even a drafter that is right every time pays less as the machine gets busier.
Prompt lookup, repeating a passage for Llama~3.2~3B, accepted all 896 of its
drafted tokens whether one request ran or four. For a single request it was
4.1 times faster than decoding without speculation on the same servers; with
four requests at once, 2.0 times, in three runs. The drafts had not changed. What
changed was the verification pass: four sequences with 16 drafts each put 68
tokens into every step instead of 4, on a machine whose arithmetic runs out at
about a dozen.

Both results follow from one inequality: speculation pays while a verification
pass costs less than the tokens it accepts. This chapter builds that inequality
from the roofline, finds where each measured case sits on it, and lists the
ways a production engine can end up on the wrong side.

\Foundations{Saved iterations must pay for extra work}{
Speculation's useful output is the number of committed tokens per verification cycle.
Its cost includes drafting, verification, state management and any lost batching
efficiency. A larger acceptance fraction is not sufficient if the draft or
verification becomes much slower. Compare against the best ordinary decode
configuration at the same workload and latency target. Chapter~\ref{ch:roofline}
explains when spare compute exists; Chapter~\ref{ch:speculative-decoding} explains
what can be accepted. Chapter~\ref{app:reproducing} keeps the denominator honest by
counting only useful output, not discarded proposals.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: the break-even batch size}

Let a step that processes $n$ tokens take $t(n)$. Below the roofline's ridge
(\Chref{roofline}) the step is bandwidth-bound and $t(n)$ barely grows with $n$;
above it, $t(n)$ grows in proportion to $n$. Without speculation, a batch of
$B$ sequences produces $B$ tokens per step of cost $t(B)$. With a free drafter,
$\gamma$ drafts per sequence and $\tau$ accepted tokens per sequence per pass,
it produces $B\tau$ tokens per pass of cost $t\bigl(B(\gamma+1)\bigr)$.
Speculation pays while
\begin{equation}
\frac{t\bigl(B(\gamma+1)\bigr)}{t(B)} < \tau .
\label{eq:spec-breakeven}
\end{equation}
While $B(\gamma+1)$ stays below the ridge's token count $n^{*}$ the left side is
near one and any $\tau > 1$ wins. Once $B$ itself is above the ridge the left
side approaches $\gamma+1$, which exceeds $\tau$ for any imperfect drafter:
every rejected draft is arithmetic taken from another sequence. The break-even
batch is therefore roughly $B_{\text{be}} \approx n^{*}/(\gamma+1)$.

The ridge is where the hardware decides. For 16-bit weights, each token in a
step does about one FLOP per byte of weights, so $n^{*}$ is about the ridge
intensity itself: some 300 tokens on an H100 in BF16, which with $\gamma = 4$
puts the break-even near 60 sequences (derived). On the M1, with a ridge of 38
FLOP per byte and the 5.0-bit average weights of its quantized Llama~3.2~3B
(\Chref{weight-quantization}), $n^{*}$ is about 12 tokens and the break-even
is two or three sequences (derived). The measured cost of verifying
on the M1 --- 2.3 single-token steps for 16 tokens of Llama~3.2~3B --- is this
arithmetic showing through.

\section{The break-even model}

Equation~\eqref{eq:spec-breakeven} with a drafter's cost added becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{\gamma\,c\,t(B) + t\bigl(B(\gamma+1)\bigr)}{t(B)} < \tau ,
\end{equation}
and every term moves with the workload. $\tau$ depends on the text and the
sampling settings; $c$ on the drafter and the hardware; the ratio of step times
on the batch and the context length. A speculation policy fixed at deployment
is therefore wrong somewhere in every day's traffic, and the engines that
handle it well measure acceptance and load continuously and adjust $\gamma$ ---
down to zero --- as they change. TurboSpec, implemented in vLLM, closes this
loop by predicting goodput for each speculation setting and choosing the best,
after its authors found that speculation added without tuning could degrade
serving \cite{liu2024turbospec}.

\section{Large batches: no idle compute}

Continuous batching (\Chref{continuous-batching}) and speculation compete for
the same idle arithmetic. At low concurrency the GPU waits on memory and
speculation fills the wait with verification; at high concurrency the batch
fills it with other sequences, and every drafted token that is rejected is work
taken from a request that would have used it. P-EAGLE's measurements trace the
curve: 1.55 to 1.69 times faster than EAGLE-3 at a concurrency of one, 1.05 to
1.23 times at 64 \cite{vllm2026peagle}. Long contexts move the ridge. When each
sequence reads a long cache, attention's bytes dominate the step and the step
stays bandwidth-bound at larger batches; MagicDec used a drafter with a sparse
cache to exploit this and reported up to 2.51 times at batch sizes from 32 to
256 \cite{sadhukhan2024magicdec} (their measurement).

\section{Low acceptance: temperature and domain}

Acceptance is a property of the text. Where the output copies its context,
drafts from the context are almost always right; where the model composes new
text, they are almost never proposed or almost always wrong. Temperature lowers
acceptance for every drafter: the target's sample at each position must equal
the draft, and a flatter distribution makes that less likely even when the
draft is the most probable token. How much depends on how certain the model
is. On the M1, raising the temperature from 0 to 0.8 barely moved prompt
lookup's acceptance on copying tasks --- all 672 drafts accepted when
repeating the passage, 571 of 624 against 579 of 624 when editing it ---
because the next word of a quotation is nearly a certainty for the model. The
losses come where the model is choosing: a separate drafter predicting a
reasoning model's thoughts was accepted less than half the time even under
greedy decoding.

\section{Draft cost and memory}

A separate drafter costs time and memory. Its time is the $\gamma c$ term; its
memory is its weights and its own cache, taken from the batch's cache budget
(\Chref{kv-cache}). On the M1 the 3-billion-parameter drafter cost 0.38 of a
target step per drafted token and added 1.9~GB of weights to the target's
5.2~GB, 37\% more, plus its own cache; with up to 16 drafts per pass, a
rejected draft chain can cost several target steps. A drafter is worth its
memory only if the
speculation it enables saves more than the batch that memory would otherwise
have held.

\section{Interactions with the rest of the engine}

Speculation touches every other mechanism in this part of the book. Drafted
tokens count against the step's token budget, so they compete with prompt
chunks (\Chref{chunked-prefill}). Constrained decoding must apply the grammar
to every verified position and stop at the first invalid draft
(\Chref{structured-output}); llama.cpp's verification samples each position
through the full sampler chain, grammar included, for that reason
(\texttt{common/sampling.cpp} at commit \texttt{d006858}). Repetition penalties
and logit biases must see exactly the tokens they would have seen without
speculation. Rolled-back cache entries must never be shared through a prefix
cache (\Chref{prefix-caching}). And streaming changes shape: tokens arrive in
bursts of up to $\gamma+1$, so time per output token improves while the gap
before each burst grows, which matters to an interface that renders token by
token.

\section{Measuring speculation honestly}

A speedup from speculation is meaningful only with its conditions: the
acceptance rate and draft length; the concurrency and the temperature; the
workload's share of decode against prefill (\Chref{prefill-decode}); and a
check that the outputs are the ones the target would have produced. Report the
distribution of time per output token, not only its mean, because speculation
makes it bimodal. Measure at the concurrency the service runs at, not at one.
And compare like with like: two runs that produce different text are two
different workloads (\Chref{benchmarking}).

\LedgerSection

Speculating at a given batch size, temperature and drafter:

\begin{ConstraintLedger}
Latency & buys & Tokens per pass above one, while the verification pass stays below the ridge (equation~\eqref{eq:spec-breakeven}). \\
Compute & spends & Every rejected draft; at batch sizes past $n^{*}/(\gamma+1)$, arithmetic taken from other sequences. \\
Memory capacity & spends & A drafter's weights and cache, taken from the batch. \\
Latency & risks & Burstier streams, and slower requests when acceptance or idle compute falls. \\
\end{ConstraintLedger}

\HappenedSection{a speedup that vanished outside its workload}

Hermon's prompt-lookup speculation gained 1.33 times on four concurrent quote
requests on an Apple M3 Pro (\Chref{speculative-decoding}). Its developers then
ran it on a Linux machine with two GPUs, Qwen2.5-1.5B quantized to four bits,
eight concurrent requests, each asked to write a 250-word essay quoting a
passage several times. The drafts were good: 1,443 of 2,624 accepted, 55\%. The
run was \textbf{6\% slower}: 133.4 tokens per second against 141.3 without
speculation (Hermon \texttt{docs/PERFORMANCE.md}, 2026-05-12; the developers'
measurement). Their explanation is this chapter's napkin math. With eight
sequences and five drafts each, a fully speculating step processed 48 tokens
instead of 8; for a small model on fast GPUs each forward pass was cheap, so
fattening it cost more than the accepted tokens saved. They wrote the rule down
--- turn speculation on for larger models at modest concurrency on text that
quotes its context, and measure before turning it on elsewhere --- and left it
off by default.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-24}

Engines are turning speculation from a flag into a controller: TurboSpec's
closed loop in vLLM chooses the speculation setting by predicted goodput
\cite{liu2024turbospec}; drafters that cost less per pass push the break-even
batch outward \cite{vllm2026peagle}; and long-context serving, where the cache
keeps steps bandwidth-bound, extends speculation to large batches
\cite{sadhukhan2024magicdec}. The break-even arithmetic will keep moving as
hardware ridges rise: each generation that raises arithmetic faster than
bandwidth makes idle compute more plentiful, and speculation more valuable.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Derivation.} With $t(n) = \max(t_0, a\,n)$, derive the break-even
batch size as a function of $\gamma$, $\tau$, $t_0$ and $a$.
\item \emph{Prediction.} For an H100 serving an 8-billion-parameter model in
FP8, estimate $n^{*}$ and the break-even batch at $\gamma = 4$.
\item \emph{Implementation.} Add an adaptive draft length to the lab of
\Chref{speculative-decoding} that shrinks $\gamma$ when recent acceptance
falls, and compare it with a fixed $\gamma$ across a workload that alternates
between quoting and free writing.
\item \emph{Falsification.} Find a concurrency at which speculation helps on
your machine at temperature 0 but hurts at temperature 1.
\end{enumerate}
