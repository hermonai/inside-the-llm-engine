\chapter{Why Inference Is Its Own Discipline}\label{ch:why-inference}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
Part I showed how to compute a token. Now imagine several people waiting for that
computation at once. We need to distinguish how fast one answer advances from how much
total work the machine completes. The chapter introduces those two rates, follows one
real prediction, and explains why serving a model is a separate engineering problem.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\section*{From a model to a service}
If one user receives 20 tokens each second, thirty-two users do not automatically
receive 640 in total. They share a machine. Conversely, grouping their work can reuse
the same weights and raise total output while slowing each conversation. Read the first
chart as two different views of the same run: the operator's total output and one
person's waiting time. The later model-configuration survey is a preview; you do not
need to memorize its architecture names.

\OpeningSection{Thirty-two conversations for less than the price of four}

On a 2020 laptop with Apple's M1 chip, llama.cpp generates text with
Llama~3.2~3B at about 20 tokens per second for one conversation. Ask it to
continue thirty-two conversations at once, each with its own history, and the
machine produces \textbf{176 tokens per second} in total --- 8.8 times as many
--- while each step of the computation takes only 3.7 times as long (measured;
the record is \texttt{research/measurements/2026-09-23-m1-llama32-3b.md}).

The model definition and weights did not change. What changed is how the
engine grouped the work. Grouping can change floating-point reduction order,
so output equivalence still needs testing; it is not guaranteed by unchanged
weights. At
one conversation, every step streams all two gigabytes of weights from memory
to produce a single token. At thirty-two, the same stream of weights produces
thirty-two tokens, one per conversation. The cost of a token fell by almost a
factor of nine, and the price was paid by each user in waiting time: each
conversation now advances at 5.5 tokens per second instead of 20.

\EngineFigure{ch01-batching}{Llama~3.2~3B on the M1's GPU, decoding $B$
conversations at once (measured). The solid line is the machine's total output;
the dashed line is what each conversation sees. The total rises in steps rather
than smoothly: from one to three conversations it barely moves, at four it
doubles, and past eight it climbs steadily. The steps are llama.cpp choosing a
different matrix kernel for each range of batch rows, which
\Chref{fast-wrong-answers} traces to source; the climb is the same stream of
weights shared by more conversations.}{fig:ch01-batching}

The curve in Figure~\ref{fig:ch01-batching} is not smooth, and its steps are
the first hint of how much an engine's software decides. From one to three
conversations the total barely rises, because the kernel llama.cpp uses for so
few rows reads the weights once per row; at four it switches to a kernel that
shares each load among the rows, and the total doubles. None of that is in the
model. That trade --- throughput for the operator against latency for the
user, bought with memory for thirty-two histories and shaped by kernels the
user never sees --- has no counterpart in how a model is trained or in how its
architecture is usually explained. It is the first example of the kind of
decision this book is about. Chapter by chapter, we will find that almost every
technique in a modern inference engine is a trade of this shape: one scarce
resource spent to relieve another, for users who are waiting.

\Foundations{Model, algorithm, engine}{
A language model assigns probabilities to the next token given a token history. A
token is an integer vocabulary entry, not necessarily a word. A decoding algorithm
turns those probabilities into a choice; an engine arranges the computation and owns
its memory. Training changes the weights. Ordinary inference changes the history and
request state while keeping the weights fixed. Consequently, two engines can implement
the same model while having different latency, memory use and failure behaviour. Keep
these three objects separate when reading a speed comparison.
Part~\ref{part:foundations} builds the numerical computation; this chapter begins
the resource accounting.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: training pays once, inference pays per token}

Training a model with $N$ active parameters on $D$ tokens costs about $6ND$
floating-point operations: two per parameter per token in the forward pass and
four in the backward pass (\cite{kaplan2020scaling}, section~1.3). Processing
one token in service --- a prompt token or a generated one --- costs about
$2N$: the forward pass alone. So the compute a model spends in service equals
the compute spent training it after about $3D$ processed tokens.

That break-even arrives. DeepSeek-V3 activates 37~billion parameters per token
and was trained on 14.8~trillion tokens \cite{deepseek2024v3}: about
$3.3\times10^{24}$ FLOPs (derived), matched in service after about 44~trillion
processed tokens. DeepSeek reported that its V3 and R1 service, in one 24-hour
period in February 2025, took 608~billion input tokens --- 56\% of them served
from a cache on disk rather than computed --- and generated 168~billion
\cite{deepseek2025inference}. That is about 430~billion computed tokens a day:
the training run's compute, spent again roughly every hundred days (derived;
the service mixes V3 and R1, which share an architecture).

The comparison of FLOPs understates the difference, because the two workloads
spend their FLOPs differently. Training processes enormous batches of known
tokens, runs at a large fraction of peak compute, and is scheduled once. A
decode step processes one unknown token per conversation, runs at a few
percent of peak compute (\Chref{roofline}), must finish within tens of
milliseconds, and is scheduled billions of times against arrivals no one
controls. The operation is the same; the engineering problem is not.

The opening's laptop shows the second half of that sentence in three lines of
arithmetic. A decode step of Llama~3.2~3B reads its 2.01~GB of weights and
performs about 6.43~billion floating-point operations, two per parameter. The
M1's GPU can read memory at 46--50~GB/s in a streaming test and can compute at
2.6~TFLOP/s by Apple's specification. Reading the weights therefore takes at
least $2.01 / 48 \approx 42$~ms, and the arithmetic at least $6.43 / 2{,}600
\approx 2.5$~ms (derived). The arithmetic units are busy for about one
twentieth of each step. Measured, a step took 49.8~ms, 20 tokens per second,
which is the memory term plus overheads. The idle nineteen twentieths are what
the opening's thirty-two conversations were spending: each extra conversation
adds another 2.5~ms of arithmetic and a small cache read to a step that was
waiting on memory anyway, until the arithmetic catches up with the memory.
\Chref{roofline} turns this into the roofline model; here it is enough to
notice that the cost of a token is set by bytes, not by FLOPs, until many
conversations share the bytes.

\section{One token, from text to text}

Before looking at engines, follow one token all the way through a real model,
with the numbers attached. The model is the one from the opening: Llama~3.2~3B,
quantized to about four bits per weight, with 28 decoder layers of width 3,072,
24 query heads and 8 key/value heads of width 128, a feed-forward width of
8,192, and a vocabulary of 128,256 tokens whose embedding table is shared with
the output layer. The prompt is \texttt{"The capital of France is"}. Every
shape below comes from the model file; the token IDs and probabilities were
measured on the M1 (record \texttt{2026-09-26-m1-pass3};
Figure~\ref{fig:ch01-one-token}).

\EngineFigure{ch01-one-token}{One token of Llama~3.2~3B, from the prompt's text
to the next word's bytes. The first step processes all six prompt tokens at
once; every later step processes one. Shapes and bytes are derived from the
model file, token IDs and probabilities measured; the dashed arrow is the loop
that makes generation sequential.}{fig:ch01-one-token}

\paragraph{Text to tokens.} The tokenizer turns the 24 bytes of the prompt
into six integers: \texttt{128000} (a special marker for the beginning of a
text), then \texttt{791} for \texttt{The}, \texttt{6864} for \texttt{\ capital},
\texttt{315} for \texttt{\ of}, \texttt{9822} for \texttt{\ France} and
\texttt{374} for \texttt{\ is}. The spaces belong to the tokens that follow
them. The model never sees characters; it sees these six numbers, and
everything the engine later does is indexed by them (Part~\ref{part:foundations}
builds a tokenizer from scratch).

\paragraph{Tokens to vectors.} Each ID selects one row of the embedding
table, a matrix of $128{,}256 \times 3{,}072$ numbers. The six rows form
$\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 3072}$. In this file the table is stored in
a six-bit format, 2,520 bytes per row, so the lookup reads 15~KB of a 323~MB
table: the cheapest step of the whole pass.

\paragraph{Twenty-eight layers.} Each decoder layer applies the same recipe
with its own weights. It normalizes each row of $\mathbf{X}$, projects it into
queries ($6 \times 3072$, 24 heads of 128), keys and values ($6 \times 1024$
each, 8 heads of 128), lets every position attend to itself and the positions
before it, projects the result back to width 3,072, normalizes again, and runs
a feed-forward network that widens each row to 8,192 and narrows it back. Each
step's output is added to its input, so $\mathbf{X}$ keeps its shape through
all 28 layers. A layer holds 100.7~million weights; the 28 hold 2.82~billion.
Along the way each layer writes the keys and values of all six positions into
the \Term{KV cache}, 112~KiB per position across the model --- 672~KiB for this
prompt --- so that later steps can attend to them without recomputing them
(\Chref{kv-cache}).

\paragraph{The last position becomes a distribution.} Only the last row of
$\mathbf{X}$ is needed to predict the next token; the other five were computed
so their keys and values could be cached. That row is normalized once more and
multiplied by the output matrix --- the same 394~million numbers as the
embedding table --- giving 128,256 scores called \Term{logits}, one per
vocabulary entry, 513~KB in 32-bit floats. The softmax turns them into
probabilities. For this prompt the five most likely continuations were:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
Token & ID & Log-probability & Probability \\
\midrule
\texttt{" Paris"} & 12366 & $-0.485$ & 0.616 \\
\texttt{" located"} & 7559 & $-2.366$ & 0.094 \\
\texttt{" not"} & 539 & $-3.309$ & 0.037 \\
\texttt{"..."} & 1131 & $-3.325$ & 0.036 \\
\texttt{" a"} & 264 & $-3.428$ & 0.032 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The model is far from certain: almost four chances in ten go to
something other than \texttt{" Paris"}. Which token is emitted is the
sampler's decision, not the model's (\Appref{sampling}). A greedy sampler takes
the most likely, token 12366.

\paragraph{Token to bytes.} Token 12366 decodes to six bytes, hexadecimal
\texttt{20 50 61 72 69 73}: a space and \texttt{Paris}. The engine sends them
to the user as soon as they form complete UTF-8 characters; a token may end in
the middle of a multi-byte character, and the engine must hold such bytes back
until the character is complete (\Chref{request-lifecycle}).

\paragraph{And again.} The next step appends token 12366 and processes only
that one new token. Its keys and values join the cache; its query attends to
all seven cached positions; its last row becomes the next distribution. The
arithmetic of that step is small --- one row instead of six --- but every weight
of every layer is read again, 2.01~GB, to produce it. Greedy decoding continued
the prompt as \texttt{" Paris. The capital of Italy is Rome"}: eight tokens at
19.8 per second, 50~ms each, after a 146~ms first step for the six prompt
tokens (measured). The first step processed six tokens in three times the time
of a later step that processed one: the prompt is processed in parallel, the
answer one token at a time. \Chref{prefill-decode} makes those two phases the
subject of their own chapter, and Part~\ref{part:foundations} builds every
operation above from scratch.

\paragraph{The step in numbers.} The walk above hides the accounting that the
rest of the book lives on. Here is one decode step of the same model --- one new
token, with $c$ tokens already in the cache --- operation by operation. Weight
counts come from the shapes; bytes are the file's, which stores most matrices at
4.5 bits per weight and some at 6.6 (\Chref{weight-quantization}); FLOPs count
a multiply and an add as two:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.34\linewidth}rrr@{}}
\toprule
Operation & Weights & Bytes read & FLOPs \\
\midrule
Embedding lookup (one row) & 3,072 & 2.5 KB & 0 \\
Q, K, V and output projections, per layer & 25.2 M & & 50.3 M \\
Attention over $c$ cached tokens, per layer & 0 & $4{,}096\,c$ (keys, values) & $12{,}288\,c$ \\

\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Three facts in that table organize everything that follows. First,
almost all the bytes and almost all the FLOPs are in two kinds of operation, the
projections and the feed-forward network, and both are matrix products whose
weights are read once per step whatever the batch (\Chref{forward-pass},
\Chref{matmul-hardware}). Second, attention is the only operation whose cost
grows with the conversation: at $c = 1{,}000$ cached tokens it reads 115~MB of
keys and values, 6\% of the weights, and at $c = 8{,}192$ it reads 940~MB, almost
half of them (\Chref{kv-cache}). Third, the ratio of the FLOPs column to the bytes
column is about three --- three operations per byte --- where the M1 could
perform 38 and an H100 almost 300. That ratio is the subject of \Chref{roofline},
and raising it is the purpose of batching.

\section{What an engine does that a model does not}

A trained model is a function: weights and the arithmetic that maps a sequence
of tokens to a distribution over the next one. Part~\ref{part:foundations}
builds that function from scratch. An \Term{inference engine} is everything
around it that turns the function into a service:

\begin{itemize}
\item it accepts requests and decides which run together in each step
(\Chref{continuous-batching}, \Chref{scheduling});
\item it keeps each request's state --- above all its KV cache --- in memory
it must allocate, share and reclaim (\Chref{kv-cache}, \Chref{paged-kv});
\item it runs the arithmetic in kernels fitted to the hardware
(Part~\ref{part:one-request});
\item it samples tokens, turns them into bytes, streams them, and ends every
request exactly once --- finished, cancelled or failed
(\Chref{request-lifecycle});
\item it does all of this without changing the answer
(\Chref{fast-wrong-answers}).
\end{itemize}

Stripped to its skeleton, every engine in this book runs the same loop. The
version below is the one the capstone engine of \Chref{capstone} implements, in
outline:

\begin{verbatim}
loop {
    admit waiting requests while memory for their longest answer is free
    plan one step: one token for every decoding request,
                   then chunks of waiting prompts, up to a token budget
    run the model once over every planned token,
        reading and writing each request's KV cache
    for every request whose last token was in the step:
        sample the next token, stream its bytes,
        end the request if it hit a stop token, its limit or a cancellation
    return the finished requests' memory
}
\end{verbatim}

\noindent Each line of that loop is a chapter's worth of decisions. How many
requests to admit is a question about memory (\Chref{kv-cache},
\Chref{paged-kv}); what to put in one step is a question about the roofline and
about latency (\Chref{continuous-batching}, \Chref{chunked-prefill}); running
the model is Part~\ref{part:one-request}; sampling and streaming are
\Chref{request-lifecycle}; and doing all of it without changing any answer is
\Chref{fast-wrong-answers}.

A short trace shows the loop at work. Suppose the step's token budget is 16 and
three requests arrive: A with a 6-token prompt at the start, B with a 12-token
prompt at the same moment, and C with a 20-token prompt after the second step.
Each wants four new tokens. The engine's steps then look like this
(illustrative, with the capstone engine's rules):

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}r>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.34\linewidth}r>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.38\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
Step & Planned tokens & Total & What happens \\
\midrule
1 & A: 6 prompt; B: first 10 prompt & 16 & A samples its first token; B's prompt is not finished \\
2 & A: 1; B: last 2 prompt & 3 & A samples its 2nd; B its 1st \\
3 & A: 1; B: 1; C: first 14 prompt & 16 & C arrived; A 3rd, B 2nd \\
4 & A: 1; B: 1; C: last 6 prompt & 8 & A 4th and done; B 3rd; C 1st \\
5 & B: 1; C: 1 & 2 & B done; C 2nd \\
6--7 & C: 1 each & 1 & C 3rd, then 4th and done \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Three things in the trace recur throughout the book. Requests join and
leave the step independently, so no request waits for another to finish; that is
continuous batching (\Chref{continuous-batching}). A long prompt is split
across steps so that it cannot freeze everyone else's next token; that is
chunked prefill (\Chref{chunked-prefill}). And the number of tokens in a step
swings from 16 to 1, so the machine is sometimes busy and sometimes nearly idle;
deciding what to put in each step is scheduling (\Chref{scheduling}). The same
weights can be served at costs that differ by an order of magnitude depending
on how well these jobs are done. That is why inference is a discipline of its
own.

\section{Why the loop is sequential}

Token $t + 1$ cannot be computed before token $t$ exists, because token $t$ is
part of its input. That dependence, called \Term{autoregression}, is the
single fact that shapes inference most. A training step sees a whole document
at once and processes all of its positions in parallel; a generation step sees
one new token and must wait for the previous step to finish. The time to
produce an answer of $n$ tokens is therefore
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{answer}} \approx T_{\text{prefill}} + n \cdot T_{\text{step}},
\label{eq:answer-time}
\end{equation}
with $T_{\text{prefill}}$ the time to process the prompt and $T_{\text{step}}$
the time of one decode step. A 500-token answer at the M1's 50~ms per step
takes 25~seconds, however many other machines are available: a second machine
cannot compute token 250 until the first has produced token 249. Training has no
such floor. A training step can process millions of tokens at once, because
every position of every document is already known, and adding machines shortens
the run almost in proportion. The same model, on the same hardware, is a
parallel problem when it learns and a sequential one when it speaks.

Parallelism must therefore come from somewhere other than one answer's tokens.
It can come from many conversations at once, which is the opening's batch; from
spreading one step's arithmetic over many processors, which shortens
$T_{\text{step}}$ (\Chref{model-parallelism}); or from guessing several tokens
ahead and checking the guesses in one step, which is speculative decoding
(Part~\ref{part:beyond-one-token}). Every one of these spends something the
single conversation did not need --- memory for other conversations,
communication between processors, arithmetic on guesses that fail --- and
each has its chapter and its ledger.

\section{Inference is stateful}

A training step's memory is fixed: the weights, their gradients and the
optimizer's state, whatever the data. Inference memory grows with its users.
Every conversation owns a KV cache that grows by one entry per token for as
long as the conversation lasts, and the engine must hold it between steps,
because recomputing it would cost as much as the whole prompt again
(\Chref{kv-cache}). For Llama~3.2~3B an entry is 112~KiB: two tensors, keys and
values, each of 8 heads of 128 numbers in 16 bits, for each of 28 layers. That
is small per token and large per conversation. At 8,192 tokens one
conversation holds 896~MiB, almost half the size of the model; llama.cpp
allocated exactly that for the opening's benchmark.

The arithmetic of how many conversations fit is the first napkin calculation
every deployment needs. On the 16~GB laptop, with the operating system and
applications taking about half, some 8~GB are available: 2~GB of weights leave
6~GB, or six or seven conversations at 8,192 tokens --- and about 65 at 800. On an
80~GB H100 serving Llama~3's 8-billion-parameter model in 16 bits, whose cache
entry is 128~KiB (32 layers of 8 heads of 128), the weights take 16~GB and the
remaining 60 or so hold about 55 conversations at 8,192 tokens, or about 90 at
4,989, the average context length DeepSeek reported for its own service
\cite{deepseek2025inference} (derived; activations and fragmentation take some
of this, as \Chref{paged-kv} measures). The opening showed that more
conversations per step lower the cost of a token; this arithmetic shows how
quickly memory ends that game. Most of Parts~\ref{part:serving}
and~\ref{part:architectures} --- paged caches, prefix sharing, grouped and latent
attention, quantized caches --- are ways of fitting more conversations into the
same bytes.

State also makes inference fragile in ways training is not. A conversation's
cache must be freed when it ends, including when the user disappears in the
middle of an answer; a server that forgets to free it leaks capacity until it
can admit no one (\Chref{request-lifecycle}). And because the state lives on one
machine, moving a conversation --- to balance load, or to survive a failure ---
means moving or recomputing its cache (\Chref{kv-storage-tier}).

\section{Two outcomes: correctness and cost}

The resources are spent for two outcomes. \textbf{Correctness} comes first: a
fast wrong answer is worthless, and many of the book's optimizations can
change answers in ways no single test run reveals --- lower-precision numbers,
reordered sums, results that depend on which other requests shared a batch
(\Chref{fast-wrong-answers}). On the M1, the chapter's model decoded one prompt
greedily --- always taking the most likely token --- to the same 256 tokens every
time it ran alone, and to a different answer from token 220 onward whenever it
ran beside three or more other conversations, because the batch changed which
kernel summed the numbers and the rounding moved a logit by less than two tenths.
No component was broken; the answer depended on the traffic. An engine that
promises reproducible output has to engineer that property; it does not come
with the model. \textbf{Cost} is measured not as raw throughput
but as \Term{goodput}: the rate of requests served within their latency
objectives \cite{zhong2024distserve}, and, per dollar, tokens at a latency
target. Throughput that users experience as a stall does not count.

A small example shows why the distinction matters. Suppose a service promises
the first token within one second and later tokens at no more than 100~ms
apart, and suppose in one minute it completes 600 requests, of which 450 meet
both promises. Its throughput is 10 requests per second; its goodput is 7.5.
Pushing more traffic through the same hardware can raise throughput and lower
goodput at the same time, and \Chref{production} measures exactly that on the
M1: past about 70\% of a server's capacity its median latency barely moves
while its slowest requests wait four times as long. An engine tuned for
throughput alone will be tuned into that region; an engine tuned for goodput
will stop before it.

Cost per token follows from goodput. A machine that rents for $c$ dollars an
hour and serves $g$ tokens per second within its objectives costs
$c / (3{,}600\,g)$ dollars per token. At an illustrative \$4 per GPU-hour and
2,000 good tokens per second that is \$0.56 per million tokens; at 200 it is
\$5.60. The factor of ten between those two numbers is the size of the
decisions this book is about, and none of them changes the model.

\section{Charging every technique}

The book charges every technique for what it spends, in a small table called
the \Term{constraint ledger}, placed at the end of each chapter's mechanism.
Each row names a resource or outcome, says whether the technique \emph{buys}
it, \emph{spends} it, \emph{moves} the bottleneck elsewhere or puts it at
\emph{risk}, and says by how much when that is known.

Writing the ledger for the opening shows the method. \emph{Compute}: batch-one
decode used about 5\% of the GPU's specified arithmetic --- 20 tokens per second
times 6.43~GFLOP is 129~GFLOP/s --- and thirty-two conversations used about
43\%: 176 times 6.43~GFLOP is 1.13~TFLOP/s, measured rates times derived work.
\emph{Latency}: each conversation's step went from 49.8~ms to 182~ms, measured.
\emph{Memory capacity}: thirty-two caches instead of one; at the benchmark's
192 tokens each, 21~MiB per conversation and 672~MiB in all, affordable here and
decisive at long contexts (\Chref{kv-cache}). \emph{Correctness}: at risk, because the batch changed which
kernel ran, and \Chref{fast-wrong-answers} shows that such a change can change
a greedy answer. Each number came from a measurement or from arithmetic on one,
and each row could be checked.

A technique whose ledger shows no cost has not been understood yet. The
discipline of writing the ledger is what keeps a book about fast-moving
techniques useful: next year's method will buy and spend the same four
resources, and its ledger can be written before its benchmarks are.

\section{A configuration file is a table of contents}

Open the configuration file published with a 2026 flagship model and most of
this book is already named in it. Qwen3.5-397B-A17B's \cite{qwen2026qwen35config}
includes, among other keys:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.43\linewidth}p{0.49\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
Key and value & What an engine must do about it \\
\midrule
\texttt{num\_key\_value\_heads: 2} & a small KV cache per token (\Chref{shrinking-kv}) \\
\texttt{layer\_types}: 45 linear, 15 full attention & keep fixed recurrent state beside a KV cache (\Chref{hybrid-models}) \\
\texttt{num\_experts: 512}, \texttt{num\_experts\_per\_tok: 10} & route tokens to experts (\Chref{mixture-of-experts}) \\
\texttt{mtp\_num\_hidden\_layers: 1} & a built-in drafter for speculation (\Chref{modern-speculation}) \\

\texttt{dtype: bfloat16} & choose what precision to serve in (\Chref{weight-quantization}) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The same reading works on the file this chapter traced. When llama.cpp loads
Llama~3.2~3B it prints the model's metadata (record \texttt{2026-09-26-m1-pass3}),
and each line is a fact an engine must act on. \texttt{n\_layer = 28},
\texttt{n\_head\_kv = 8} and \texttt{n\_embd\_head\_k = 128} fix the cache at
112~KiB per token. \texttt{n\_head = 24} against \texttt{n\_head\_kv = 8} says
that three query heads share each key/value head, which is grouped-query
attention and the reason the cache is a third of what it would otherwise be
(\Chref{shrinking-kv}). \texttt{n\_ff = 8192} and \texttt{n\_expert = 0} say that
every token runs the same feed-forward weights: a dense model, with no routing.
\texttt{n\_ctx\_train = 131072} says how far the model was trained to attend,
which bounds how large a cache a request may build. \texttt{freq\_base\_train =
500000} fixes the rotary position encoding every kernel must reproduce exactly
(\Appref{rope}). And \texttt{file type = Q4\_K - Medium} with \texttt{5.01 BPW}
says the weights average five bits each, mixing formats by tensor, which is why a
step reads 2.01~GB rather than the 6.4~GB of the same model in 16 bits
(\Chref{weight-quantization}). None of these is a tuning knob; each is a
contract between the model and the engine, and breaking any one of them
produces fluent, wrong text rather than an error.

Its August 2026 successor, Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B, keeps the recipe and scales it:
92 layers in 23 groups of three linear-attention layers and one full-attention
layer, 4 key/value heads, 512 experts of which 10 are routed plus one shared,
2.4~trillion parameters with 95~billion active, and 262,144 positions natively,
extensible to about a million \cite{qwen2026qwen38}. Other 2026 checkpoints add
the rest. Kimi~K3 ships its weights in MXFP4, a four-bit floating-point format
with a shared scale per block of 32 values \cite{moonshot2026kimik3}
(\Chref{low-precision-float}); gpt-oss-120b limits half its layers to a
128-token attention window \cite{openai2025gptoss} (\Chref{long-context});
DeepSeek-V3 stores keys and values as a compressed latent \cite{deepseek2024v3}
(\Chref{shrinking-kv}). A decade ago an engine could assume one architecture.
Today it reads the configuration to find out which of a dozen mechanisms it
must implement --- and each has its own entry in the ledger.

\section{How this book is organized}

Part~\ref{part:physics} is the physics: the roofline, the two phases, the KV
cache and the napkin math that predicts a deployment's cost in five minutes.
Part~\ref{part:one-request} makes one request fast: matrix multiplication and
attention kernels on real hardware, and quantization in integer and
floating-point formats. Part~\ref{part:serving} serves many requests:
lifecycle, batching, paged memory, prefix caching, scheduling, structured
output and many models on one server. Part~\ref{part:beyond-one-token} makes
each step produce more than one token. Part~\ref{part:architectures} follows
the model architectures that reshape engines. Part~\ref{part:scaling-out}
scales across GPUs and nodes; Part~\ref{part:hardware-you-have} runs models
larger than the hardware. Part~\ref{part:correctness} keeps answers correct
and measurements honest, and Part~\ref{part:production} operates, compares and
finally builds an engine. Part~\ref{part:foundations} builds the model's
computation from scratch, from bytes to a decoder, for readers who want to
write every line themselves; the capstone engine of \Chref{capstone} serves it.

Three subjects are deliberately left out. The book does not teach training or
fine-tuning, except where an engine must know what training produced --- a
quantization-aware checkpoint, a draft head trained beside the model. It does not
rank models by quality; it treats a model's answers as the thing an engine must
not change, and measures when it does. And it does not survey products; it reads
engines' source at recorded commits and measures what they do, because a
feature list says what an engine can be configured to do, not what it does by
default.

\LabSection{a fake model with real costs}

The lab in \texttt{code/labs/ch01-fake-model/} reproduces the opening without
a model. It fills a matrix the size of a small model's weights --- 537~MB of
32-bit floats, 32,768 rows of 4,096 --- and runs ``decode steps'' that multiply
it by $B$ activation vectors, for $B$ from 1 to 64. The weights are random, so
the outputs mean nothing; the bytes are real, so the costs are. Before timing
anything, each batch's output is checked against two oracles on a sample of
rows: a kernel that computes one output at a time, which must agree bit for bit,
and a 64-bit sum, which must agree to within $10^{-5}$ of the row's scale. The
heart of the lab is one function:

--threads 4} from \texttt{code/labs}. \emph{Predict} first: the program prints
the bytes one step reads; divide your machine's memory bandwidth by them to get
the batch-one rate. On the M1, with four threads, two runs gave (measured,

\emph{Explain} the table with the chapter's two numbers. From one to four
vectors the step time does not change: every step reads the same 537~MB at
about 57~GB/s, nearly the most this machine's memory delivers, and the extra
arithmetic hides inside the wait. At eight the step time doubles, because the
four cores now compute about 115~GFLOP/s, their limit, and the arithmetic is
the wait. Past that point more vectors add work in proportion and tokens per
second stays flat. The GPU of the opening has the same two regimes at different
numbers. \emph{Break} it in two ways. Run with \texttt{--threads 1}: one core
cannot keep up with memory even at $B = 1$ (73--78 tokens per second at about
40~GB/s), so the machine is compute-bound from the start and batching buys less.
Run with \texttt{--mib 8}: the matrix now fits in the processor's caches, the
memory regime disappears, and the lab's story with it. To \emph{extend} it, add
a second matrix and run the two in sequence, as a layer's attention and
feed-forward products are run, and check that the step time is the sum of the
two streams; or give each ``sequence'' a private array that grows by 16~KiB per
step, as a KV cache does, and find the step count at which reading the arrays
costs as much as reading the weights. The second extension is
\Chref{kv-cache}'s argument, measured on your own machine.

\HappenedSection{one context, four users}

Hermon, the Rust inference engine whose measured results this book uses as
evidence, recorded the payoff of the opening measurement in its own
performance notes: on an Apple M3 Pro, serving a 3-billion-parameter Llama
model quantized to four bits to four concurrent users from one shared
context, it produced 41.4 tokens per second in aggregate
against 18.5 for Ollama serving the same four requests (Hermon
\texttt{docs/PERFORMANCE.md}, measured 2026-05-11; the one-context batched
runtime is the default at commit \texttt{2a3fd52},
\texttt{crates/hermon-runtime/src/dispatch.rs}, so DEFAULT). The
mechanism is the one measured above: one pass over the weights served four
sequences. Before that runtime, Hermon served concurrency with a pool of
independent contexts, each with its own cache and its own pass over the
weights, and four users cost four times the memory traffic; the rewrite that
replaced it is described in \Chref{real-engines}.

The same document then multiplies three separately measured speedups ---
batching, prefix caching and speculation --- into a combined estimate of about
five times. The book treats such a product as a hypothesis, not a
measurement: each speedup was measured on a different workload, and their
effects need not compose (\Chref{benchmarking}). Hermon's authors say as
much in the next sentence; the habit of saying it is worth copying.

The largest published account of the same trade at production scale is
DeepSeek's description of the service behind its V3 and R1 models
\cite{deepseek2025inference}. Over one 24-hour period in February 2025 the service
ran on an average of 226.75 nodes of eight H800 GPUs, peaking at 278, and the
company costed it at \$87,072 for the day, assuming \$2 per GPU-hour. Users saw
20 to 22 tokens per second --- about what the M1 in the opening gives a single
conversation --- while each node produced about 14,800 output tokens per second
during decoding and consumed about 73,700 input tokens per second during
prefill. The ratio is the chapter's opening at data-center scale: roughly 700
conversations shared each node's steps (derived: 14,800 divided by 21), each
waiting for all the others, and none of them seeing the machine's speed. The
design choices behind those numbers --- separate machines for prefill and
decode, experts spread across 144 GPUs in each decode unit, batches split in two
so that communication hides behind computation, and 56\% of input tokens served
from a disk cache of earlier prompts --- are the subjects of
\Chref{disaggregated-serving}, \Chref{expert-parallelism} and
\Chref{kv-storage-tier}. DeepSeek also published what its tokens would have
earned at its API prices, \$562,027 for the day, and said its actual revenue was
substantially lower because much of the traffic was free or discounted.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-27}

The models an engine must serve changed shape between 2024 and 2026. The
largest open checkpoints are mixtures of experts with a small active fraction:
Kimi~K3 has 2.8~trillion parameters and activates 104~billion
\cite{moonshot2026kimik3}; Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B has 2.4~trillion and activates
95~billion \cite{qwen2026qwen38}; DeepSeek-V4-Pro has 1.6~trillion and activates
49~billion \cite{deepseek2026v4}; Thinking Machines' Inkling has 975~billion
and activates 41~billion, routing each token to 6 of 256 experts plus 2 shared
ones, and takes images, video and audio as input \cite{thinkingmachines2026inkling}.
Their attention is increasingly hybrid or compressed --- GLM-5.3 caches 576
numbers per token per layer, a compressed latent, and lets an indexer choose
2,048 cached tokens for each query, over up to about a million positions
\cite{zai2026glm53} --- their weights increasingly stored in four-bit floating
point, and their advertised contexts reach a million tokens. Smaller models
follow the same pattern: gpt-oss-120b runs on a single 80~GB GPU because its
experts are stored in MXFP4 \cite{openai2025gptoss}. For an engine, the
frontier is no longer one architecture made fast but many mechanisms made to
coexist. The engines have responded by becoming configurable machines for those
mechanisms: vLLM's default model runner now prepares each step's inputs on the
GPU while the previous step runs, SGLang overlaps its scheduler with the GPU by
default, and both read a model's configuration to decide which cache layouts,
attention kernels and expert placements to build (\Chref{real-engines}). The
chapters that follow take those mechanisms one at a time, each with its cost.
