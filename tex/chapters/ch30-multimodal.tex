\chapter{Multimodal Inference}\label{ch:multimodal}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
A multimodal model reads images, audio or video through an encoder whose
outputs enter the decoder as hundreds or thousands of tokens. The encoder is a
separate, compute-bound stage with its own batching and caching, and the token
flood it produces reshapes prefill and memory. This chapter counts tokens per
image from a real configuration, prices the encoder, and covers caching
encoder outputs, scheduling encoder work, and disaggregating it from the
language model.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{One photograph, a thousand tokens}

Show Gemma~4~E4B a page of this book as a picture and ask it to describe the
image in one sentence. On the M1, through Ollama, the question alone is 23
prompt tokens. With the page attached at 544 by 768 pixels the prompt became
201 tokens; at 1,087 by 1,536 pixels, \textbf{761} --- the image about 740 of
them, one for every 48-by-48-pixel square, as the model's patch size and pooling
predict (measured; record \texttt{2026-09-24-m1-part5}). Prefilling the larger
request took 7.9 seconds, where the text alone took 0.3. Sent a second time,
the same request took 0.06 seconds: Ollama recognized the identical prompt,
image included, and reused its cache. The smallest version of the page, 182 by
256 pixels, still cost 82 tokens, four times what its area implies, so a floor
applies somewhere between pixels and tokens (llama.cpp sets one explicitly: 252
tokens for Gemma~4).

A multimodal request is, to the engine, a long prompt that arrives with an
extra stage in front of it: an encoder that turns pixels into vectors and a
token count set by resolution rather than by what the user typed. This chapter
counts those tokens, prices the encoder, and follows the caching, scheduling
and disaggregation that serving them has produced.

\Foundations{A modality becomes a model input}{
An image, waveform or video is not inherently a sequence of text tokens. A
model-specific processor converts it into patches, features or other representations,
which the model consumes through its declared interface. Text length alone can
therefore understate both prefill work and retained state. Equal image dimensions do
not imply equal token counts across architectures. Chapter~\ref{ch:forward-pass}
explains why tensor shapes determine work; Chapter~\ref{app:formats} explains why
configuration and preprocessing belong to model identity. Separate encoder execution,
transfer, prefill and decode when locating a multimodal bottleneck.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: tokens and FLOPs per image}

A vision transformer cuts an image into square patches of $p$ pixels, embeds
each, and runs a transformer over them; a projector then maps the results into
the language model's embedding space, often merging $s \times s$ neighbouring
patches into one token on the way. An image of $h \times w$ pixels becomes
\begin{equation}
n_{\text{img}} \approx \frac{h\,w}{(p\,s)^{2}}
\label{eq:image-tokens}
\end{equation}
tokens. Gemma~4's smaller models use $p = 16$ and merge $3 \times 3$ patches
(GGUF keys \texttt{vision.patch\_size} and \texttt{projector.scale\_factor}; the
same values in llama.cpp's \texttt{tools/mtmd/clip.cpp} at commit
\texttt{d006858}), so each token covers $48 \times 48$ pixels. The model card's
budgets of 70, 140, 280, 560 and 1,120 tokens per image therefore correspond to
images of about 0.16 to 2.6 megapixels (derived): a budget is a resolution.

The encoder's arithmetic is about twice its parameters per patch, plus
attention over the patches. Gemma~4~E4B's vision tower has 16 layers of width
768 and about 150 million parameters (\texttt{research/FRONTIER.md}); at 280
tokens, 2,520 patches cost it about 1 TFLOP (derived). The language model then
prefills those 280 tokens like any others: at about 4 billion parameters in its
layers, 2.2 TFLOPs (derived). The encoder is a separate stage, but it is not
the largest one; the image's tokens are, and they stay in the cache for the
rest of the conversation at the model's cost per token.

\section{Encoders in front of the decoder}

Most multimodal models are three pieces: an encoder per modality, a projector,
and the language model. The encoder is small next to the decoder --- about 150
million parameters for Gemma~4's E2B and E4B, about 550 million for its 26B and
31B models (model card) --- but it runs on every new image, over hundreds or
thousands of patches at once, so it is compute-bound like a prefill. Its output
replaces placeholder tokens in the prompt: the engine tokenizes the text with a
run of image placeholders, runs the encoder, and writes the encoder's vectors
where the placeholders' embeddings would have gone before the decoder's
prefill. From that point the image is ordinary context.

Gemma~4's 12B model drops the encoders. According to its model card, it
projects raw image patches and audio waveforms into the language model's
embedding space with lightweight linear layers \cite{google2026gemma4}. That
moves the encoder's work into the decoder's prefill, where it is batched and
cached with everything else.

\section{How an image becomes tokens}

Equation~\eqref{eq:image-tokens} hides the decisions that make it true for a
given image: how the image is resized to fit a budget, whether its aspect ratio
is kept, whether it is tiled into crops or processed whole. Gemma~4 supports
``variable image resolution through a configurable visual token budget'', and
recommends lower budgets for classification, captioning and video, higher ones
for OCR and small text (model card, \cite{google2026gemma4}). Other families
differ: vLLM's own benchmark describes 640-by-640 images as about 400 visual
tokens each for Qwen3-VL-4B \cite{vllm2025epd}. llama.cpp bounds each Gemma~4
image to between 252 and 280 tokens and resizes bilinearly
(\texttt{tools/mtmd/clip.cpp} at \texttt{d006858}); another engine, or another
request with a different budget, would give the same photograph a different
number of tokens.

\section{Encoder compute and caching}

The same image is often sent many times: a document asked several questions,
a product photo in every request of a shopping assistant, a screenshot
re-sent with each step of an agent. Engines cache at several levels. The
preprocessed image can be cached so a repeat skips decoding and resizing: vLLM
caches its multimodal processor's outputs up to 4~GB by default
(\texttt{mm\_processor\_cache\_gb}, \cite{vllm2026optimization}). The
encoder's output can be cached by the image's content hash so a repeat skips the
encoder; with encoder disaggregation that cache is shared across requests
\cite{vllm2025epd}. And the decoder's cache over the image's tokens
can be reused by prefix caching, provided the block hashes include the image:
vLLM adds the image's hash to the hash of every block containing its
placeholder tokens, since the placeholders alone are identical for every image
(\texttt{docs/design/prefix\_caching.md} at commit \texttt{bcdacfc}). Without
that, two different images behind the same text would share a cached prefix and
one of them would be answered about the other.

\section{Scheduling encoder work}

The encoder adds a stage with its own cost to a scheduler built for text.
vLLM gives the encoder its own per-step budget of input tokens and a separate
encoder-output cache, both set to the step's token budget and not yet
configurable (\texttt{max\_num\_encoder\_input\_tokens} and
\texttt{encoder\_cache\_size} in \texttt{vllm/config/scheduler.py} at
\texttt{bcdacfc}), so an image-heavy request cannot monopolize a step. The encoder can also run with data
parallelism across tensor-parallel ranks, replicating its weights, which vLLM
reports improves throughput and time to first token by about 10\%
(\texttt{mm\_encoder\_tp\_mode="data"}, \cite{vllm2026optimization}). Images
arrive with the prompt, so encoder work falls on time to first token; a
scheduler that treats it as free prefill underestimates exactly the latency
users of multimodal models notice.

\section{Audio and video}

Audio and video multiply the token flood. Gemma~4 accepts audio of up to 30
seconds on its E2B, E4B and 12B models, and video of up to 60 seconds processed
at one frame per second (model card, \cite{google2026gemma4}). At its budgets
of 70 to 280 tokens per frame, a minute of video is 4,200 to 16,800 tokens
before the question is asked (derived). Video is where token budgets, frame
sampling and encoder caching across overlapping clips decide whether a request
fits at all.

\section{Separating the encoder}

Because the encoder's work is compute-bound, arrives in bursts and has its own
memory needs, it can run on separate devices, as prefill and decode can
(\Chref{disaggregated-serving}). Singh and colleagues proposed
encode--prefill--decode disaggregation and report, against systems that run
encoding with prefill, up to 15 times lower peak memory, batch sizes up to 22
times larger, 10 times more images per request, and up to 71\% lower time to
first token (their measurement, \cite{singh2025epd}). vLLM added native encoder
disaggregation in release 0.11.1: encoder workers write image embeddings to an
encoder cache that prefill and decode workers read, which also makes the cache
shared across requests \cite{vllm2025epd}.

\section{Preprocessing is part of the model}

An image reaches the encoder after decoding, resizing, cropping, normalizing
and patch ordering, and each step was fixed when the model was trained. The
reference implementation is usually a Python image processor; engines that
cannot run Python re-implement it. llama.cpp re-implements each supported
model's preprocessing in C++, with a resize algorithm chosen per model family
(\texttt{tools/mtmd/clip.cpp} at \texttt{d006858}). A mismatch --- a different
interpolation, a different rounding of the target size --- changes the pixels,
the tokens and possibly the answer, without any error. Multimodal correctness
testing therefore starts before the model: the preprocessed tensor is
compared with the reference processor's on the same image
(\Chref{fast-wrong-answers}).

The decoder's input rows have a history before they reach the language stack. Cropping, patch order, normalization and feature projection can all change that history. A cache key based only on the text prompt would miss those model inputs.

\end{ConstraintLedger}

\HappenedSection{encoder disaggregation}

vLLM merged native encode--prefill--decode disaggregation in November 2025
and measured it on Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct with four A100 GPUs, 640-by-640 images
(about 400 visual tokens each) and short or long text inputs
\cite{vllm2025epd}. With one image per request it barely helped: 23 to 24
requests per second. With four images per request, goodput doubled from 6 to
12 requests per second, with P99 times to first token and per output token often
20 to 50\% lower, and with 2,000-token texts goodput stayed 2 to 2.5 times
higher (the developers' measurement). The report's own caveat is the chapter's
napkin math: ``with longer inputs, image-encoding costs become a small
fraction of total work.'' Separating the encoder pays when images dominate the
prefill, and not otherwise.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-24}

Two directions pull against each other. Some models drop the encoder and
project raw patches straight into the decoder (Gemma~4~12B), making images
ordinary prefill; serving systems meanwhile split multimodal pipelines into
more stages, with vLLM-Omni decomposing any-to-any models into a graph of
stages and reporting up to 91.4\% lower job completion time than baselines
\cite{yin2026vllmomni}. Token budgets per image are becoming a request
parameter, which makes image cost something the caller chooses and the
scheduler must price.
