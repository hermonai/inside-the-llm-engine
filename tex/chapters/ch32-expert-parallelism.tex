\chapter{Expert Parallelism at Scale}\label{ch:expert-parallelism}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
Placing experts on different GPUs turns every mixture-of-experts layer
into an all-to-all exchange: tokens are dispatched to their experts' devices
and the results are combined back. This chapter prices that exchange, covers
the dispatch and combine kernels built for it, load imbalance and redundant
expert replicas, and overlapping communication with computation, and explains
when wide expert parallelism across many GPUs beats keeping experts
local.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{An all-to-all in every layer}

In early 2025 DeepSeek described how it serves V3 and R1. Decode ran on 18
nodes of eight GPUs, and each GPU held just two of a layer's 256 routed experts
and its shared expert; prefill ran on 4 nodes, nine routed experts per GPU, with
32 redundant copies of the busiest experts (the operator's report,
\cite{deepseek2025inference}). Each GPU holds a sliver of every MoE layer, and
every token needs eight slivers per layer. So every token's hidden state leaves
its GPU for up to eight others and eight results come back, in each of the
model's 58 MoE layers.

For one token of DeepSeek-V3 that is about 168~KiB per MoE layer --- the
7,168-element hidden state sent to eight experts in FP8 and returned from them
in BF16 --- and 9.5~MiB per token per forward pass (derived). At the 50~GB/s of
one 400~Gb/s InfiniBand port, the traffic of a single token takes about
200~$\mu$s, and a decode step in which a GPU serves 128 tokens needs 1.3~GB of
it: 26 milliseconds of network time per step if nothing overlapped (derived,
upper bounds, since routing that keeps some experts on the same node sends part
of it over NVLink instead). DeepSeek reported 20 to 22 tokens per second per
user from that system, about 45 to 50 milliseconds a step
\cite{deepseek2025inference}. At this scale the machine that serves a
mixture-of-experts model is first of all a network, and the engineering of
expert parallelism is the engineering of hiding it
\todo{measure: dispatch and combine latency and bandwidth on a multi-GPU node,
for example with DeepEP's test suite or vLLM's all-to-all backends}.

\Foundations{Routing becomes physical movement}{
When experts live on different devices, a router's logical assignment becomes a
dispatch of activation rows and a return of expert outputs. An all-to-all exchange
permits different destinations to receive different data; it is not an all-reduce. The
slowest destination can govern completion even when average load looks balanced.
Chapter~\ref{ch:mixture-of-experts} establishes expert selection, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:model-parallelism} establishes collectives. Distinguish the bytes of
activations sent to experts from the bytes of expert weights retained on each device;
balancing one does not automatically balance the other.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: dispatch bytes per token}

With $k$ experts per token, hidden width $d$, dispatch precision $b_{\text{d}}$
and combine precision $b_{\text{c}}$ bytes, one token moves at most
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{token}} = k\,d\,(b_{\text{d}} + b_{\text{c}})
\label{eq:ep-bytes}
\end{equation}
bytes per MoE layer, out and back. For DeepSeek-V3 ($k = 8$, $d = 7{,}168$, FP8
out, BF16 back) that is 172,032 bytes; for Qwen3-30B-A3B ($k = 8$, $d = 2{,}048$,
BF16 both ways), 65,536 bytes (derived). Two things reduce it. Tokens routed to
several experts on the same GPU or node need to cross the slow link once, and
DeepSeek-V3 was trained to send each token to at most 4 nodes (report section
2.1.2, \cite{deepseek2024v3}), so the inter-node traffic per token is bounded by
the nodes, not the experts. And a shared expert, computed where the token
already is, sends nothing.

Compare the arithmetic. A routed expert of DeepSeek-V3 does about $6 \times
7{,}168 \times 2{,}048 \approx 88$ million FLOPs per token, so eight of them do
705 million, while moving 172,032 bytes: about 4,100 FLOP per byte of network
traffic (derived). An H100's dense FP8 peak of 1,979~TFLOP/s against a 50~GB/s
NIC needs about 40,000 FLOP per network byte to balance --- the dispatch, not the expert, sets
the step time unless communication overlaps computation, which is why every
system in this chapter is built around overlap.

\section{Placing experts}

Expert parallelism assigns each expert to one device, so a layer's $E$ experts
are spread over $G$ devices, $E/G$ each, and every device holds all of the
attention and shared layers or a data-parallel share of them. The weights no
longer need replicating: DeepSeek-V3's 671 billion parameters, mostly experts,
fit across enough devices that each holds a few gigabytes of experts and has
most of its memory left for cache. The attention layers are typically run data
parallel --- each device serves different sequences --- which is how vLLM
deploys DeepSeek-style models: EP for the experts, DP for attention, with
$\text{EP} = \text{TP} \times \text{DP}$ ranks (\texttt{docs/serving/expert\_parallel\_deployment.md}
at commit \texttt{bcdacfc}), and how DeepSeek describes its own service
(``Routed Expert EP32, MLA/Shared Expert DP32'' in prefill;
\Chref{shrinking-kv}).

Placement is not arbitrary. Experts that are often chosen together can share a
node, so a token's traffic stays local; DeepSeek's load balancer places
``experts of the same group'' on the same node whenever possible to cut
inter-node traffic \cite{deepseek2025eplb}.

\section{Dispatch and combine}

An MoE layer under expert parallelism runs in four steps. The router picks each
token's experts where the token is. \emph{Dispatch} sends each token's hidden
state to the devices holding its experts --- an all-to-all, since every device
sends to every other. Each device runs its experts on the tokens it received,
as a grouped GEMM (\Chref{mixture-of-experts}). \emph{Combine} sends the results
back to the tokens' devices, where they are weighted by the gates and summed.

The all-to-all is the hard collective. Unlike an all-reduce, its message sizes
depend on the routing, so they are known only after the router runs, change
every step, and are uneven. DeepSeek's DeepEP library implements dispatch and
combine as dedicated GPU kernels with FP8 dispatch and BF16 combine; its
current README reports, for 8,192 tokens per batch at DeepSeek-V3's shape,
``bottleneck'' bandwidths of 90~GB/s over RDMA across two nodes of eight
Hopper GPUs using 12 of each GPU's SMs, 61~GB/s across four nodes with 6 SMs,
and 726~GB/s over NVLink within one Blackwell node with 64 SMs (vendor numbers,
\cite{deepseek2025deepep}). The SM counts matter: every SM spent moving tokens
is an SM not multiplying them.

\section{Imbalance and redundant experts}

The all-to-all finishes when the busiest device finishes. Even perfectly
uniform routing leaves some device above the mean: with $\mu$ token--expert
pairs per device on average across $G$ devices, the busiest gets about
$1 + \sqrt{2\ln G/\mu}$ times the mean. A seeded simulation with DeepSeek-V3's
routing shape gives 1.06 at 4,096 tokens per step on 32 GPUs, 1.19 at 512
tokens and 1.38 at 128; on 128 GPUs at 512 tokens, 1.48 (derived by
simulation; script in the Part VI record). Decode, with its small batches per
device, sits at the bad end of that range --- and real routing is not uniform.
vLLM's documentation warns that token counts across experts ``can be highly
skewed'' in practice \cite{vllm2026eplb}.

The remedy is to replicate the hot experts. DeepSeek duplicates high-load
experts, chosen from online statistics and adjusted periodically, and packs
the copies onto devices to even the load: 32 redundant experts in prefill,
and in decode a design where 64 of 320 GPUs host redundant and shared experts
(report section 3.4, \cite{deepseek2024v3}). Its open-source EPLB offers a
hierarchical policy when nodes divide expert groups and a global one otherwise
\cite{deepseek2025eplb}. vLLM's EPLB collects load statistics every forward
pass, rebalances every 3,000 engine steps by default, and warns of the memory
bill: one redundant expert per rank costs about 2.4~GB for DeepSeek-V3
\cite{vllm2026eplb}. Redundancy trades memory --- and so cache capacity ---
for tail latency.

\section{Overlapping communication with compute}

If dispatch and combine cannot be made cheap, they can be hidden. DeepSeek's
prefill processes two micro-batches of similar size at once, overlapping the
attention and MoE computation of one with the dispatch and combine of the
other (report section 3.4.1, \cite{deepseek2024v3}). Within a kernel, the
same idea appears as communication that proceeds while the GPU computes,
without occupying the SMs the computation needs. The cost of overlap is
latency: a request's step now waits for its partner micro-batch, and the batch
must be large enough to split. Overlap therefore suits prefill and
throughput-oriented decode better than a single interactive stream.

\section{Wide expert parallelism across nodes}

Why spread one model over 144 GPUs rather than 8? Because the batch that makes
MoE decode efficient is enormous (Equation~\ref{eq:moe-ridge-batch},
\Chref{mixture-of-experts}): each GPU should see enough tokens per expert to
fill its matrix units, and with two experts per GPU and tokens gathered from
the whole cluster, it does. Wide EP converts many small, bandwidth-bound expert
products into fewer, fuller ones, at the price of the network. SGLang's team
reported running DeepSeek on 96 H100 GPUs with prefill--decode disaggregation
and large-scale EP at 52.3 thousand input and 22.3 thousand output tokens per
second per node for 2,000-token inputs, and up to 5 times the output throughput
of plain tensor parallelism on the same hardware (the developers'
measurement, \cite{sglang2025largeep}). Rack-scale NVLink domains move the
trade again: 72 GPUs sharing 130~TB/s of NVLink (\Chref{model-parallelism})
put a whole wide-EP group inside one fast fabric.

\section{Low-latency kernels for decode}

Decode steps are short and latency-bound, and they want CUDA graphs, which
cannot depend on how many tokens each expert receives. The answer is a masked
layout: allocate every expert's buffer at its maximum size and pass the true
counts as data, so one captured graph serves every step. DeepGEMM's grouped
GEMM supports exactly this masked layout for decode (\Chref{mixture-of-experts},
\cite{deepseek2025deepgemm}), and vLLM offers separate all-to-all backends for
the two regimes: \texttt{deepep\_high\_throughput}, with a contiguous layout for
prefill, and \texttt{deepep\_low\_latency}, with CUDA-graph support and a masked
layout for decode (\texttt{docs/serving/expert\_parallel\_deployment.md} at
\texttt{bcdacfc}).

The expert output is not useful until it can be associated with the original token and routing weight. Read the diagram in both directions: dispatch changes ownership of the work, and combine restores its logical row order. Communication overlap cannot remove either dependency.

\LedgerSection

Spreading experts across $G$ devices with an all-to-all per MoE layer:

\begin{ConstraintLedger}
Memory capacity & buys & Each device holds $E/G$ experts of every layer instead of all of them; the freed memory becomes cache. \\
Compute & buys & Tokens gathered from the whole cluster fill each expert's matrix products; batches per expert grow with $G$. \\
Latency & spends & Two all-to-alls per MoE layer, 168~KiB per token per layer for DeepSeek-V3, finished by the busiest device. \\
Memory capacity & spends & Redundant experts to tame imbalance: about 2.4~GB per redundant expert per rank for DeepSeek-V3. \\
\end{ConstraintLedger}

\HappenedSection{an open-source dispatch library}

DeepSeek released DeepEP on the second day of its February 2025 open-source
week as ``the first open-source EP communication library for MoE model training
and inference'': all-to-all kernels within and across nodes, high-throughput
kernels for training and prefill, low-latency kernels for decode, native FP8
dispatch, and control over how many GPU resources communication takes
\cite{deepseek2025openweek}. It became a dependency of the open engines' expert
parallelism: vLLM's installation guide for EP starts with installing DeepEP
and DeepGEMM, and its high-throughput and low-latency all-to-all backends are
built on it (\texttt{docs/serving/expert\_parallel\_deployment.md} at
\texttt{bcdacfc}). The library kept moving: its second version replaced its
communication layer with an NCCL-based backend and reports up to 1.3 times the
first version's peak performance while using up to 4 times fewer SMs (vendor
numbers, \cite{deepseek2025deepep}). The episode is a pattern this book keeps
meeting: a serving technique becomes industry infrastructure the day a
production team publishes the kernels behind it.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-24}

Expert parallelism is being pulled in two directions. Fabrics grow: rack-scale
NVLink domains of 72 GPUs let wide EP run inside one high-bandwidth network,
and NVIDIA's Dynamo reports seven times the throughput per GPU for DeepSeek-R1
on such a rack (vendor claim, \cite{nvidia2026dynamo}). And models grow sparser: 16 of 896 experts in
Kimi~K3 (\Chref{mixture-of-experts}) raises both the number of devices worth
spreading over and the imbalance each step must absorb. Dispatch libraries,
load balancers that move experts while serving, and routing trained with the
deployment's topology in mind are where the work is.
