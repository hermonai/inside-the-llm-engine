\chapter{Napkin Math: Latency, Throughput and Cost per Token}\label{ch:napkin-math}

\begin{ChapterIntent}
The previous chapters give three numbers for any model on any machine: bytes
per token, FLOPs per token and cache bytes per token. This chapter turns them
into a five-minute model of an inference deployment --- step time, the batch
size at which decode stops being bandwidth-bound, tokens per second per user
and in aggregate, time to first token, memory headroom and dollars per million
tokens at a latency target. It shows how to calibrate the model's two
efficiencies on the reader's own machine, checks its predictions against
measurements on the laptop, reconstructs a published data-center deployment
from its configuration, and catalogues where napkin math goes wrong: kernel
efficiency, launch and scheduling overhead, communication, and real workload
mixes.
\end{ChapterIntent}

\OpeningSection{Predicting a benchmark before running it}

Before the M1 measurements of \Chref{roofline} were taken, three predictions
could have been written down from two numbers each.

\emph{Batch-one decode.} Llama~3.2~3B reads 2.01~GB per token; a streaming
kernel moves 49~GB/s on that GPU. Prediction: at most 24 tokens per second.
Measured: 21.0. The prediction was 15\% high, and the direction of the error
was known in advance --- a bound can only be approached.

\emph{Prefill.} 512 tokens at 6.4~GFLOP each is 3.3~TFLOP; at the GPU's
2.6~TFLOP/s the prompt cannot take less than 1.27~s. Measured: 2.07~s, so the
kernels ran at 61\% of peak. The bound was right; the efficiency had to be
measured once.

\emph{Batched decode.} Thirty-two sequences need 206~GFLOP per step, at least
79~ms at peak, and 2.01~GB of weights, at least 41~ms at 49~GB/s. Prediction: a
step of about 79~ms, 405 tokens per second. Measured: 182~ms and 176 tokens per
second --- the prediction was 2.3 times too optimistic, because the kernel
running at that batch size pays for a 32-column tile whatever the batch, at a
fraction of the GPU's peak (\Chref{roofline}).

Two predictions within 15\% and one off by more than a factor of two is the
normal state of napkin math, and the skill is knowing which is which. When the
model captures the binding resource, it is accurate. When an implementation
detail binds instead, the model's error \emph{is} the finding: it points at the
detail. This chapter builds the model and then lists the details.

\Foundations{A model with declared omissions}{
An estimate is useful because its assumptions are visible. Separate resident bytes
from bytes transferred per step, and separate per-request latency from aggregate
tokens per second. A service can improve the second while worsening the first. Use the
resource equation to identify a lower bound, then add the queueing and software costs
it omits; do not silently call a lower bound a prediction. Decimal GB and binary GiB
differ, as Appendix~\ref{app:notation} explains. Before using the chapter's equations,
write down batch size, context length, precision and the boundary at which time is
measured.
}

\NapkinSection{Napkin math: the step-time model}

A decode step for $B$ sequences with mean context $\bar{S}$ reads the weights
once, reads each sequence's cache, and performs each sequence's FLOPs. With
effective bandwidth $\eta_\beta\beta$ and effective compute $\eta_\pi\pi$, its
time is about
\begin{equation}
t_{\text{step}}(B,\bar{S}) \approx
\max\!\left(\frac{W + B\,\bar{S}\,m_{kv}}{\eta_\beta\,\beta},\;
\frac{B\,F_{\text{token}}(\bar{S})}{\eta_\pi\,\pi}\right) + t_0,
\label{eq:step-time}
\end{equation}
where $W$ is the weight bytes read per step, $m_{kv}$ the cache bytes per
token (\Chref{kv-cache}), $F_{\text{token}}$ the FLOPs per token
(\Chref{forward-pass}) and $t_0$ a fixed per-step overhead: launching kernels,
scheduling, sampling. Everything a deployment is judged by follows from it:
\begin{align}
\text{time per output token} &= t_{\text{step}}, &
\text{throughput} &= B/t_{\text{step}}, \label{eq:throughput}\\
\text{cost per token} &= \frac{\text{price per GPU-second}}{B/t_{\text{step}}}, &
B_{\max} &= \frac{M_{\text{HBM}}-M_{\text{weights}}-M_{\text{act}}}{m_{kv}\bar{S}}.\nonumber
\end{align}
Five inputs describe the model ($W$, $F_{\text{token}}$, $m_{kv}$ and the
resident weight bytes) and four the machine ($\beta$, $\pi$, capacity, price).
The two efficiencies are the only numbers that must be measured rather than
looked up. The chapter's lab is a calculator that evaluates exactly this
model; every table in the chapter is its output, and its core is a dozen lines:

Read the rows by hand once. At $B = 1$ the step reads 15.1~GB of weights and
0.6~GB of cache: 15.7~GB at $0.8 \times 3.35 = 2.68$~TB/s is 5.9~ms, while

the weights are still read once, but the cache is read 32 times: $15.1 + 32
\times 0.60 = 34.4$~GB, 12.9~ms. The compute side has grown to 0.95~ms, still
thirteen times shorter. The step's growth from 5.9 to 12.9~ms is all cache.

\section{The knee: where decode turns compute-bound}

\Chref{roofline} found that batching moves decode toward the compute roof and
that it arrives at $B^{*}\approx b_wI^{*}/2$ --- about 300 sequences for BF16
weights on an H100. The table never gets there. Every row is bandwidth-bound,
and at $B=98$ memory is full.

The cache is the reason. Each added sequence adds $F_{\text{token}}$ to the
compute side of equation~\eqref{eq:step-time} but $\bar{S}m_{kv}$ to the
bandwidth side. Decode can become compute-bound at any batch size only if the
first grows faster than the second:
\begin{equation}
\bar{S} < \frac{F_{\text{token}}(\bar{S})}{\eta_\pi\pi}\cdot\frac{\eta_\beta\beta}{m_{kv}}
\approx 470\ \text{tokens}
\label{eq:knee-context}
\end{equation}
for Qwen3-8B on an H100 (derived; $F_{\text{token}}$ itself grows with the
context, so the calculator solves for the crossing). Above about 470 tokens of
mean context, adding sequences adds more memory time than compute time, and
decode stays bandwidth-bound however large the batch. The threshold does not
move if both weights and cache are stored in FP8: the bytes halve and the FP8
compute peak doubles. Long-context decode is a memory problem by construction,
and the techniques that change it are the ones that cut $m_{kv}$
(\Chref{shrinking-kv}, \Chref{kv-quantization}) or read less of the cache
(\Chref{long-context}).

\section{Adding the KV cache}

The cache sets the batch size as well as the step time. With 80~GB of HBM,
16.4~GB of weights and 4~GB set aside for activations (illustrative), the H100
holds 98 sequences of 4,096 tokens. At that batch, the cache is 59~GB of the
74~GB each step reads: \textbf{80\% of the bytes in a decode step are cache,
not weights}. Shorter contexts allow larger batches and make the weights
matter again; longer contexts make the cache matter more. A napkin model that
leaves out the cache is wrong by a factor of five at this operating point.

\section{Calibrating the model on your own machine}

The model has two numbers that cannot be looked up, and they must come from
measurements on the machine in question. The labs of Chapters~\ref{ch:roofline}
and~\ref{ch:prefill-decode} already produced them for the M1's GPU:

\begin{itemize}
\item $\eta_\beta$, the fraction of nominal bandwidth a real kernel moves: the
streaming kernel reached 46--50~GB/s of the nominal 68, $\eta_\beta \approx 0.72$;
llama.cpp's decode reached 42~GB/s, 0.62.
\item $\eta_\pi$, the fraction of peak compute a real kernel sustains: the
prefill plateau of \Chref{prefill-decode}, 248--297 tokens per second at
6.43~GFLOP each, is $\eta_\pi = 0.61$--$0.73$ against Apple's 2.6~TFLOP/s.
\item $t_0$, the fixed cost per step: small on this machine, because the
batch-one step measured below (40.9~ms) is within 1\% of the time the 2.01~GB of
weights take to stream at the streaming kernel's 49~GB/s; it is the number that
matters most for small models on fast GPUs, where a step lasts a millisecond and
launches a hundred kernels or more (\Chref{kernel-languages}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Prefill in the same model}

Prefill of a 4,096-token prompt costs about 62~TFLOP --- the layers' matrix
products plus causal attention --- or about 104~ms at 60\% of the H100's peak
(derived). That is the prompt's contribution to its own time to first token.
It is also time the GPU is not decoding: one such prefill costs as much GPU
time as four decode steps of the full 98-sequence batch. In a mixed workload,
prompts and replies compete for the same seconds, and the price of a token
depends on how many prompt tokens came with it (\Chref{chunked-prefill},
\Chref{prefix-caching}).

The same numbers price an input token. A prompt token of Qwen3-8B costs about
16~GFLOP of matrix products and attention at 2,000--4,000 tokens of context, 27~$\mu$s
at 60\% of the H100's BF16 peak: about 37,000 prompt tokens per second, or about
\$0.03 per million input tokens at the rental price below (derived) --- a tenth
of the \$0.31 per million output tokens at a full batch. The ratio is the
roofline again: a prompt token shares its weight bytes with thousands of others
in the same step, a generated token with at most a hundred. It is why pricing
input and output tokens separately makes sense, and why a service's cost depends
on its mix of the two.

\section{From tokens per second to dollars per million tokens}

At \$3.99 per GPU-hour --- Lambda's on-demand price for an H100 in an
eight-GPU instance, checked on 2026-09-23 and certain to change --- a GPU that
produces 3,534 tokens per second costs \$0.31 per million output tokens. At
batch one it costs \$6.51. Batching alone changes the price of a token
twenty-fold.

Quantization changes it again. Store the same weights and cache in FP8 and the
same GPU holds 224 sequences, reads half the bytes per sequence, and produces
about 8,000 tokens per second: \$0.14 per million. A B200 at \$6.69 per hour
holds 555 such sequences in its 180~GB and produces about 20,000 tokens per
second: \$0.09 per million (all derived with the same illustrative
efficiencies; prefill and idle time not included).

\section{Goodput: throughput that meets its objective}

Throughput is only worth selling if users accept the latency it comes with.
Suppose the service promises a time per output token of at most 15~ms --- about
67 tokens per second per user. Equation~\eqref{eq:step-time} then limits the
BF16 H100 to $B\le41$, 2,750 tokens per second and \$0.40 per million: the
promise costs 30\% more per token than running the memory full. In FP8 the same
promise allows 108 sequences and \$0.15 per million, and on a B200 292 and \$0.10
(derived). At a looser 50~ms objective, all three configurations are limited by
memory, not by the objective. The latency target, not the hardware, often sets the price
\cite{zhong2024distserve}. The worked problems add a time-to-first-token
objective and find which one binds first.

\section{Time to first token under load}

Equation~\eqref{eq:step-time} prices a step; it says nothing about how long a
request waits before its first step. That wait is a queue, and queues have one
behaviour every engineer should carry around: as the arrival rate $\lambda$
approaches the service's capacity $\mu$, waiting grows like
$\rho/(1-\rho)$, where $\rho = \lambda/\mu$ is the utilization. At half of
capacity the typical wait is about one service time; at 80\% it is four; at
90\%, nine; past 100\%, the queue grows without limit and so does every waiting
time. The napkin model gives $\mu$ --- the requests per second the machine can
finish at the chosen batch --- and queueing turns it into a time to first token.

The book measured this curve on the laptop, with llama-server serving
Llama~3.2~3B from four slots to an open-loop stream of requests of about 200
prompt tokens and 48 generated tokens (measured, record
\texttt{2026-09-26-m1-part8}). Completions topped out at about 0.52 requests per
second. The 99th-percentile time to first token was 2.1~s at 0.23 requests per
second (45\% of that capacity), 6.3~s at 0.36 (69\%), 11.9~s at 0.43 (82\%), and
29 and 42~s once arrivals exceeded the capacity. The median moved less --- 0.9~s
up to 69\%, then 1.4~s, then 12.7~s --- because a queue's tail feels the load
first. Between 45\% and 82\% of capacity the tail wait grew 5.7-fold, and
$\rho/(1-\rho)$ grows 5.6-fold over the same range: the shape of the formula,
measured on a laptop.

For a deployment the lesson is a rule of provisioning. A service that must keep
its time-to-first-token tail within a few service times cannot run its machines
near 100\% of the capacity the napkin model computes; it must run them at 60 to
80\% and pay for the idle remainder. The chapter's goodput section priced a TPOT
objective at 30\% more per token; a tail-latency objective on TTFT costs another
20 to 40\% of capacity on top. \Chref{scheduling} chooses where to sit on the
curve, and \Chref{production} measures this same server's knee in detail.

The first-token side is just as predictable and more often forgotten. On the
same laptop a 2,048-token prompt took 7.3~s to prefill (measured, at 282 prompt
tokens per second), and the prefill rate falls as prompts grow
(\Chref{prefill-decode}): a 32,000-token document would take more than two
minutes before the first word of an answer, even at the 225 tokens per second
measured for 4,096 tokens (derived). That is why pasting a long document into a
local model feels slow while chatting with it does not, and why caching a
document's prefix is worth as much on a laptop as in a data center
(\Chref{prefix-caching}).

Newer machines change the inputs, not the method. Apple quotes 153~GB/s for the
M5, 614~GB/s for the M5~Max and 1.2~TB/s for the M5~Ultra with up to 512~GB of
memory \cite{apple2025m5,apple2026m5ultra}. A 70-billion-parameter model in four
bits reads about 40~GB per token; at 72\% of the quoted bandwidth --- the fraction
the M1's streaming kernel reached --- that is about 3 tokens per second on an M5,
11 on an M5~Max and 22 on an M5~Ultra (derived). Whether the
model fits --- and how much cache is left for context once it does --- decides the
rest. The prediction takes a minute; a download of 40~GB takes longer, which is
the practical case for doing the arithmetic first.

\section{Which input matters}

A napkin model is most useful when it says which of its inputs is worth
improving. Change each input of the Qwen3-8B, BF16, H100 row by 20\% at the full
batch, and the cost per million output tokens moves as follows (derived with the
lab's calculator):

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
Change & Largest batch & Step & Cost per million \\
\midrule
None (4,096 tokens of context) & 98 & 27.7 ms & \$0.314 \\
Memory bandwidth, or $\eta_\beta$, $+20\%$ & 98 & 23.1 ms & $-16.7\%$ \\
Peak compute $+20\%$ & 98 & 27.7 ms & $0.0\%$ \\
Memory capacity $+20\%$ & 125 & 33.8 ms & $-4.4\%$ \\
Weight bytes $-20\%$ & 104 & 28.0 ms & $-5.0\%$ \\
Cache bytes per token $-20\%$ & 123 & 27.8 ms & $-20.1\%$ \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\noindent Three things stand out. Compute does not matter at all at this
operating point: every step is bandwidth-bound, so a faster tensor core buys
nothing. Capacity and weights matter only a little: more room buys a larger
batch, but each added sequence adds its cache to every step's reads, so most of
the gain is eaten. And the cost is exactly proportional to the cache bytes per
token and to the context --- halving the cache per token is worth as much as
halving the price of the GPU, and doubling the conversation's length doubles the
cost of every token in it. This is the quantitative case for the cache chapters
of Part~\ref{part:architectures} (\Chref{shrinking-kv}, \Chref{kv-quantization}):
at long context the cache, not the model, is what a token costs.

The same analysis at batch one gives a different ranking. Only bandwidth and
weight bytes matter, each almost in proportion, and the cache hardly at all.
Which input to improve depends on where the deployment runs on the
throughput--latency curve, and the napkin model is the cheapest way to find out.

\section{A procedure for napkin math}

The chapter's calculations follow the same seven steps each time, and writing
them down makes them quick enough to do in a design meeting.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Bytes per step.} From the configuration: the weights a step
multiplies, at the stored precision --- all but the input table in a dense model,
the union of the batch's experts in a mixture.
\item \emph{FLOPs per token.} Two per multiplied weight, plus attention's
$4\,L\,S\,H_qd_h$ at the context in question.
\item \emph{Cache per token.} Equation~\eqref{eq:kv-per-token} of
\Chref{kv-cache}, at the cache's precision, counting only the layers that keep
a growing cache.
\item \emph{Largest batch.} Memory less weights less a reserve, divided by the
cache of one sequence.
\item \emph{Step time and bound.} Equation~\eqref{eq:step-time} at each batch of
interest, with measured efficiencies if you have them.
\item \emph{Objectives.} The batch a TPOT objective allows, the utilization a
TTFT tail allows, and the smaller of those and the memory limit.
\item \emph{One measurement.} Check one prediction on the real system before
trusting the rest, and assign its error to a term.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The last step is the one that is skipped, and the one that makes the
other six worth doing: without it, a napkin model is a guess with units.

\section{Where napkin math breaks}

Equation~\eqref{eq:step-time} is only as good as its assumptions, and each
fails somewhere a later chapter lives.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Kernel efficiency} is not a constant. It depends on shapes and
batch sizes, as the M1's staircase and the calibration table showed
(\Chref{matmul-hardware}).
\item \emph{Fixed overheads} $t_0$ --- kernel launches, synchronization,
scheduling, sampling --- can dominate short steps (\Chref{kernel-languages}).
\item \emph{Communication} between GPUs appears in no term; tensor and expert
parallelism add it to every layer (\Chref{model-parallelism},
\Chref{expert-parallelism}).
\item \emph{Workload mix}: real batches mix prompts and replies of different
lengths, and the mean hides the tail (\Chref{benchmarking}).
\item \emph{Mixture of experts}: the weights read per step grow with the batch,
as more experts are touched (\Chref{mixture-of-experts}), and real routing is
not uniform.
\item \emph{Tokens per step}: speculative decoding produces more than one
token per sequence per step (\Chref{speculative-decoding}).
\item \emph{Run-to-run variance}: on the laptop, the first passes over freshly
written weights ran 20--25\% slow and other programs moved single points by as
much again (\Chref{roofline}'s lab kept every run); compare a prediction with the
best of several runs, and report the spread with it (\Chref{benchmarking}).
\end{itemize}

\LabSection{a cost calculator you can trust}

The lab in \texttt{code/labs/ch06-napkin/} is the calculator itself: a
standard-library Python program that reads a model's Hugging Face
\texttt{config.json} (or one of three built-in configurations) and a machine
description --- bandwidth, peak compute by precision, memory, price --- and prints
this chapter's tables for any context length, precision, batch list and latency
objective. Its unit tests reproduce the chapter's tables, so a change that moves
a number in the book fails a test. From the repository root:

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
python3 code/labs/ch06-napkin/napkin.py --model qwen3-8b --machine h100 --tpot 15
python3 code/labs/ch06-napkin/napkin.py --model qwen3-30b-a3b --machine h100 --batches 1,8,32
python3 code/labs/ch06-napkin/napkin.py --model llama-3.2-3b --machine m1-gpu \
    --bytes 0.626 --kv-bytes 2 --context 2064 --eta-b 0.72 --eta-p 0.7 --batches 1,2,4,8
\end{Verbatim}

\noindent \emph{Predict} with it before you measure: take your machine's
bandwidth and peak from its datasheet, $\eta_\beta$ and $\eta_\pi$ from the labs
of \Chref{roofline} and \Chref{prefill-decode}, and write down the step time
\texttt{llama-batched-bench} will report at two contexts. \emph{Run} the
benchmark as the calibration section did. \emph{Explain} each error by the term
it belongs to: an error that does not change with context is in the weight term
(a kernel reading weights more than once, or a fixed overhead); an error that
grows with context is in the cache term (an attention kernel that does not
stream its cache). \emph{Break} the model deliberately: run a mixture-of-experts
model and compare the calculator's uniform-routing prediction with its measured
steps, or run with flash attention off and watch the cache term's error grow ---
\Chref{prefill-decode} measured that path reading the cache at a third of the
speed.

\HappenedSection{a published deployment, reconstructed}

DeepSeek has published statistics from its own serving of DeepSeek-V3 and R1
over one day in February 2025 \cite{deepseek2025inference}: each decode
instance spanned 18 eight-GPU H800 nodes, with each GPU holding two routed
experts and the shared expert; each node produced about 14,800 output tokens per
second, while users saw 20--22 tokens per second; and the average cache length
per output token was 4,989 tokens. From those operator-reported figures alone
(derived): each GPU produced about 1,850 tokens per second, so it carried about
88 conversations at a time, each stepping about every 48~ms. At the \$2 per
GPU-hour DeepSeek assumed, decode GPUs cost about \$0.30 per million output
tokens; charging the whole day's fleet to output tokens gives \$0.52 per
million, and to all tokens, input included, \$0.11.

Equation~\eqref{eq:step-time} can price most of that 48~ms step from the model's
configuration --- the third worked problem does it line by line. One GPU's step
reads about 21~GB of weights and 31~GB of latent cache (88 conversations of 4,989
tokens at 69~KiB each), 15~ms at the H100's bandwidth; performs about 6.4~TFLOP
in FP8 and 7.5~TFLOP of attention in BF16, 11~ms at the datasheet's peaks; and,
in a term the single-GPU model does not have, sends each token's hidden state to
the GPUs holding its eight experts and gathers the results back in each of 58
layers, about 0.9~GB per step, 18~ms over a 50~GB/s InfiniBand link
\cite{deepseek2024v3}. The three floors sum to 44~ms against a measured step of
about 48. DeepSeek overlaps them with a five-stage pipeline that splits
attention into two parts; the reconstruction says the overlap is far from
complete, and that the step is balanced across memory, arithmetic and the
network rather than bound by any one of them --- which is exactly why a
deployment of that size needs the communication terms of
Part~\ref{part:scaling-out}.

\FrontierSection{2026-09-27}

The inputs to this chapter's model move faster than its structure. Per-GPU
memory bandwidth reached 8~TB/s with the B200 and did not rise in its successor
while four-bit compute did; NVIDIA's Rubin raises it to 19.2~TB/s
\cite{nvidia2026verarubin} (\Chref{roofline}). Four-bit weights and caches halve
$W$ and $m_{kv}$ again, and 2026 checkpoints ship in them
\cite{moonshot2026kimik3,openai2025gptoss}; disaggregated serving prices
prefill and decode on separate machines (\Chref{disaggregated-serving}); and
speculative decoding changes the tokens produced per step
(\Chref{modern-speculation}). Rental prices change monthly: re-check them
before trusting any dollar figure here.
