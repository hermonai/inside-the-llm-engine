# Part VII measurements on an Apple M1: offloading on unified memory, shared bandwidth, and an expert-cache replay

**Dates:** 2026-09-24, 18:01–20:24 UTC (offload, contention, gpucopy, mmapload,
selftest, replay, offload re-run, repack round 0); 2026-09-26, 08:02–08:20 UTC
(repack round 1, Hermon's Metal benchmark).
**Used by:** Chapters 35, 36 and 37 (openings, napkin math, mechanism
sections, ledgers, Happened sections), Figures `fig:ch36-hit-rate` and
`fig:ch37-shared-bandwidth`. Figure `fig:ch35-slowest-link` is derived from
Equation `eq:offload-time`, not from this record.
**Raw outputs:** [`2026-09-24-m1-part7/`](2026-09-24-m1-part7/). Local paths
are redacted to `<scratch>`, `<ollama-blobs>`, `<home>` and `<tmp>`.

## Questions

1. On a machine where CPU and GPU share one memory, what does moving a
   model's layers from the GPU to the CPU cost llama.cpp's decode and prefill?
   What do finer splits cost: feed-forward tensors on the CPU, or the KV cache
   and attention on the CPU? (Chapters 35, 37)
2. Why did splits between the extremes decode slower than either extreme?
   (Chapters 35, 37)
3. How much does ordinary CPU memory traffic slow GPU decode and a GPU bulk
   copy? (Chapter 37)
4. Does llama.cpp's Metal backend load weights zero-copy, and what does
   `--no-mmap` change? (Chapter 37)
5. Does Hermon's kernel self-test pass on this M1 at the commit the book
   cites? (Chapter 36)
6. Can the hit rates Hermon's developers published for their expert pager be
   reproduced from the benchmark's own trace generator, and what do LRU, a
   learned frequency policy and the offline optimum achieve in steady state?
   (Chapter 36; derived by simulation)
7. Do Hermon's Metal-versus-CPU attention results reproduce on this M1?
   (Chapter 37)

### Expert-cache replay (`expert_replay.py`, derived by simulation)

The routing trace is regenerated exactly as `bench_experts.c` generates it: the
64-bit LCG (multiplier 6364136223846793005, increment 1442695040888963407,
seed `0x9E3779B97F4A7C15`, result `(state >> 33) % n`), a per-layer
Fisher–Yates permutation drawn first, the seed reset before each
configuration, uniform picks or `idx = (int)(pow(n, 1 − u) − 1)` with
`u = rnd(2^30)/2^30` (a truncated power law of exponent 1; the benchmark's
exponent argument is unused), then de-duplication within a token. The cache
follows `experts.c`: one LRU over every resident (layer, expert) record;
records a layer has acquired are pinned until the layer releases them; the
K6.1-era acquire (`be0288a`) installs each miss at once, the current one
(`2a3fd52`) classifies hits and misses first and installs misses after. Shape:
48 layers × 128 experts, 8 per token; records 3,059,712 bytes (Q6_K down
projection, 24 layers) or 2,654,208 bytes (Q4_K, 24 layers); caches of
`int(f × 6,144)` blocks for f = 2, 5, 10, 25, 50%.

## Limits

- One laptop, heavily loaded and short of memory for the whole run (see
  Conditions). The partial-offload and repacking results are measurements of
  *this* machine's memory pressure as much as of llama.cpp; the mechanism
  evidence is in the buffer sizes and the swap and page-in counters, not in
  the token rates alone, and the token rates are given as ranges.
- Unified memory has no PCIe link, so these runs say nothing about a discrete
  GPU's offloading costs; Chapter 35's PCIe figures are Hermon's records and a
  marker for re-measurement on the RTX 5060 Ti host.
- The contention runs use synthetic copy processes; real co-running
  applications read memory in other patterns.
- The replay is a simulation of the benchmark's synthetic traces, not of a
  model's router; it checks hit rates and byte counts, not device throughput.
